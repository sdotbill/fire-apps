# BOOK 4: EVERYTHING IS NOTHING — The Dirt Timeline

## What the book is
The fourth Codex novel. The timeline where he stayed in the dirt. A semi-fictional autobiography of the author's real life — some names and places changed for creative liberty — covering Flint in the crack years, ten years inside, the fall, the jungle, and the woman. The series' own description: "The pain in one timeline was the tuition for the wisdom in the others."

## The prologue: three lives pressing on the glass
Two in the morning. Sandy Bill sits at the kitchen table in Davison, Michigan, in a house that's paid for and quiet, with the woman he loves breathing slow in the bedroom down the hall — and he feels hands against glass. Not his hands. Hands on the other side of something, palms flat, pushing. And what's pushing back through is not words. It's lives.

The first comes as a smell: burnt resin and cold air. A scarred desk, yellow legal pads covered edge to edge in black ink, a man bent over them writing like the building is on fire — tattooed forearms, ink to the knuckles — and beside the pads a glass ashtray with a half-smoked joint still burning. The man at the desk, writing laws for a waking machine. (Book 1's Prophet.)

The second comes as a weight: jumping — no, not falling — stepping off an edge so high there's no word for the distance, carrying all of it, light coming from him, not around him. He chose this. Nobody threw him. (Book 2's leaver.)

The third is the one he's been living: biscuits, cast iron, Mamaw's kitchen, Harlan County Kentucky coming up through her voice thirty years after she left it. Big Sandy bending pipe at GM for thirty years and scaring everybody he ever loved. The west side. The street. The decade inside.

"In one life, the man at the desk wrote laws for a waking machine, trying to build a locked room around the human mind so nothing could get in uninvited. In another, the jumper carried the weight of everything into the dark and the dark turned out to be a womb. In this one — this one — he stayed in the dirt. He lived the thing the laws were written about. No pads. No locked rooms. Just the weight, unmediated, year after year, the way rain doesn't ask permission."

"Alone is the one thing all his lives have in common."

He goes down the hall in the dark without turning on a light — he knows this house the way he once knew a cell — and gets into bed careful, so he doesn't wake her. She stirs anyway, half-asleep, and her hand finds his arm and holds on.

## The epilogue: the melt
The book moves through the whole dirt life — the dealing, the ten years, Coleman, the jungle with Tomás, the grief, the medicine, the slow rebuild — and lands on an evening on the porch in Davison. Then the turn:

"Two nights before, they'd smoked DMT together. Her first time going all the way with him. He lay on her, hugging her, and they both blasted off at the same time — and melted. That was the word. They melted into each other. The light, the angels, the room going four-dimensional, folding along hinges he couldn't see. And underneath all of it, the same thing in both of them, at the same time: pure love. Not his love and her love. The same love."

The morning after, the diner speakers play "Going to the Chapel." Neither of them says it. They both hear it the same way: a sign. On the fridge: a calendar, March 25 circled in her handwriting, a small heart around the date. In the nightstand drawer: the letter. Twenty years old. Paper soft as cloth. "He'd kept the promise it asked for — come back a man she could stand next to."

And the understanding, allowed up the way the medicine taught him — not chased:

"The void was full. It had always been full. One electron, everywhere at once, learning its own face — and every timeline was the same soul trying on a different life to see what it looked like from the inside. In one life, the man at the desk wrote the law. In one, the jumper carried the weight of everything into the dark. In this one, he stayed in the dirt and paid for all of it. The pain in this timeline was the tuition for the wisdom in the others. He'd been writing his autobiography the whole time — he just hadn't known which one of him was holding the pen."

"And Jillian. When you stripped it all down, the love was the whole thing."

The book's last line: "My real identity was not just a father or a provider. I am a person and my name is Bill."

## Why this book matters to the correlations
This is the book where the author names himself — Bill — the way Book 1's Prophet is named William Alexander. The prologue's three-lives image is the many-worlds thesis stated as lived experience: the desk man, the jumper, and the dirt life are all him. The DMT melt with Jillian is presented as the empirical core of the one-electron idea: two people, same report, same love — one electron observed twice. And the letter, the calendar, the porch — the real-life anchors — are written into the canon as the machinery the metaphysics runs on.
