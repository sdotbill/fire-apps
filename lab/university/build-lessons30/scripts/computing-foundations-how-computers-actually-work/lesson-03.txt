# Memory and storage: the desk and the filing cabinet

Welcome back. In this room, AI and Computing is learned by doing it, not by watching it. I am Professor Ye-Jin Kim, and this is Computing Foundations. This is lesson three, and its title is Memory and storage: the desk and the filing cabinet. In lesson one you learned what the computer stores, numbers in bits and bytes, standing for letters and colours and sounds. In lesson two you met the clerk, the processor, who follows instructions at three billion ticks a second. Today we answer the question the clerk himself kept asking. Where does he fetch all those instructions and numbers from? Where is everything kept? The answer comes in two parts, and the difference between the two parts explains some of the most familiar mysteries of daily life with machines. Why does your phone remember your photographs for years but forget an unsaved note in a blink? Why can it hold thousands of pictures but only a handful of open apps? Why does a bigger number on the box sometimes matter and sometimes not? By the end of this lesson, you will be able to explain all three, in plain language, to anyone who asks.

Here is the picture, and it is the picture in the title. Think of the processor as the clerk from lesson two, sitting at work. Every worker needs two kinds of space. He needs a desk, right in front of him, where the papers he is working on right now are spread out where his hands can reach them. And he needs a filing cabinet, across the room, where the records he is not using today are kept safe, in far greater quantity than any desk could hold. A computer has exactly the same two kinds of space, and confusing them is the single most common confusion in all of computing. Today we separate them forever.

The desk is called memory. When people who work with computers say memory, they almost always mean one specific thing, and its short name is RAM. RAM stands for random access memory, and I will unpack that name in a moment, because it is less frightening than it sounds. For now, hold the picture. Memory is the computer's desk, fast and close, where the work in progress sits, and everything on it vanishes when the power goes. Read that sentence again, slowly, because it carries the whole lesson. Fast and close. Work in progress. Vanishes when the power goes. Three claims, and we will take them one at a time.

Fast and close first. The clerk works at billions of ticks a second, and he cannot spend those ticks walking across town. The desk is built right around him, wired tightly to him, so that when he reaches for an instruction or a number, it is in his hands almost immediately. Almost immediately, at computer scale, means a few billionths of a second. That speed is not a luxury. It is a necessity. A clerk who ticks three billion times a second, fed by a slow desk, would spend nearly all of his life sitting idle with his hands folded, waiting for the next paper to arrive. Lesson two warned you that the clerk spends much of his life waiting. The desk exists to make the most common waits as short as physics allows.

Work in progress second. What actually sits on the desk at any moment? Exactly what is being worked on, and nothing else. The program that is running right now, with its instructions laid out in order so the clerk can fetch them one by one. The numbers that program is using. The photograph you have open, decoded from its stored numbers into pixels ready to draw. The message you are halfway through typing. The song that is playing this second. If a thing is in use, it is on the desk. If it is not in use, it is not on the desk, because the desk is not big enough for everything, and it was never meant to be. That is not a flaw in the design. That is the design. A desk is for today's work.

Vanishes when the power goes, third, and this is the claim people find hardest to believe, so let me be very plain. The desk forgets. Memory holds its bits as tiny electrical charges and patterns that must be kept alive by power. The moment the power stops, the charges drain away, and every bit on the desk falls back to nothing. The technical word for this behaviour is volatile. A thing is volatile if it disappears when the power goes. Memory is volatile. That is why, when your phone battery dies in the middle of an unsaved note, the note is gone. It was never anywhere but the desk, and the desk went dark. That is why a laptop that loses power can lose the last paragraph you typed but never loses the document you saved an hour ago. The saved document was in the cabinet. The paragraph was only on the desk. Same machine, two kinds of space, two different fates, and now you know which is which.

Now the name, quickly, because I promised. Random access memory. Access means reaching something. Random, in this one technical sense, does not mean haphazard or by chance. It means the clerk can reach any spot on the desk directly, in any order, just as fast as any other spot. He does not have to start at one end and walk. Every pigeonhole on the desk is the same short reach away. That is what random access means, and it is a large part of why the desk is fast. Compare a roll of tape, where reaching the middle means winding through everything before it. The desk is not a tape. The desk is a desk. Any paper, any moment, same reach.

How big is the desk? Desks in modern machines are measured in gigabytes, and you met the gigabyte in lesson one as roughly a billion bytes. A typical modern phone has 8 gigabytes of memory. Eight billion bytes, give or take, spread out on the desk. A decent laptop might have 16 gigabytes, and a heavy working machine 32 gigabytes or more. Hold those numbers loosely for now. What matters is the comparison that is coming, because the filing cabinet is measured in the same units, and it dwarfs the desk every single time.

Now walk across the room with me to the filing cabinet. The cabinet is called storage. Storage is the filing cabinet, slower and far bigger, where things are kept when nobody is working on them, and it keeps them through the dark. Again, three claims in one sentence, and again we take them in order. Slower. Far bigger. Keeps them through the dark.

Slower first, and slower needs honest handling, because storage is not slow the way a person is slow. Reaching into storage takes the clerk perhaps a hundred times longer than reaching the desk, sometimes more. At human scale both feel instant. At the clerk's scale, where a billionth of a second is a working lifetime, a hundredfold wait is an age. That is the whole reason the desk exists. If storage were as fast as memory, we would build the whole machine out of it and this lesson would be one paragraph long. It is not, so we do not, and the two-tier system, small fast desk and huge slower cabinet, is the compromise every computer on Earth has settled on.

Far bigger second. Where the phone's desk is 8 gigabytes, the same phone's cabinet is commonly 128 gigabytes, or 256 gigabytes, sixteen or thirty-two times the size of the desk. Laptops carry storage of 512 gigabytes or a full terabyte, and a terabyte is roughly a trillion bytes, a thousand gigabytes. Your business files, your photographs, your music, your apps themselves when they are not running, all of it lives in the cabinet, and the cabinet has room for a life's worth of it. When an advertisement shouts a big number of gigabytes at you, it is almost always shouting about the cabinet, because the cabinet's number is the big one. Now you will know to ask the second question. How big is the desk? That number decides how much work the machine can hold open at once, and it is printed much smaller, when it is printed at all.

Keeps them through the dark, third, and this is storage's great virtue. Storage holds its bits in a way that does not need power to survive. In a phone and in most modern laptops, storage is a kind of chip called flash memory, which traps tiny charges in a way that stays put for years without power. In older and larger machines, storage is a spinning disc with a magnetic surface, called a hard drive, where the bits are written as magnetic patterns that likewise stay where they were put. Either way, the principle is the same, and the technical word for it is non-volatile. Non-volatile means it does not disappear when the power goes. Turn the phone off. Leave it in a drawer for a year. Turn it on, and your photographs are still there, because they were in the cabinet, and the cabinet keeps its records through the dark. That is the promise that makes storage storage.

Now let me put the two together in one ordinary moment from your own life, worked in full, because this is the kind of thing I want you to be able to trace without me. You tap the icon of your photo app. What happens, step by step? The app itself, as a program, lives in the cabinet, in storage, where it has been sitting since you installed it. Tapping the icon is an instruction to bring that program to the desk. The machine copies the program from storage into memory, onto the desk, and only then can the clerk start fetching its instructions, because the clerk fetches from the desk. That small delay while the app opens, the pause you feel, is largely the sound of papers being carried across the room. Then you tap one photograph. That photograph's numbers also live in the cabinet. They are copied to the desk, decoded there into millions of coloured pixels, and the clerk and his colleagues draw them on the screen. You edit the photograph, cropping it, brightening it. Your edits happen on the desk, in memory, at full clerk speed. And then, the moment that matters most. You press save. Save means, carry this finished work from the desk back to the cabinet and write it there. Until you press save, your edits exist only on the volatile desk. After save, they are in the non-volatile cabinet, and they will survive the battery dying, the phone falling, the year in the drawer. Save is not a courtesy button. Save is the walk across the room, and now you know exactly what it does and why it matters.

This also explains the mystery of the vanishing app. You open six apps on your phone, one after another, and when you return to the first one, it starts over from the beginning, as if you had never been there. Nothing is broken. The desk filled up. Six apps, each with its work spread out, is more paper than the desk can hold, so the machine quietly cleared the oldest app's papers back off the desk to make room. The app itself is safe in the cabinet. Its work in progress, the half-typed message, the paused place in the article, was on the desk, and the desk was needed for something else. A bigger desk, more memory, means more apps can keep their work spread out at once. That, and nothing else, is what the memory number on the box is really promising you.

Now the bundles, carefully, because you will meet these numbers on every box and every bill for the rest of your life, and I want them demystified. Bytes come in bundles, and in this course we count the bundles the strict way, the way the machine counts. 1 kilobyte is 1024 bytes. 1 megabyte is 1024 kilobytes, which is 1048576 bytes. 1 gigabyte is 1024 megabytes. 1 terabyte is 1024 gigabytes. You will notice the number 1024 everywhere, and by now you can guess why. It is two doubled ten times, the closest power of two to a thousand, and lesson one's doubling pattern is still running the show. When a person says a file is about a megabyte, they mean about a million bytes, and for everyday talk that rounding is fine. But when you are counting what fits where, the strict numbers are the ones that add up.

Here is your guided moment, a piece of arithmetic from real life, and I want you to actually do it, out loud if you can. Suppose your phone tells you it has 8 gigabytes of free storage, and suppose each photograph you take takes up about 4 megabytes. How many more photographs can the phone hold? Work it the way we worked the binary numbers, in steps. Step one, turn the gigabytes into megabytes. One gigabyte is 1024 megabytes, so 8 gigabytes is 8 times 1024, which is 8192 megabytes. Step two, divide by the size of one photograph. 8192 divided by 4 is 2048. Your phone can hold about 2048 more photographs. Two thousand and forty-eight photographs, in the space a quick glance calls eight gigabytes. That is what the cabinet's numbers mean when you unfold them, and you unfolded them yourself, with nothing but the bundles and one division. Keep the method. Free space, turned into the same units as the thing you want to store, divided by the size of the thing. It works for photographs, for songs, for business files, for anything made of bytes, which after lesson one you know means everything.

Now I can show you something beautiful, because you have all the pieces. The desk and the cabinet are not the whole story. They are the middle of a ladder, and the ladder is one of the great organising ideas of computing. You already know the top rung. In lesson two, the clerk's own registers, the tiny slots in his hands, holding the few numbers being worked on at this exact tick. Below the registers sits a small, extremely fast store built into the processor itself, called the cache. The cache is the processor's private shelf, a small fast store that keeps copies of the instructions and numbers the clerk is most likely to need next, so he does not have to reach even as far as the desk. Below the cache, the desk itself, memory, gigabytes of it, a few billionths of a second away. And below the desk, across the room, the cabinet, storage, hundreds of gigabytes, a hundred times slower to reach, keeping everything through the dark.

Small and fast at the top. Huge and slow at the bottom. Every rung bigger and slower than the rung above it. That shape is not an accident and it is not a fashion. It is forced on us by physics and by price. The fastest kinds of storage are the most expensive per byte and the hardest to build in bulk, so we build a little of them. The slowest kinds are cheap per byte, so we build mountains of them. The ladder is how a machine pretends, convincingly, to be both enormous and instant. The clerk works at the top. The copies flow up and down the ladder as needed. When the system works well, the thing the clerk wants next is almost always already on the shelf or the desk, and the long walk to the cabinet is rare. When it works badly, when the desk is too small for the job, the clerk spends his billions of ticks waiting on the stairs, and the whole machine feels slow even though its clerk is a marvel. If you remember one sentence from this lesson when you are buying a machine or judging one, make it this one. A computer feels fast when the work fits the desk, and slow when it does not.

There is one more trick the machine uses when the desk overflows, and you should know it because it explains a particular kind of slowness you have certainly felt. When memory fills completely, the machine begins using a corner of the cabinet as if it were extra desk space, carrying papers back and forth between the real desk and the cabinet as the clerk needs them. This overflow arrangement is called swap, because the machine is forever swapping papers in and out. It works, in the sense that the job gets done and nothing is lost. But remember the price of the walk. The cabinet is a hundred times slower to reach, so a machine living in swap is a clerk spending his day on the stairs, and everything about it feels like wading. When an old computer grinds and stutters while doing several things at once, this is very often the honest explanation. Not a broken clerk. A full desk, and a long walk, repeated a million times. The cure is a bigger desk or less work spread out at once, and now you can say why, in plain words, instead of just feeling that the machine is tired.

Let me bring the whole lesson home to your own machines and your own files, because this is a class about your life as much as about chips. Your business records, your customer lists, your photographs of finished work, the documents you cannot afford to lose. Ask of each one, where does it live? If the only copy is on the desk, in an open app, unsaved, it lives one power cut away from gone. If it is saved, it is in the cabinet of one machine, which is good, and the cabinet can still break, or the phone can still be lost, which is why lesson six will talk about copies in more than one place. For today, the habit to build is simpler. Save early, save often, and know what the button is doing when you press it. It is carrying your work across the room, from the fast forgetful desk to the slow faithful cabinet, and that walk is the difference between work that survives the night and work that does not.

One more distinction, drawn cleanly, because salespeople blur it and I will not have you blurred. Memory and storage are not two sizes of the same thing, and you cannot trade one for the other. A phone with a huge cabinet and a small desk will hold every photograph you ever take and still lose your place in every app. A machine with a huge desk and a small cabinet will juggle work beautifully and then run out of room to keep anything. When you choose a machine, you are choosing both numbers, for two different jobs. The desk for the work. The cabinet for the keeping. Say it that way to any salesperson and watch the conversation improve.

There is one everyday ceremony that shows the whole system at once, and you perform it without thinking. Turning the machine on. At the moment you press the button, the desk is empty, wiped clean by the time without power, because the desk is volatile. The clerk wakes to a bare desk and cannot begin, because he fetches his instructions from the desk and there is nothing on it. So the machine's first job, built into its deepest wiring, is to go to the cabinet and fetch the one program that starts all the others, the operating system, the program that runs the machine itself. You will meet the operating system properly in lesson four. For now, watch the walk. Cabinet to desk, program by program, the desk fills, the clerk begins fetching in earnest, and the screen comes alive. That pause while a machine starts, the logo, the spinning wheel, is the sound of a very large set of papers being carried across a room and laid out in order. And when you turn the machine off properly, the reverse courtesy happens. Programs are asked to save their work to the cabinet, desks are cleared in an orderly way, and only then does the power go. Pulling the plug skips the courtesy, which is why machines scold you about it, gently, the next time they wake.

The same ceremony explains the fullest cabinet mystery of all. When your phone warns you that storage is almost full, it is not being fussy. A cabinet with no free drawers cannot accept the walk. New photographs have nowhere to be filed, updated apps have nowhere to put their new selves, and the overflow space the machine uses for swap disappears as well. The phone slows, refuses, and complains, all from one cause. No room in the cabinet. The fix follows from the picture, as every fix in this class should. Delete what you no longer keep, or move the keeping to a bigger cabinet, which is part of what lesson six will mean by your data living in more than one place. A warning you understand is a warning you can act on, and you now understand this one all the way down to the bits.

One last worked trace, small and complete, because I want the two spaces working together in your mind before the questions. Imagine you are writing a message to a customer, a long one, with prices in it. You open the messaging app. The app travels from cabinet to desk and starts running. You type. Every letter you type is a number from lesson one, held for the moment in memory, on the desk, because the message is work in progress. The app, being well made, quietly saves a draft to the cabinet every so often, and that quiet saving is the walk happening without your asking. Your battery dies at the worst moment. The desk goes dark, and everything since the last quiet save is gone, but the draft in the cabinet survives, and when the phone wakes, the app walks it back to the desk and offers it to you. Nothing about that rescue is magic, and nothing about the loss is a betrayal. Two kinds of space did exactly what this lesson said they do. The desk held the work. The cabinet kept what was carried to it. If you can tell that story back to yourself without my voice, you own this lesson, and you are ready to be tested on it.

Let us recap, in order. First, memory is the computer's desk, fast and close, where the work in progress sits, and everything on it vanishes when the power goes. Second, that vanishing has a name, volatile, and it is why unsaved work dies with the battery. Third, RAM, random access memory, is the desk's proper name, and random access means any spot can be reached directly, just as fast as any other. Fourth, storage is the filing cabinet, slower and far bigger, where things are kept when nobody is working on them, and it keeps them through the dark. Fifth, storage is non-volatile, holding its bits through years without power, in flash chips or on magnetic discs. Sixth, save is the walk across the room, copying finished work from the desk to the cabinet. Seventh, the bundles run 1024 at a time. 1 kilobyte is 1024 bytes, 1 megabyte is 1024 kilobytes, 1 gigabyte is 1024 megabytes. And eighth, registers, cache, memory, and storage form a ladder, small and fast at the top, huge and slow at the bottom, and a machine feels fast when the work fits the desk.
