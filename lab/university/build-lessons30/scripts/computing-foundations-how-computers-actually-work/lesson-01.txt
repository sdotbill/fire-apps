# Bits and the audacity of counting with two fingers

Welcome. In this room, AI and Computing is learned by doing it, not by watching it. I am Professor Ye-Jin Kim, and this is Computing Foundations, the class where we find out how computers actually work. This is lesson one, and its title is Bits and the audacity of counting with two fingers. Settle in. By the end of this lesson you will know what a bit is, why a machine that can only tell the difference between two things can still count, spell, show photographs, and play music, and you will be able to count to thirty-one on the fingers of one hand. That last part is not a party trick. It is the whole secret, hiding in your own hand.

Let me say plainly what this lesson teaches and why it is worth knowing. Every photograph on your phone, every message you have ever sent, every file for your business, every song and every video, is stored inside a computer as numbers. And every one of those numbers is built out of the smallest possible choice, a choice between two things only. If you understand that one small choice, and how it multiplies, you understand the ground floor of every computer on Earth. Nothing later in this course will make sense without it, and everything later in this course is built on top of it. So we start here, at the bottom, where the ground is solid.

Here is my promise about how I teach. I will never hand you a word without telling you what it means in plain language first. I will never ask you to guess what a good answer looks like. In this room, the worked example is a moral act, because nobody should have to guess what good looks like. When I set you a small problem, I will work one in full, out loud, before you ever have to try. Watch for that. It is coming in this very lesson.

First, let us talk about counting itself, because counting is something you already know how to do, and the computer does it in a stranger and simpler way than you do.

When you count, you use ten symbols. They are the digits zero, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, and nine. Ten symbols. That system is called decimal, and the word decimal simply means based on ten. It is almost certainly based on ten because you have ten fingers, and human beings learned to count on their fingers long before they learned to write numbers down. There is nothing magic about ten. It is just the number of fingers we happen to have. If we had been born with eight fingers, we would probably count in eights and think it was the most natural thing in the world.

Now here is the audacity in the title of this lesson. A computer counts with two fingers. Not ten. Two. The computer has only two symbols to count with, and they are zero and one. That system is called binary, and the word binary simply means based on two. Everything a computer does, all of it, is done with those two symbols and nothing else. When I first heard that, years ago, I thought it sounded impossible, like trying to write a novel with only two letters. It is not impossible. It is just slower to read and much, much faster to build. By the end of this lesson, you will see why.

The smallest piece of this whole story has a name, and the name is bit. A bit is one single choice between two values, and those two values are zero and one. That is the whole definition, and I want you to hold onto it exactly as I said it. A bit is one single choice between two values, zero and one. The word bit is short for binary digit, which just means one digit in the binary system, the way the number seven is one digit in your ordinary decimal system. One bit, one choice, two possible answers. That does not sound like much, and on its own it is not much. One bit can say off or on. One bit can say no or yes. One bit can say dark or light. But wait until you see what happens when bits travel in groups, because that is where the power is.

Why two? Why would anyone build a machine on such a small choice? The answer is electricity, and it is a practical answer, not a poetic one. Inside a computer, a bit is carried by electricity in a wire, and the machine reads the bit by asking a simple question about that electricity. Is the voltage high, or is the voltage low? Voltage is just the push behind electricity, the pressure that makes it flow. High push, the machine reads a one. Low push, the machine reads a zero. Two states of electricity, two symbols, and the machine never has to guess. Imagine instead that we tried to build a computer on ten levels of voltage, one for each decimal digit. The machine would have to tell the difference between level four and level five in a wire full of noise and heat and tiny imperfections, billions of times every second, and it would get it wrong. With two levels, far apart, there is almost nothing to get wrong. High is high. Low is low. That reliability is the entire reason for binary. Computers count with two fingers because two fingers are almost impossible to misread.

You already own a perfect picture of a bit, and you use it every day. A light switch. A light switch has exactly two states, off and on, and it is never halfway in any way that matters. One switch, two states. That is one bit, sitting on your wall. Now picture the panel in a hallway with four switches in a row. Each switch is off or on, and the row as a whole can be in many combinations. Off off off off. On off off off. Off on off on. Count the combinations and you get sixteen, and we will see in a few minutes exactly why it is sixteen and not some other number. Hold that picture of the switch panel in your mind, because a computer is, at the bottom, an enormous panel of switches, switching faster than anything you have ever seen, and keeping perfect track of every combination.

Now let us learn to count in binary, slowly, with our hands, because your hands are the best teaching tool you own.

In decimal, when you count, you fill up one place and then you carry. You count zero, one, two, all the way to nine in the first place, and when you run out of symbols you write a one in the next place and start again, and that gives you ten. The places in decimal are worth one, then ten, then one hundred, then one thousand. Each place is worth ten times the place before it, because decimal is based on ten.

Binary works in exactly the same way, except each place is worth two times the place before it, because binary is based on two. So the binary places, reading from the right, are worth one, then two, then four, then eight, then sixteen, then thirty-two. Say those place values with me, because you will use them in every lesson that follows. One, two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two. Each one is double the last. That doubling is the heartbeat of this entire subject.

Now count with me in binary, and watch how the carrying happens sooner, because we run out of symbols sooner. Zero is written as 0. One is written as 1. Now we have used both of our symbols in the first place, so to say two, we carry. Two is written as 10 in binary. Read that carefully. It is not ten. In binary, the written form 10 means one two and no ones, which is two. Three is written as 11, meaning one two and one one. Four needs a new place, and four is written as 100, meaning one four, no twos, and no ones. Five is written as 101, meaning one four, no twos, and one one. Six is written as 110. Seven is written as 111. Eight is written as 1000. Do you see the pattern? The symbols look like the decimal numbers you know, but they mean something different, because the places are worth different amounts. The written form is just a row of switches. The value depends on what each place is worth.

Here is how you read any binary number, and this is a skill, so we will do it as a worked example, in full, the way I promised. Take the binary number 101, written as the digits one, zero, one. Step one, write out the place values under the digits, from the right. The rightmost digit sits over the ones place. The middle digit sits over the twos place. The leftmost digit sits over the fours place. Step two, multiply each digit by its place value. One times four is four. Zero times two is zero. One times one is one. Step three, add them. Four plus zero plus one is five. So the binary number 101 is five. That is the whole method, and it never changes, no matter how long the binary number gets. Places, multiply, add. Places, multiply, add. If you can do those three steps, you can read any number a computer has ever stored.

Let us do one more together, a bigger one, so the method settles. Take the binary number 1101, the digits one, one, zero, one. The places from the right are one, two, four, and eight. The leftmost one sits over eight, so that is eight. The next one sits over four, so that is four. The zero sits over two, so that is zero. The last one sits over one, so that is one. Eight plus four plus zero plus one is thirteen. The binary number 1101 is thirteen. Good. You have just read a computer number the way the computer means it, and you did it in your head, with no machine to help you. That is doing it, not watching it, and that is how this room works.

Now it is time for your fingers to earn their place in the title of this lesson. This is the guided part, the doing part, so actually do it. Hold up one hand, palm facing you, and look at your five fingers. Here is the idea. Each finger is a switch. A finger held up is a one. A finger folded down is a zero. Your hand is now a row of 5 bits, and like any row of bits, it can hold a number.

We need to agree on what each finger is worth, and we use the place values you already learned. Let us say your thumb, on the right of your raised hand as you look at it, is worth one. The next finger is worth two. The middle finger is worth four. The next finger is worth eight. And the little finger, on the far end, is worth sixteen. One, two, four, eight, sixteen, one for each finger. The number your hand is holding is simply the total of the fingers that are up.

Try it with me, step by step. Start with a fist. Every finger down, every bit zero. Your hand is holding zero. Now raise only your thumb. One finger up, worth one. Your hand is holding one. Fold the thumb down and raise the finger worth two. Your hand is holding two. Now raise the thumb as well. Two plus one. Your hand is holding three. Fold both down and raise only the middle finger, the one worth four. Your hand is holding four. Raise the thumb too. Four plus one is five, and notice what your hand is showing. Middle finger and thumb up, the rest down. That is the binary number 101 spread out on your own hand, the same 101 we worked out a moment ago. You are not imagining a computer. You are being one, slowly, at human speed.

Keep going on your own for a moment, because your hand will teach you faster than my voice can. Show me seven. That should be the fingers worth four, two, and one, all up together, because four plus two plus one is seven. Show me ten. That is the finger worth eight and the finger worth two, because eight plus two is ten. Show me thirteen. Eight plus four plus one, so the eight finger, the four finger, and the thumb. If your hand found thirteen, you have understood binary counting completely, and everything else in this lesson is just that same idea getting bigger.

Now here is the question that makes people sit up. What is the biggest number one hand can hold? Raise all five fingers. Sixteen plus eight plus four plus two plus one. Add them. Sixteen and eight is twenty-four, and four is twenty-eight, and two is thirty, and one is thirty-one. One hand, five fingers, and the biggest number it can hold is 31. And how many different numbers can that hand hold, counting zero? It can hold every number from zero up to thirty-one, and that is 32 different values in total. Five fingers, thirty-two values. Remember that pair of numbers, because a pattern is about to show itself, and the pattern is the most important arithmetic in computing.

The pattern is this. Every time you add one more bit, the number of possible values doubles. Watch it happen. With 1 bit, you have two values, zero and one, and that is 2 values. With 2 bits, each value of the first bit can pair with each value of the second, and you get 4 values, zero through three. With 3 bits, you get 8 values. With 4 bits, you get 16 values. With 5 bits, your hand, you get 32 values. With 6 bits, 64 values. The count of values is two multiplied by itself once for each bit. Teachers call that a power of two, and all that phrase means is two doubled over and over. So if X is the number of bits, then the number of values doubles X times. Hold onto that sentence in plain words. More bits, double the values, every single time. That is why a handful of extra bits is never a small thing. Eight bits is not a little more than seven bits. It is exactly twice as much room.

Now use both hands. Ten fingers, ten bits. The values double ten times, and the total is 1024 different values, the numbers from zero up to 1023. Two hands, and you can already count past a thousand. A person counting on ten ordinary decimal fingers can show ten things. A person counting in binary on the same ten fingers can show one thousand and twenty-four different numbers. That is the audacity of counting with two fingers. The two-symbol system looks poorer and turns out richer, because combinations multiply. Every bit you add doubles the world the machine can describe.

This doubling is also why computers feel like they leap instead of grow. When an engineer adds bits to a machine, she does not add a little room. She doubles the room, and then doubles it again. Small changes in the number of bits make enormous changes in what can be represented. Keep that in your pocket for the whole course. Whenever a number in computing looks strangely large or strangely specific, like two hundred and fifty-six or one thousand and twenty-four, look for the doubling underneath it. It is almost always there.

Which brings us to the most famous group of bits in the world, the byte. A byte is a group of 8 bits, always eight, no exceptions. It is the standard bundle, the way a dozen is a standard bundle of twelve eggs. When people talk about the size of a file or the memory of a phone, they count in bytes and in bundles of bytes, and we will meet those bigger bundles in lesson three. For now, the byte itself. Eight bits, each one zero or one. How many different values can a byte hold? Double two, eight times. Two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two, sixty-four, one hundred and twenty-eight, two hundred and fifty-six. A byte holds 256 different values, the numbers from zero up to 255. That number, two hundred and fifty-six, is going to follow us through this entire course. You will meet it in letters, in colours, in sound. When you see it, you will know what it means. Eight switches. Two hundred and fifty-six combinations.

So a computer counts, and it counts in binary, and it stores its counts in bytes. But counting alone does not give you a photograph or a letter to your sister. Numbers by themselves are just numbers. Here is the turn in the road, and it is the most important idea in this lesson, so I will say it slowly. The trick is representation. Representation means using numbers to stand for other things, by an agreed code. A code, in this sense, is simply an agreement. We agree that a certain number means a certain letter, and then whenever the machine stores that number, we read it as that letter. The number does not look like the letter. It does not need to. The agreement does all the work.

Let me work the set problem for this class in full, right now, the way I promised I would, because this problem looks small and it opens like a trapdoor. The problem is this. A computer is just counting with electricity, so what has to be added to counting before you get a word processor? Keep every claim checkable. Here is the worked answer, in four parts. Counting gives you numbers. You must add representation, numbers standing for letters, colours, and sounds by agreed codes. You must add stored instructions, the program kept as data the machine can fetch, and we will spend all of lesson four on that. You must add conditional branching, which means do this if that, the machine choosing its next step based on what it finds. And you must add input and output, a way for the world to reach the counting and for the counting to reach the world, a keyboard and a screen. A word processor is those four things, wearing a friendly face. Counting, plus representation, plus stored instructions, plus branching, plus input and output. That is the shape of every program you will ever use, and today we are mastering the first two parts of it, counting and representation.

So let us see representation at work with letters, because letters are where a word processor begins. There is an old and famous agreed code for letters, and its name is ASCII. You say it like askee. In ASCII, every letter and every common symbol is given a number, and the machine stores the number and trusts the agreement. In that code, the capital letter A is the number 65. The capital letter B is the number 66. The capital letter C is the number 67. The small letter a is the number 97. A space, the empty gap between words, is the number 32. A full stop is the number 46. So when you type the word CAB into your phone, the phone does not store anything that looks like letters. It stores three numbers, sixty-seven, sixty-five, and sixty-six, one byte each, and the screen draws the letters back for you because the screen is following the same agreement. Your whole message, every word of it, is a row of numbers under the surface. The letters are a kindness the machine does for your eyes.

And each of those numbers is itself stored in binary, in a byte, as eight switches. The letter A is sixty-five, and sixty-five in binary is 01000001. Read it with your method. The places are one hundred and twenty-eight, sixty-four, thirty-two, sixteen, eight, four, two, and one. The only ones in that row sit over sixty-four and over one, and sixty-four plus one is sixty-five. So the letter A, at the very bottom, is one byte where the sixty-four switch and the one switch are on and every other switch is off. When you press the A key on your phone, that is what you are really doing. You are setting eight switches in a pattern that the whole world of computing has agreed to read as A. I find that beautiful, and I am not embarrassed to say so. The grandest library ever built will rest on rows of switches just like that one.

Letters are only the beginning of representation. Colours work the same way, and once you see it, you will never look at a photograph on your phone the same way again. A photograph on a screen is made of tiny dots, and each dot is called a pixel, which is just a short way of saying picture element. The colour of each pixel is stored as three numbers, one for how much red is in it, one for how much green, and one for how much blue. Each of those three numbers is stored in one byte, so each one runs from zero up to two hundred and fifty-five. Zero red, zero green, and zero blue gives you black. Two hundred and fifty-five of all three gives you white. Two hundred and fifty-five red, zero green, and zero blue gives you a pure, strong red. Mix them in between and you can make any colour your eye can name. How many colours in total? Two hundred and fifty-six choices for red, times two hundred and fifty-six for green, times two hundred and fifty-six for blue, and the total is 16777216 different colours, more than sixteen million, from three bytes per dot. Three bytes. Twenty-four switches. Sixteen million colours. That is representation doing heavy lifting, and it is the same trick as the letters, numbers standing for something else by agreement.

Sound is representation too. A sound is a wave, a wiggle in the air, and a computer stores music by measuring that wiggle thousands of times every second and writing down each measurement as a number. Play the numbers back in order, at the same speed, and the wiggle is rebuilt and your ear hears the song. The voice you are listening to right now reached you exactly that way, as numbers, measured and rebuilt. Photographs, songs, letters, videos. Different agreements, same foundation. Numbers all the way down to the switches, and switches all the way up to the things you love.

Let me bring this home to your own life, because an idea you cannot use is an idea you do not yet own. Your phone, the one in your pocket, is a counting machine that never stops. When its screen says a photograph takes up three megabytes, it is telling you that the agreements for that picture, its pixels and colours, took about three million bytes to write down. When a message from your business arrives, it arrives as bytes, numbers for letters, flying as electricity and light. When your phone feels slow and someone tells you it needs more memory, they are talking about how many bytes it can keep close at hand, and that is lesson three, so hold the thought. For today, just notice this. Nothing in that phone is stored as the thing itself. There are no tiny letters hiding inside it and no tiny photographs. There are only numbers, stored as patterns of high and low electricity, waiting to be read back through the right agreement. If you understand that sentence, you understand the ground floor of computing, and most people who use computers every day of their lives have never once been told it.

One more small thing before we recap, because it will save you confusion later. People who work with computers often talk about bigger bundles of bytes, and the names sound alike. A kilobyte is a bundle of bytes, roughly one thousand of them, and in the strict counting we will use in this course it is 1024 bytes, which is two doubled ten times. A megabyte is roughly a million bytes. A gigabyte is roughly a billion bytes. Your phone probably holds one hundred and twenty-eight gigabytes or more, which is more than a hundred billion bytes, each one eight switches. The exact bundles get their own careful treatment in lesson three. For now, hear the rhythm of it. Byte, then thousands of bytes, then millions, then billions, and at every level it is still the same humble bit underneath, the single choice between zero and one.

Let us recap, in order, the way we built it. First, a bit is one single choice between two values, zero and one, and inside the machine that choice is carried by electricity, high for one and low for zero, because two far-apart states are almost impossible to misread. Second, binary is counting based on two, and its places are worth one, two, four, eight, sixteen, and thirty-two, each double the last, and you read a binary number by multiplying each digit by its place and adding. Third, every extra bit doubles the number of values, so 5 bits give you 32 values and 10 bits give you 1024 values, and your own two hands proved it. Fourth, a byte is 8 bits and holds 256 values, from zero to 255. Fifth, representation is the great trick, numbers standing for letters, colours, and sounds by agreed codes, so the letter A is the number 65 and a pixel is three numbers that agree to be a colour. And sixth, counting alone is not a computer program. Add representation, stored instructions, conditional branching, and input and output, and counting becomes a word processor. Six points. If those six are clear in your mind, this lesson has done its work.
