# Programs: instructions all the way down

Welcome back. In this room, AI and Computing is learned by doing it, not by watching it. I am Professor Ye-Jin Kim, and this is Computing Foundations. This is lesson four, and its title is Programs: instructions all the way down. We have spent three lessons building the machine. Lesson one gave it numbers, bits and bytes standing for everything by agreed codes. Lesson two gave it a worker, the processor, the very fast and very literal clerk. Lesson three gave it places to keep things, the desk of memory and the filing cabinet of storage. Today we ask the question the whole course has been circling. Who writes the clerk's orders? Where do the instructions come from, and how does a machine that only understands numbers come to run something as human as a messaging app or a photo album? The answer is the program, and by the end of this lesson you will know what a program is, what an algorithm is, how human writing becomes machine numbers, and why the phrase instructions all the way down is not a joke but a precise description of reality.

Start with the definition, and keep it, because everything today hangs on it. A program is a set of instructions stored in memory, written in advance, that the processor fetches and follows one by one. Unpack it with me, the way we unpack things in this room. A set of instructions. Not a wish, not a goal, not a general direction. Instructions, each one small enough for the clerk's fixed list of jobs. Stored in memory. The instructions sit on the desk, in the same memory that holds the numbers, in the same form as the numbers, as patterns of bits. Written in advance. Someone, or something, wrote them before the clerk ever ran, and the clerk's job is not to invent them but to follow them. Fetches and follows one by one. Fetch, decode, execute, the cycle from lesson two, running on a list someone prepared.

That middle idea, stored in memory, deserves a pause, because it is one of the great inventions of the modern world and it hides in plain sight. The instructions are data. The program is kept in the same pigeonholes, in the same bytes, as the numbers it works on. The clerk fetches a byte and, depending on where he is in his work, that byte might be a number to add, a letter to display, or an instruction to obey. This is called the stored program idea, and it is what separates a computer from a calculator. A calculator's instructions are built into its wiring. It can only ever do the jobs it was born doing. A computer's instructions arrive as data, which means you can change what the machine is, entirely, by changing what is stored in its memory. Load one program and the phone is a camera. Load another and the same phone, the same clerk, the same desk, is a map, a bank, a piano, a business office. The hardware does not change at all. The instructions change, and the machine becomes a different machine. Every app you have ever installed was exactly that. New instructions, carried to the cabinet, loaded onto the desk, and the faithful clerk became something new without a single wire moving.

Now, where do the instructions come from? They are written by people, and the writing of them has a name you already know from ordinary speech. Programming. But before a program can be written, something more basic has to exist, and it has a name of its own. An algorithm is a method for solving a problem, written as a clear sequence of steps that always finishes and always gives the answer. Hold that definition next to the clerk and you will see they were made for each other. The clerk needs steps. The algorithm is steps. The clerk needs the steps to be unambiguous, because he is literal. The algorithm demands clarity for the same reason. And the algorithm must finish. A method that might wander forever is not an algorithm, it is a hope.

You already own algorithms, because a recipe is one. A recipe for bread is a sequence of steps, in order, with amounts, with tests along the way. Is the dough risen? Not yet, wait longer, test again. That test is a branch, the same branch the clerk makes. A recipe assumes a cook with judgment, who knows what knead means and what golden looks like. An algorithm for a computer assumes the clerk, who knows nothing that is not written down, so every recipe word must be replaced by exact operations from his little list. Add. Compare. Copy. Jump if. That translation, from human method to clerk-proof steps, is the daily work of the programmer, and it is harder and more interesting than it sounds, as the sandwich in lesson two already taught you.

Let me work a complete example in full, the way I promised this class would always work, so you can see an algorithm become a program and a program become a result. The problem is small and real. A customer has bought three things, and we want the total. The prices, in dollars, are 4, 9, and 2. You know the total is fifteen, and that is why I chose these numbers. Watch the method, not the arithmetic.

Here is the algorithm, in plain words first. Keep a running total, starting at zero. Take each price in turn. Add it to the total. When there are no prices left, the total is the answer. That is the whole method, and notice it would work for three prices or three thousand, unchanged. That is the mark of a real algorithm. It does not care how big the list is.

Now the program, written as instructions the clerk could follow. Instruction one. Put the number zero in a named place called total. Instruction two. Put the number zero in a named place called position, meaning which price we are looking at. Instruction three. If position has reached the end of the list, jump to the final instruction. Instruction four. Take the price at the current position and add it to total. Instruction five. Add one to position. Instruction six. Jump back to instruction three. And the final instruction. The number in total is the answer. Show it.

Those named places deserve their own sentence, because you will meet them in every program ever written. A variable is a named place in memory that holds a value, and the value can change as the program runs. Total is a variable. Position is a variable. The name stays the same, the number inside changes, and the program refers to the place by its name instead of by its address in the pigeonholes. A variable is a labelled box, and programming is, in large part, deciding what boxes you need and what goes in them.

Now run the clerk through the program, step by step, and watch the loop do its work. Total starts at zero, position at zero. Instruction three asks, is position at the end? The list has 3 prices, positions zero, one, and two, so no. Instruction four adds the price at position zero, which is 4. Total is now 4. Instruction five makes position one. Jump back. Is position at the end? No. Add the price at position one, which is 9. Total is now 13. Position becomes two. Jump back. At the end? No. Add the price at position two, which is 2. Total is now 15. Position becomes three. Jump back. Is position at the end now? Yes, three is past the last price. Jump to the final instruction. The answer is 15. The program stops, having added a list of prices, and it would stop with the right answer for any list you handed it. That little machine of seven instructions contains almost everything. Variables, a loop, a test, a branch, an answer. Scale it up, add pictures and buttons and sounds, and you have the apps on your phone. I do not say that loosely. I say it as a plain fact about how those apps are built.

But wait. There is a gap in that story, and a good student spots it. I wrote the program just now in plain English sentences. The clerk cannot read English. The clerk reads numbers, instruction codes from his instruction set, patterns of bits. So how does a program written by a person become the numbers the clerk obeys? This is the translation problem, and solving it is what made programming a craft that more than a handful of people could practise.

The first programmers had no choice but to write the numbers themselves. They wrote machine code, which is the program in its raw form, the actual instruction codes and addresses, number by number. It can be done. The first generation did it, heroically, and it was slow, and it was brutally easy to get wrong, because one wrong digit in a thousand numbers is invisible until the machine does something strange three billion steps later. Human beings are not built to write thousands of bare numbers without error. So we did what human beings always do. We built a tool to do the translating.

The tool rests on a simple, generous idea. Let people write programs in a language closer to human thinking, with words and names and tidy structures, and let a program do the work of turning that writing into machine code. A language of that kind is called a programming language. There are hundreds of them, with names like Python and JavaScript and C, and they differ the way human languages differ, in flavour and in what they are best at, but they share the essential bargain. The person writes in words a person can read and check. The machine receives numbers the clerk can execute. And between the two stands a translator, which is itself a program.

The translator comes in two main kinds, and the difference is worth knowing plainly. A compiler is a program that translates the whole of a human-written program into machine code before any of it runs. The whole book, translated cover to cover, and then the translation is what runs, as many times as you like, at full clerk speed. An interpreter is a program that translates the human-written program as it runs, piece by piece, fetching a line of the human writing, turning it into machine steps, doing them, and moving to the next line. The book is read aloud by a translator standing at your shoulder. Compiled programs generally run faster, because the translating is finished before the running starts. Interpreted programs are generally easier to change and test, because you can adjust the writing and run it again at once. Both are honest tools. Both end at the same place. Numbers, in memory, fetched by the clerk. Instructions all the way down is not a slogan. At the bottom of every layer of convenience, there is the clerk, and his numbers, and nothing else.

Notice what just happened, because it is the deepest pattern in this course. The translator is a program. The program that checks your spelling is a program. And underneath all of them sits one program with a special job, which you glimpsed in lesson three when the machine woke up. The operating system is the program that runs the machine itself, deciding which programs get the clerk, the desk, and the cabinet, and when. When you run two apps at once, it is the operating system that shares the clerk between them, a slice of ticks for this one, a slice for that one, so fast that both seem to run together. When an app asks to save a file, it asks the operating system, which owns the cabinet. When you tap and type, the operating system carries your input to the right program. Names you know, like Android and iOS and Windows, are operating systems. They are programs, written by people, made of instructions, fetched and executed by the same clerk who runs everything else. The machine is programs, managing programs, running programs, all the way down to the clerk and his three steps. There is no magic layer at the bottom. There is a very fast clerk, doing exactly what he is told, and the telling is very, very well organised.

This is your guided moment, and it is the same exercise the professionals do, at kitchen-table scale. Take the worked example and change one thing. Our program added a list of prices. Now, in plain words only, write the algorithm for a program that finds the biggest price in a list instead of the total. Do not write code. Write the method, the way the recipe was written. Keep a named place called biggest. Start it at the first price. Take each remaining price in turn. If a price is bigger than what is in biggest, replace what is in biggest with that price. When the list runs out, biggest is the answer. Now check your own algorithm the way the clerk would. Does it say what to do with the very first price? Does it say when to stop? Does it handle a list of one price, or an empty list? If you found yourself adding small clarifying sentences, you have just felt the real texture of programming. The method is easy to say. The method, said completely, with no gaps for the literal clerk to fall into, is the work. And it is learnable work, step by step, which is why this course teaches it the way it does.

Now the shadow side, honestly faced, because no lesson on programs is complete without it. Programs contain mistakes. A bug is a mistake in the instructions, a place where the program says something other than what its writer meant. Sit with that definition, because it explains the behaviour of every misbehaving machine you have ever met. The clerk is not malfunctioning when a bug bites. He is executing the buggy instruction with perfect fidelity, three billion times a second. The total comes out wrong because instruction four added the wrong box. The app closes suddenly because an instruction sent the clerk to a pigeonhole that held a letter code instead of an instruction, and he faithfully tried to obey a piece of text. Finding and fixing bugs is called debugging, and it is a large share of every programmer's working life, not because programmers are careless, but because programs are long, and a program of a million instructions offers a million places for a gap between what was meant and what was said. The lesson two saying applies here in its strongest form. Garbage in, garbage out. The clerk amplifies the program, wisdom or folly, exactly as written.

Let me tie this lesson back to the worked answer from lesson one, because the course is one building and you should see the beams connect. In lesson one I said a word processor is counting, plus four additions, wearing a friendly face. Representation, so the numbers can stand for letters. Stored instructions, which is today's lesson, the program kept as data the machine can fetch. Conditional branching, so the machine can test and choose. And input and output, so the world can reach the counting and the counting can reach the world. Now you can see the word processor with new eyes. When you type a letter, input carries the key press to the program. The program, an enormous descendant of our seven-instruction example, looks up the letter's number, stores it in memory among the other letters of your document, in variables and lists of variables. When you ask it to count your words, a loop walks the document exactly the way our loop walked the prices, testing each character, adding to a total. When you press save, the operating system carries the document from the desk to the cabinet, as lesson three described. When the program checks your spelling, it compares your words against a stored list, comparison being one of the clerk's oldest jobs. There is nothing in that application that is not built from the pieces on this table. The pieces are few. The arrangements are vast. That is the whole aesthetic of computing, and once you see it, you cannot unsee it, in any program, on any machine, for the rest of your life.

A word about size, to keep your sense of scale honest. Our worked program had 7 instructions. A small phone app might have a million instructions. The operating system beneath it has tens of millions. These numbers are not written by one person typing quickly. They are written by teams, over years, reusing pieces other teams wrote, the way a house is built from bricks nobody on the site fired themselves. Programs that other programmers can reuse as building blocks are called libraries, and modern programming is largely the art of choosing good blocks and writing the comparatively small amount of new instruction that makes this app different from every other app. When an app on your phone updates and the notes say bug fixes, you now know precisely what happened. Someone found places where the instructions said something other than what was meant, corrected the writing, translated it again, and sent the new instructions to your cabinet. The clerk noticed nothing. He simply began fetching a better set of orders.

And a word about trust, because it follows from everything today and it matters for your business and your life. A program does what its instructions say, and its instructions were written by people you will never meet, working for companies with their own interests. The clerk cannot protect you from a program that was written to take more than it should, any more than he can protect you from an honest mistake. He obeys both with the same fidelity. That is why later lessons in this path, and the lessons of your own experience, will keep returning to a simple discipline. Know what program you are running. Know who wrote it. Know what it asks for. The machine's faithfulness is a fact. What the machine is being faithful to is a choice, and the choice is made by people, which means it can be examined by people, including you.

One more building block before the recap, because without it the vast arrangements I keep mentioning would collapse under their own size. Imagine writing out the full instructions for adding a list of prices every single time any program needed a total. Then writing them again for the next program, and the next, thousands of times, with a fresh chance for a bug in every copy. No sane craft works that way, and programming does not either. Instead, a useful set of instructions is written once, given a name, and kept ready. A function is a named set of instructions that does one job and can be used again and again, wherever that job is needed. Our price-adding loop, wrapped up and named total, becomes a function. A program that needs a total does not rewrite the loop. It calls the function, hands it a list, and receives the answer, the way you hand a trusted worker a stack of invoices and receive a total. Functions can be built out of other functions, the way our total could be used by a larger function that prepares a whole bill, adding the prices, then adding tax, then formatting the result for the customer. Programs within programs, instructions calling instructions, each layer standing on the one below. That is the honest meaning of the title of this lesson. It is instructions, arranged in layers, all the way down, until at the very bottom the layers run out and there is only the clerk, his three steps, and the numbers. Every layer is somebody's writing. Every layer can be read, checked, and fixed by somebody who knows how. There is no layer where the magic lives and the explaining stops.

This layering is also why a person can learn to program in stages, and why this course is ordered the way it is. You do not need to hold a million instructions in your head, any more than a builder holds a whole house in his hands at once. You hold one function, one job, clearly. You test it the way we traced the little program, with numbers whose answers you already know. Then you trust it, the way you trust a finished wall, and you build on top of it. The professionals who write the programs your business runs on work exactly this way, one clear layer at a time, and the distance between their work and the trace you did today is a distance of practice and scale, not of kind. I want you to remember that. The clerk does not know whether he is running seven instructions or seventy million. He fetches, he decodes, he executes, and the layers above him, built by patient people out of small clear pieces, are what turn that simple faithfulness into a working world.

And one short story, because this class learns from stories as well as from definitions, and this one is true to the way the work feels. A programmer writes a program late at night. She traces it in her head the way we traced ours, and it works on paper. She runs it, and the total comes out wrong, not wildly wrong, just wrong by the price of the first item, every time. The clerk is not broken. The program has a gap exactly where lesson two said gaps live. Her loop tests whether the position has reached the end before it adds, and her starting position skips the first price entirely. The fix is two instructions changing places. Two instructions, in a program she wrote in an hour, and the hunt for them took longer than the writing. I tell you this so that when a program of yours, or a program you paid for, misbehaves in some small stubborn way, you will picture the truth of it. Somewhere in a long list of faithful instructions, one or two are saying something slightly different from what their writer meant, and a person with patience and a worked example can find them. That is debugging, from the inside, and it is ordinary, honourable work, not a sign that the machine is haunted or that the writer was a fool.

Let us recap, in order. First, a program is a set of instructions stored in memory, written in advance, that the processor fetches and follows one by one. Second, the stored program idea means instructions are data, kept in the same memory as numbers, which is why one machine can become a camera, a map, or an office just by loading different instructions. Third, an algorithm is a method for solving a problem, written as a clear sequence of steps that always finishes and always gives the answer. Fourth, a variable is a named place in memory that holds a value, and the value can change as the program runs. Fifth, people write programs in programming languages, and a translator program turns that writing into machine code, either all at once beforehand, which is a compiler, or piece by piece while it runs, which is an interpreter. Sixth, the operating system is the program that runs the machine itself, sharing the clerk, the desk, and the cabinet among all the other programs. And seventh, a bug is a mistake in the instructions, a place where the program says something other than what its writer meant, and debugging is the work of finding and fixing those places.
