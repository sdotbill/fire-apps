# Files, formats, and where your data actually lives

Welcome back. In this room, AI and Computing is learned by doing it, not by watching it. I am Professor Ye-Jin Kim, and this is Computing Foundations. This is lesson six, the final lesson of the course, and its title is Files, formats, and where your data actually lives. You have built the whole machine now. Bits and bytes. The clerk and his three steps. The desk and the filing cabinet. Programs, instructions all the way down. Networks, packets crossing the planet. Today we finish with the thing you actually touch every day, the file. The photograph. The document. The song. The spreadsheet with your business numbers in it. By the end of this lesson you will know what a file really is, why the same photograph can be a huge file or a small one depending on its format, what people are truly saying when they say your data is in the cloud, and how many places your most important photograph is sitting in right now. That last answer surprises almost everyone, and it matters, because some of those places are safer than you think and one of them is less safe than you hope.

The definition first, one last time, plain and keepable. A file is a named collection of bytes, kept in storage, that a program can open, read, and write as one thing. Unpack it. A collection of bytes. Underneath the icon and the friendly name, a file is bytes, lesson one's bytes, numbers in rows, nothing else. Named. The name is how you and the machine refer to the collection as a whole, instead of pointing at a stretch of the cabinet. Kept in storage. Files live in the cabinet, lesson three's cabinet, because files are for keeping, and the desk is only where a file is worked on while a program has it open. And the last clause is the soul of it. A program can open, read, and write it as one thing. When you open a photograph, you do not fetch its millions of bytes one by one. You name the file, and the operating system hands the whole collection to the program as a single object. The file is the unit of keeping, the way the byte is the unit of counting and the packet is the unit of travelling. Everything you own, digitally speaking, you own as files.

The name itself teaches more than people realise, so let us look at one closely. A file's name usually comes in two parts, joined by a dot. The first part is the name you chose, the human part, like a label on a folder that says what is inside. The second part, after the dot, is called the extension, and it is a short code, usually three or four letters, that announces the file's format. You have seen them all your life. A name ending in the letters for a text file. A photograph ending in the letters of its picture format. A document ending in the letters of the program family that made it. The extension is a promise, made by whoever wrote the file, about how the bytes inside are arranged. And that word, format, is the second great idea of this lesson, so here is its definition, and keep it next to the first. A format is the agreement that says how the bytes inside a file are arranged, so that a program opening the file knows what the numbers mean.

You should feel a click of recognition there, because a format is lesson one's representation, grown up and given a filing system. Representation said, numbers stand for letters and colours by agreed codes. A format is the whole agreement, written down, for one kind of thing. The format for a plain text file says, these bytes are numbers for characters, one after another, in this code, and nothing else is hidden in here. The format for a common kind of photograph says, these first bytes describe the picture's width and height, and the rest are compressed colour information, arranged in this exact pattern, to be rebuilt by this exact method. A program that knows the format can open the file and make sense of it. A program that does not know the format sees only numbers with no agreement attached, a book in a language it cannot read. This is why a file can be perfectly intact and perfectly useless at the same time. The bytes survived. The agreement is missing. When a customer sends your business a file your programs cannot open, nothing is broken and nothing is lost. You are simply holding numbers whose arrangement nobody on your machine has been told. The cure is a program that knows that format, and now you understand the cure instead of just hoping for it.

Now the question everyone asks about photographs, answered properly. Why is one photograph a small file and another, of what looks like the same scene, an enormous one? Three reasons, and you have the tools for all three. First, the count of pixels. A modern phone camera captures a picture of perhaps 4000 pixels wide by 3000 pixels tall. Multiply, the way we multiply in this class. 4000 times 3000 is 12000000 pixels, twelve million dots, each one needing its colour stored. Second, the bytes per pixel. Lesson one taught you a pixel's colour is three numbers, three bytes, one each for red, green, and blue. Twelve million pixels at three bytes each is 36000000 bytes, thirty-six megabytes, for the raw picture with nothing clever done to it. Third, and this is where the format earns its keep, compression. Compression is the art of describing the same thing with fewer bytes, by finding patterns in the numbers and writing the patterns instead. A photograph of a blue sky has thousands of neighbouring pixels that are nearly the same blue. The raw format writes that blue out in full, thousands of times. A compressing format writes, in effect, this blue, repeated across this whole stretch, and moves on. The common photograph format on phones does this kind of work aggressively and well, and it brings our thirty-six megabyte picture down to about 3 megabytes, roughly a twelfth of the raw size, small enough to send as those two thousand packets from lesson five. Same camera. Same scene. The file's size is mostly a story about how cleverly its format describes patterns, and now you can read that story.

Compression has a fork in it, and an honest course names both branches. Some compression is lossless, which means the original numbers can be rebuilt exactly, every last bit, from the compressed file. Nothing is thrown away. The patterns are merely described more cleverly. Text files and business documents are compressed this way when they are compressed at all, because a contract that comes back almost identical is not identical, and almost is not good enough for words and numbers that carry money. Other compression is lossy, which means some of the original numbers are thrown away on purpose, because a program has judged that your eye or your ear will not miss them. The photograph format on your phone is lossy. It keeps the patterns your eye is sensitive to and quietly discards fine detail your eye would never have caught, and in exchange it gives you that twelvefold saving. Lossy is not cheating. It is a bargain, openly made, detail traded for size, and for photographs of birthdays and finished jobs it is a bargain worth making every time. But know which bargain a file has made. A lossy copy can never be turned back into the original, because the discarded numbers are gone. If a file is the only record of something that matters, the original, uncompressed or losslessly kept, is the one your cabinet should hold.

A file also carries a quiet passenger, and it explains a small mystery you may have wondered about. Alongside the main content, many formats store metadata, which means data about the data. A photograph's file commonly records when it was taken, what camera took it, its dimensions, and often where on Earth it was taken, written there by the phone's sense of its own location. That is how your photo app can show you a map of your pictures, and sort them by the day, without you ever typing a word. The file told it. Metadata is useful, and it is also worth knowing about with clear eyes, because a photograph you send to a stranger may carry the location of your home or your business inside it, in numbers you never see on the screen. Lesson five taught you to think about who can read what you send. Metadata is part of that thinking. The file is never only the picture. It is the picture, plus its papers.

Now, organisation, briefly, because a cabinet without order is a pile. Files are grouped into folders, which are named collections of files, and folders can sit inside folders, the way a cabinet has drawers and each drawer has dividers. Every file therefore has not just a name but a place, and the full description of its place, folder inside folder inside folder, is called its path. When a program cannot find a file, it is usually the path that is wrong, not the file. The file is sitting exactly where it was put. The directions were wrong. And deleting, since we are being honest about everything today, deserves its sentence. When you delete a file, the machine mostly removes its name and its place from the cabinet's index and marks the space as free. The bytes themselves often linger for a while, until new files are written over them. Deleting is closer to tearing the label off a folder and pencilling the shelf as empty than it is to burning the contents. That is why deleted files can sometimes be recovered, and why a machine being sold or given away deserves more than a quick delete of the things you cared about. The cabinet remembers more than its index admits.

And now the title's final question, the one I promised would surprise you. Where does your data actually live? Start with the answer you already know. A file you saved lives in the storage of the machine you saved it on, in flash chips in your phone, on the drive in your laptop. That is place one, and for an older way of computing it was the only place. But your phone is almost certainly doing something else as well, quietly, in the background, over the network from lesson five. It is copying your files to servers owned by the company whose services you use, and those copies are what people mean by the cloud. So let us say it with total plainness, because this sentence dissolves a decade of fog. The cloud is other people's servers, computers in data centres, rented out as storage and programs that your machine reaches over the network. There is no sky involved. There are buildings, full of servers, with power and cooling and staff, in particular places under particular laws, and your photographs are on their drives. That is not a reason for alarm. Those buildings are usually far better guarded, powered, and backed up than any phone in any pocket. But it is a reason for clear thinking. A file in the cloud lives in two kinds of place at once, your cabinet and theirs, under your account, reachable by your password, which is why lesson five's habits about passwords and lures guard your files as surely as any lock guards a drawer.

The copying has a name of its own. When your phone and the cloud keep the same file in step, so that a photograph taken in the morning is on the server by the time you sit down, and a document edited on the laptop appears on the phone, that keeping-in-step is called synchronisation, or sync for short. Sync is a program, of course, lesson four's programs again, watching for changes and sending packets, lesson five's packets, carrying the changed bytes to the other copies. Sync is why your data feels like it lives everywhere. It does live in several places, and the places are kept in agreement by tireless, literal programs doing exactly what they were told.

Which brings us to the most practical idea in the entire course, and I have saved it for last on purpose, because you now have everything needed to understand why it is true. A backup is a second copy of a file, kept in a different place from the first, made so that losing the first copy is an inconvenience instead of a disaster. Read the middle of that definition again. In a different place. A second copy on the same phone is not a backup against the phone being lost, because both copies go in the same moment. A copy in the cloud is a genuine second place, which is the cloud's deepest virtue, deeper than convenience. And here is the rule that follows, and I give it to you as the working law of this whole subject. If a file exists in only one place, it is one accident away from not existing at all. Phones are dropped in lakes. Cabinets fail without warning. Buildings, even good ones, have bad days. The files that survive history, the photographs your grandchildren will hold, the records your business stands on, are the files that lived in more than one place when the accident came. Count your copies of anything that matters. If the count is one, you do not have a keeping problem tomorrow. You have one today, and it is fixable today, cheaply, with the tools already in your pocket.

This is your guided moment, the last one of the course, and it gathers the whole lesson into one honest inventory. Take one photograph that matters to you, the one you would rescue first. Now count its homes, out loud, one by one. It lives in your phone's storage, in the flash chips, as a lossy compressed file in the phone's photograph format. That is one. If your phone syncs, it lives again on a server in a data centre, in the same format, under your account. That is two. If you sent it to someone you love, it lives on their phone as well, and probably in their cloud too. That is three, and four. If it appears in a message thread, the messaging service's servers hold their own copy. The count grows quickly, and notice the two lessons hiding in it. Your data is harder to lose than you feared, because it has quietly become a small crowd of copies. And your data is in more hands than you remembered, because every copy is a place, and every place has keepers. Both lessons are the same lesson, seen from two sides. Data lives in places, places are physical, and a person who knows the places can make clear-eyed decisions about all of it. You are now such a person.

Before the recap, let me give you one more worked example, small and complete, because file sizes for words behave very differently from file sizes for pictures, and the difference is worth feeling in numbers. Take a full page of writing, a long letter to a customer. A page holds roughly 2000 characters, letters, spaces, and punctuation together. In a plain text format, each character is one byte, lesson one's agreement, one number per letter. So the whole page, the whole letter, is about 2000 bytes, two kilobytes, for everything on it. Now compare the photograph we counted earlier, three megabytes after compression. Three megabytes is 3000 kilobytes. Your photograph weighs as much as 1500 pages of writing. Fifteen hundred letters to customers, or one picture of the finished job. That is why your cabinet fills with photographs and videos while a lifetime of documents barely makes a dent, and why the businesses that drown in storage costs are always drowning in images, never in words. Words are nearly free to keep. Pictures are the heavy furniture. When you plan where your business files will live, plan around the furniture.

The same arithmetic, turned around, is quietly hopeful for your records. A lifetime of invoices, contracts, and customer lists, kept as documents, is a rounding error in a modern cabinet. There is no storage excuse for keeping only one copy of them, and after today's lesson there is no knowledge excuse either. The copies cost almost nothing. The loss, if it comes, costs whatever the records were worth, which for a business can be everything. I do not teach this as fear. I teach it as arithmetic. Small numbers on one side, large numbers on the other, and a decision that makes itself once both sides are on the table where you can see them. That, in the end, is what this whole course has been training. Not fear of the machine, and not worship of it either. Clear numbers, plain definitions, and decisions that make themselves.

One last distinction, drawn the way I like distinctions drawn, between two words people use as if they were twins. Saving and backing up are not the same act. Saving, from lesson three, is the walk from the desk to the cabinet of the same machine. It protects your work from the power going. Backing up is a copy arriving in a different place entirely, another machine, another building, another keeper. It protects your work from the machine going. A man can save faithfully every hour of his life and still lose everything in one bad minute, because all his saving happened inside a single cabinet. And a man can back up once, years ago, and lose everything since, because the copies were never kept in step. The complete habit is both, saving as you work and copies flowing to a second place as you go, and the beautiful fact of modern machines is that both can be made automatic, done by programs, while you think about your actual work. Set it once, with clear eyes, knowing exactly what each copy is and where it sleeps. Then get on with your life, in a house where the records can survive the accidents. That is the practical summit of this course, and it is entirely within your reach tonight.
