# Networks: computers talking, and what can go wrong

Welcome back. In this room, AI and Computing is learned by doing it, not by watching it. I am Professor Ye-Jin Kim, and this is Computing Foundations. This is lesson five, and its title is Networks: computers talking, and what can go wrong. For four lessons, our computer has been alone. A brilliant clerk, a desk, a filing cabinet, programs written in advance, all of it inside one box. But the computer in your pocket is almost never alone, and it is not very interesting alone. Its photographs travel to other machines. Its messages arrive from across oceans. Its programs consult computers in buildings you will never see, in towns you cannot name. Today the machines start talking to each other, and by the end of this lesson you will know what a network is, how a message actually crosses the world, why it travels in small pieces with addresses on them, and the honest list of what can go wrong along the way, including the part where strangers try to listen in, and what honest people do about that.

The definition first, as always, plain and keepable. A network is a set of computers connected so that they can exchange data, pass messages, and share what they have. Two computers and a connection is a network, the smallest one there is. Your phone and your laptop, sharing a photograph across your kitchen, are a network at work. The internet, which we will come to properly, is the largest network, built by joining millions of smaller networks together until the whole planet is one conversation. The connection itself can be a wire, carrying electricity, or a glass fibre, carrying light, or open air, carrying radio waves, which is what Wi-Fi is. Wi-Fi is simply a network connection made by radio instead of by wire. The clerk does not care which it is. Bits are bits, and a bit that arrives by radio is worth exactly as much as a bit that arrives by copper.

Now, how does a message actually travel? Here is where most people's picture is wrong, so let me replace it with the right one. The wrong picture is a pipe, one continuous stream flowing from your phone to your friend's phone, the way water flows through a hose. The right picture is the postal service. When you send a photograph to a friend, the photograph does not travel whole. It is broken into small pieces, each piece is put in its own envelope with an address on it, the envelopes travel by whatever routes are open, some fast, some slow, some by different roads entirely, and at the far end the envelopes are gathered, checked, and opened in the right order, and the photograph is rebuilt. If that sounds inefficient, hold your judgment. By the end of this section you will see it is one of the smartest designs in all of computing.

The pieces have a name. A packet is a small piece of a message, travelling with the address of where it is going and a note of where it belongs in the whole. Every word of that matters. Small piece, because small pieces are easy to move, easy to resend, and easy to share a road with. The address of where it is going, because the road is shared by billions of envelopes and each one must be directable on its own. And a note of where it belongs in the whole, a sequence number, because envelopes that travel different roads arrive out of order, and the receiver must be able to lay them back in sequence, envelope twelve after envelope eleven, before opening them.

How small is small? A typical packet carries about 1500 bytes of actual message, plus its addressing. Now let us count, the way this class counts, in a worked example you can check. Suppose you send a photograph that takes up 3 megabytes. In strict bundles, that is 3145728 bytes. Divide by the 1500 bytes each packet can carry. 3145728 divided by 1500 is about 2097. Your one photograph crosses the world as roughly 2097 separate packets, each with the same destination address, each with its own sequence number, travelling independently, and your friend's phone rebuilds the picture from the pile. When the picture appears on her screen a second later, whole and perfect, that rebuilding is part of what just happened, invisibly, at machine speed. I never stop finding that wonderful. A photograph, shredded into two thousand envelopes, thrown onto a shared planetary road system, and reassembled without a single piece out of place, as a routine event, billions of times a day.

The address on each packet deserves its own attention, because addresses are where networks become a system instead of a tangle. Every machine on a network has an address, a number that identifies it, the way a house has a number on its street. On the internet, the classic form of the address is 4 numbers separated by dots, each number between zero and two hundred and fifty-five. You will recognise that range. Zero to two hundred and fifty-five is exactly one byte's worth of values, lesson one's byte, so the classic internet address is 4 bytes long, one byte per number. That address is called an IP address, and IP stands for Internet Protocol, a name you are about to meet again. When your phone sends a packet, it writes two addresses on the envelope. Its own address, so the answer can find its way back, and the destination's address, so the packet can find its way there. A packet without both is a letter with no destination and no return address, and the network treats it the way you would. It goes nowhere useful.

Who reads the addresses and moves the packets along? Machines called routers. A router is a computer whose whole job is to receive packets, read their destination addresses, and send them onward along the best available road toward that destination. Picture a sorting office that never sleeps and sorts at the speed of light. Your packet will usually pass through several routers on its journey, each one making one decision. Not the whole route. Just the next hop, the next office closer to the destination. This is a profound design choice, and it is why the internet can be so large. No single machine needs to know the whole map. Each router knows its neighbours and a sense of direction, the way you can cross a country by following signs town by town without holding the whole road atlas in your head at once. Local knowledge, faithfully applied, packet by packet, produces a global result. You have seen that pattern before in this course. Simple steps, arranged well, at enormous scale. It is the clerk's philosophy, grown up into a planet.

For any of this to work between machines built by different companies, in different countries, in different decades, the talking itself must follow agreed rules. You have met this idea twice already, as the codes of lesson one. Here it grows up and gets its grown-up name. A protocol is an agreed set of rules for how computers talk, so that a machine built by one company can understand a machine built by another. Protocols decide everything about the conversation. How a conversation starts. How packets are addressed. How the receiver says, I got that one, send the next. How errors are reported. How the conversation ends. The Internet Protocol, the IP in the address, is the protocol that handles addressing and routing, the envelope system itself. Its usual partner is the protocol that handles reliability, checking that every packet arrived and asking for the missing ones again, and its initials are TCP. When people say TCP/IP, they are naming the pair of agreements the whole internet runs on, the envelope rules and the delivery-checking rules together. Nobody owns these agreements. They are published, public, and free for anyone to follow, which is precisely why everyone did, and why a phone built this year can talk to a machine built before its owner was born.

Two more roles, and then we can trace a whole conversation. When your phone asks for something, a web page, your messages, a map, it is playing the role called the client. The client is the machine that asks. The machine that answers has the other famous name. A server is a computer that waits for requests and answers them, serving programs, pages, and files to the machines that ask. A server is not a different species of machine. It is an ordinary computer, usually a powerful one in a building full of its colleagues, running a program whose whole job is to wait politely, listen for requests, and respond. Your messages live on servers. When a friend sends you a message and your phone is off, the message waits on a server, patient as a clerk with a pigeonhole, until your phone wakes and asks for it. That is why the message arrives the moment you switch on. It was never travelling all that time. It was being served, held ready by a machine whose job is holding things ready.

And a distinction that will keep you clear-headed for years. The internet and the web are not the same thing. The internet is the network itself, the roads, the envelopes, the sorting offices, the whole planetary system for moving packets. The web is one famous use of those roads, the system of pages and links you visit with a browser. Your email also rides the internet, and your messages, and your video calls, and none of those are the web. The web is a city. The internet is the road network the city is built on, and other cities use the same roads. People who confuse the two end up confused about everything built on them, and you will not be one of those people.

This is your guided moment, and it is a trace, the way we traced the little program in lesson four. Trace one message with me, from your phone to a friend's phone, naming each stage out loud. Stage one. You type the message and press send. Your phone's program turns the letters into numbers, lesson one, and breaks the numbers into packets, each with your address and your friend's address, or rather the address of the server that serves your friend, written on it. Stage two. The packets leave your phone by radio, Wi-Fi or the mobile network, to the first router they meet. Stage three. Router by router, hop by hop, each reading only the destination and choosing the next road, the packets cross whatever distance separates you, under streets and, if your friend is far away, under an ocean, as light in glass fibres. Stage four. The server receives the packets, checks them against their sequence numbers, asks again for any that went missing, and rebuilds your message whole. Stage five. Your friend's phone, asking politely as a client does, receives the message from the server and rebuilds it on its desk, and the letters appear. Five stages, and every technology in this lesson appears in the trace exactly once, doing its own job. If you can retell that trace without me, you understand how the internet works at the level most of its users never reach. Try it once more in your head before we move on. Phone, router, roads, server, phone.

Now the second half of the title, faced squarely. What can go wrong? The honest list, in order of ordinariness. Packets get lost. A router is overwhelmed, a road is cut, a radio signal fades at the wrong moment, and an envelope simply never arrives. This is so ordinary that the system is built expecting it. The checking protocol notices the gap in the sequence numbers, and the receiver asks for that packet again, and the sender sends another copy. You never see it happen. You only see its cost, the slight delay while a video stutters or a page hesitates. Roads get crowded. When too many packets want the same road at the same moment, they queue, and everything slows, the way traffic slows, and for the same reason. That crowding is called congestion, and the delay it causes, the time a packet spends travelling and queuing, is called latency. A connection can have enormous capacity and still feel slow if its latency is high, the way a wide highway with a two-hour jam moves many cars and pleases none of them. Addresses get confused, or machines go away. The server you are asking may be switched off, broken, or simply gone, and your request travels faithfully to a door nobody answers. And cables get cut, storms take down radio towers, and whole regions can fall quiet for a while. The network's great strength is that it was designed for exactly this world. Many roads, independent packets, local decisions. Cut one road and the routers, faithful clerks that they are, simply start choosing the next best road, and the conversation flows around the damage like water around a stone.

But there is a darker item on the list of what can go wrong, and a foundations course that skipped it would be failing you. Packets travel through machines owned by strangers. A letter in the post can be steamed open. A packet can be copied as it passes, and neither sender nor receiver would ever know. On an open network, anything you send can, in principle, be read by someone it was never meant for, and some of what you send is worth reading. Passwords. Bank details. Private messages. Business numbers. So the network's users developed the same answer the letter-writers developed centuries ago, improved by mathematics. Sealed envelopes, of a very strong kind.

The sealing is called encryption. Encryption is scrambling a message with a secret key so that anyone who intercepts it sees only nonsense, and only the intended reader can unscramble it. A key, here, is a secret number, held by the people who are allowed to read. The scrambling is not a simple shuffle. It is arithmetic, done by programs, using the clerk's own operations at enormous scale, arranged so that unscrambling without the key would take longer than anyone can wait, while unscrambling with the key is instant. When you see a small padlock sign beside a web address, it means the conversation between your machine and that server is encrypted. The packets still travel the shared roads. Strangers can still copy them as they pass. What they copy is nonsense to them, and that nonsense is the whole point. Your business can cross a planet of strangers' machines and remain your business. In lesson four I told you the clerk obeys any program faithfully, including a dishonest one. Encryption is the other side of that coin. Faithful arithmetic, arranged by honest people, standing guard over everything you send.

The remaining danger is not technical at all, and it is the one that harms the most people. The network carries messages from anyone, including people who lie about who they are. A message can claim to be from your bank and be from a thief. A page can wear the colours of a company you trust and belong to a stranger. This trick has a name, phishing, which is fishing with a lure, and the lure is your trust. No protocol can fully protect you, because the attack is not on the machines. It is on the reader. The defences are habits, and they belong in a foundations course, so here they are, plainly. Be suspicious of urgency, because honest institutions rarely demand instant action by message. Check where a message actually came from and where its links actually lead, not where they claim to lead. And remember lesson four's discipline, which now covers the whole network. Know what program you are running, know who you are really talking to, and treat a request for your password or your money arriving out of the blue as a lure until it proves otherwise. The clerk cannot smell a lie. You can learn to. That is a human job, and it stays human.

One more piece of honest arithmetic before the recap, because network speeds are advertised at everyone and understood by few. A connection's speed is measured in bits per second, how many bits the road can carry each second. A typical home connection might offer 100 megabits per second, one hundred million bits streaming past. Notice the unit. Bits, not bytes. Advertisements count in bits because the number is eight times bigger, and now you know a byte is 8 bits, so you can translate for yourself. A file of 100 megabytes is 800 megabits of travelling, and on a perfect 100 megabit connection it would take 8 seconds to arrive. Divide the file, counted in bits, by the road, counted in bits per second, and you have the seconds. Real roads are never perfect, so real times are longer, but the arithmetic gives you the floor, and a person who can compute the floor cannot be fooled by the ceiling painted on the box.

Wait. A careful student has spotted a gap, and I want to honour it before the recap. I told you every machine has a number for an address, and packets travel by numbers. But you do not type numbers when you visit a website or open an app. You use names. Names are for people. Numbers are for machines. Something must translate between them, every time, invisibly, and that something is one of the hardest-working agreements on the internet. The Domain Name System is the internet's phone book, a vast distributed directory that translates the names people remember into the numbers machines use. When you ask for a site by name, your machine first consults this directory, receives the number belonging to that name, and only then writes its packets, because packets cannot be addressed to a name any more than a letter can be delivered to a nickname. The directory is distributed, meaning there is no single phone book in one building that the world depends on. Copies and partial copies live on servers everywhere, asking each other, caching answers, updating constantly. If the directory fails, the roads are still there and the machines are still there, but nobody can find anybody, which is why a certain kind of internet failure feels so strange. Nothing is broken, and nothing can be reached. You now know enough to diagnose that feeling. The names stopped resolving into numbers.

Your own home sits inside this picture in a way worth seeing once, clearly. The box your provider gave you, the one with the blinking lights, is doing three of this lesson's jobs at once. It is a router, sorting packets between your machines and the world. It runs your Wi-Fi, the radio connection your phone joins. And it gives each of your machines a local address, so the printer, the laptop, and the phone can find each other across the kitchen without troubling the planet. When the connection at home fails and someone tells you to turn the box off and on again, you can now translate that advice into clerk language. You are restarting a small, faithful computer whose programs have got themselves into a state their writers did not intend, and a fresh start hands it clean instructions and an empty desk. It works for the same reason it worked in lesson two. The clerk was never broken. He was trapped, and you have freed him.

And notice, as we stand back, what the network has done to the meaning of the word computer. In lesson one, a computer was a box that counts. By lesson four, it was a box that counts, remembers, and follows programs. Now the box is a doorway. The programs it runs reach across the world as a matter of routine. The files it opens may live on another continent. The friend it talks to is a machine in a rack in a building with no sign on it. Nothing about the clerk changed. He still fetches, decodes, and executes, three billion times a second, in the phone in your hand. What changed is the reach of his errands. His instructions now routinely say, ask a machine across the ocean, and wait for its answer, and the waiting, which lesson three taught you to respect, is measured in the latency of roads and the patience of servers. The whole modern world is that sentence, running at scale. You understand it now from the bit upward, and very few people on Earth can honestly say that.

One more ordinary miracle deserves a sentence before we close, because you use it every day and it gathers the whole lesson into one gesture. A video call. Your voice is measured thousands of times a second and turned into numbers, lesson one's representation, working on sound. The numbers are broken into packets, addressed, and sent hop by hop across the roads, and here the design shows its judgement, because for a live voice a late packet is a useless packet. There is no time to ask for it again. So the call does not ask. It lets the missing sliver of sound go, covers the gap as gracefully as it can, and keeps the conversation in the present moment, which is why a bad connection makes a voice crackle and drop syllables instead of pausing to repair itself. Email makes the opposite choice. A missing packet in a message is worth waiting for, so the checking protocol asks, and asks again, until every envelope has arrived. Same roads, same packets, two different bargains with loss, chosen by the programs to fit the job. When you know that, the behaviour of your own machines stops being weather and becomes engineering, visible and understandable, and that change, from weather to engineering, is the real product of this entire course. Hold onto that thought when your own connection next misbehaves in the middle of something that matters. Instead of shaking the phone, you will find yourself asking the engineer's questions. Is it the road, the address, the server, or the crowd? Four suspects, and this lesson has taught you how each one behaves and how each one announces itself.

Let us recap, in order. First, a network is a set of computers connected so that they can exchange data, pass messages, and share what they have. Second, messages do not travel whole. They are broken into packets, and a packet is a small piece of a message, travelling with the address of where it is going and a note of where it belongs in the whole. Third, every machine has an address, the classic internet address being 4 numbers, each one byte's worth, from zero to two hundred and fifty-five. Fourth, routers are the sorting offices, reading each packet's destination and choosing the next hop, needing only local knowledge to produce a global result. Fifth, a protocol is an agreed set of rules for how computers talk, and the internet runs on such agreements, addressing and routing in one, delivery-checking in the other. Sixth, the machine that asks is the client, and a server is a computer that waits for requests and answers them. Seventh, the internet is the road system, and the web is only one city built on it. And eighth, what can go wrong is a real list. Packets are lost and resent, roads congest and latency grows, machines go silent, strangers listen, which encryption answers by scrambling, and liars send lures, which only your own habits answer.
