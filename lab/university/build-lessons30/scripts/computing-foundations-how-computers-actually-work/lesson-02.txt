# The processor: a very fast, very literal clerk

Welcome back. In this room, AI and Computing is learned by doing it, not by watching it. I am Professor Ye-Jin Kim, and this is Computing Foundations. This is lesson two, and its title is The processor: a very fast, very literal clerk. In lesson one, you learned what the computer stores. Bits, bytes, numbers standing for letters and colours by agreed codes. Today you meet the worker who does something with all those stored numbers. Every computer has one, your phone has one, and by the end of this lesson you will know exactly what he does, how fast he does it, and why you must never, ever blame him for faithfully following a foolish instruction. He has no judgment. That is not an insult. It is his job description, and understanding it will save you from a hundred confusions later.

Let me unpack the title first, the way I like to do, piece by piece. Very. Fast. Literal. Clerk. Four words, and each one earns its place. A clerk is a person who works through paperwork, step by step, following written instructions, fetching what is needed, doing what the paper says, and filing the result. That is the processor. The word processor itself, in this lesson, does not mean the writing program from lesson one. Here, the processor is a piece of hardware, a chip, the working part of the machine. Its full name is the central processing unit, and people shorten that to CPU. Central, because everything passes through it. Processing, because it works on what passes through. Unit, because it is one part among several in the whole machine. When you hear CPU from today onward, picture the clerk at his desk, and you will not go far wrong.

Here is the definition I want you to keep, word for word if you can. The processor is the part of the computer that follows instructions, one after another, exactly as they are written. Follows instructions. One after another. Exactly as written. There is no fourth idea hiding in that sentence, and the third idea, exactly as written, is the one that runs the world. We will spend a good part of this lesson on what exactly as written really means, because it explains both the power of computers and most of their famous failures.

First, what does the clerk actually do all day? His whole working life is one small cycle of 3 steps, repeated without pause. The steps have names, and the names are plain. Fetch. Decode. Execute. Say them with me. Fetch, decode, execute. Fetch, decode, execute. That loop is the heartbeat of every computer ever built, and we are going to take each step slowly.

Fetch means the clerk gets the next instruction. The instructions live in the computer's memory, which is lesson three's territory, so for today just picture a long numbered row of pigeonholes, each one holding either a number or an instruction. The clerk keeps a note of where he is in that row. That note has a name, and the name is the program counter. The program counter is simply the clerk's note of which instruction comes next, the number of the pigeonhole he should visit. Fetch means he walks to that pigeonhole, takes out the instruction written there, and brings it back to his desk. Then, as a matter of routine, he updates his note to point at the next pigeonhole, so he is ready for the next fetch. Small step. Utterly reliable. Done billions of times without a sigh.

Decode means the clerk works out what the instruction is asking for. Remember from lesson one that everything inside the machine is numbers, and instructions are no exception. An instruction arrives at the desk as a number, a pattern of bits in a byte or a few bytes. Part of that pattern is a code number that names the job to be done, and the rest of the pattern names what to do the job on. Decode is the moment the clerk looks at the code number and says, in effect, ah, this is an adding instruction, and these are the two numbers it wants me to add. There is a fixed list of jobs the clerk knows how to do, and that list is called the instruction set. The instruction set is the complete list of jobs a processor can be told to do, and it is not long. Adding. Subtracting. Comparing two numbers to see which is bigger. Copying a number from one place to another. Jumping to a different instruction when a condition holds, which is the conditional branching you met in lesson one. A few dozen kinds of jobs, a few hundred at the most, and every program you have ever used, every game, every message, every photograph being shown, is built by stringing those few simple jobs together in enormous, careful crowds. Complexity in computing never comes from complicated steps. It comes from simple steps, numbered in the billions, arranged with great care.

Execute means the clerk does the job. If the instruction said add, he adds, using a part of the chip built for arithmetic, and he puts the answer in a small holding place on his own desk. Those holding places have a name too. They are called registers. A register is one of the processor's own tiny storage slots, the fastest storage in the whole machine, sitting right where the work happens. Think of the difference between the top of your desk and a warehouse across town. Registers are the top of the desk. Only a handful of numbers fit there, the ones being worked on right now, but the clerk can reach them instantly, because they are already in his hands. Execute, put the result in a register or send it back to memory, and then the cycle begins again. Fetch the next instruction. Decode it. Execute it. Fetch, decode, execute, for as long as the machine is switched on and there are instructions to follow.

Let me work a tiny example in full, because nobody should have to guess what this looks like when it runs. Picture a short program of 4 instructions, stored in memory, whose whole job is to add two numbers and keep the answer. The numbers we will add are seven and five, and you already know the answer is twelve, which is exactly why I chose them. A worked example should use numbers you can check, so you can watch the clerk instead of worrying about the arithmetic.

Instruction one says, fetch the number seven from memory and put it in a register. The clerk fetches the instruction, decodes it, and executes it, and now his first register holds seven. Instruction two says, fetch the number five from memory and put it in a second register. Fetch, decode, execute. The second register holds five. Instruction three says, add the two registers together and put the answer in the first register. Fetch, decode, execute. The adding part of the chip takes seven and five and makes twelve, and the first register now holds twelve. Instruction four says, copy the number in the first register back into memory, so the answer is kept. Fetch, decode, execute. Twelve is stored. The program ends. Four instructions, twelve steps of the cycle, and the machine has done a sum.

Now, that might sound like a lot of ceremony for seven plus five, and at human speed it is. You did that sum in your head before I finished the sentence. But notice what the ceremony buys. The clerk never gets bored. He never misreads a seven as a one because his eyes are tired. He never decides the answer is probably twelve and writes it down without adding. He does the steps, all the steps, every time, in order, and he does them the same way the billionth time as the first time. Give him a program of four instructions and he is a toy. Give him a program of four billion instructions and he is your phone, drawing your photographs, checking your spelling, and guiding you down the highway, all of it fetch, decode, execute, all the way down. Same clerk. Same three steps. Just more of them, and faster than you can imagine. Which brings us to the word fast, and I do mean very fast.

How fast is the clerk? Let us build the answer honestly, in stages, because the final number is so large that it means nothing until you climb up to it.

A good human clerk, working carefully with paper, might complete one simple instruction every few seconds. Say one instruction in one second, to be generous, and to keep the arithmetic clean. One per second. Now, the processor in an ordinary modern phone or laptop completes billions of instructions in that same second. Not thousands. Not millions. Billions. To make that word into a number you can hold, the speed of a processor is measured by its clock. The clock is the heartbeat of the processor, a steady tick that keeps every step in time. Each tick of the clock is called a cycle, and in each cycle the clerk can complete a step of his work, sometimes a whole simple instruction, sometimes part of one. Clock speeds are measured in a unit called the hertz, which simply means times per second. One hertz is one tick per second. The processors in modern machines tick at around 3 gigahertz. Giga means billion, so 3 gigahertz means the clock ticks 3000000000 times every second. Three billion ticks. Every second. While you read this sentence aloud, the clerk in your phone has ticked more times than there are seconds in your whole life so far. I am not exaggerating for effect. That is the plain arithmetic of three billion ticks a second against the eighty or so years a long life contains.

Here is a comparison I use with every class, because it finally makes the speed felt. Imagine the clerk worked at human speed, one instruction per second, and we asked him to run a program of 3000000000 instructions, a program your phone handles in about a second. At one per second, that program would take 3000000000 seconds. There are about thirty million seconds in a year, so the human clerk would need roughly one hundred years to finish what your phone does while you blink. And the machine clerk does not need the hundred years, or the coffee, or the sleep. He needs the second. When people say computers are fast, this is the size of the word fast that they mean. The clerk is not cleverer than you. He is not clever at all. He is simply tireless at a speed that turns patience into power, because a simple job done three billion times a second stops being a simple job. It becomes a photograph appearing, a voice being recognised, a business being run.

Now the other word in the title, and in some ways the more important one. Literal. The clerk does exactly what the instruction says. Not what the instruction meant. Not what a sensible person would have understood. What it says. If the instruction contains a mistake, the clerk will carry out the mistake at full speed, three billion times a second, without pausing to wonder whether the result looks odd. He has no common sense, because common sense was never in his job description. His job description is fetch, decode, execute, and there is no fourth step called think about whether this is wise.

Let me give you the classic classroom example, and then it is your turn to try it, because this idea is learned by doing and not by watching. Suppose you ask a friend to make you a sandwich, and you give the instruction, put the peanut butter on the bread. Your friend, being a person, understands everything you did not say. Open the jar first. Use a knife. Spread it on one side. A reasonable amount. Now imagine giving that same instruction to our clerk, who understands nothing that is not written down. Put the peanut butter on the bread. He takes the sealed jar and sets it down on top of the loaf. Done. Instruction executed, exactly as written. You protest, and he is innocent, and he is right to be innocent, because the instruction was wrong, not the clerk. Every missing detail in an instruction is a place where the literal clerk will surprise you, and he will surprise you in the direction of the words you actually gave him.

This is your guided moment, so do it properly. Think of one small job from your own daily life, something you could explain to a person in one sentence. Making a cup of tea. Buttering toast. Locking up the house at night. Now rewrite that one sentence as instructions for the clerk, and keep asking yourself, at every single step, what have I assumed that I have not said? Where is the kettle in your instructions? Did you say to fill it? Did you say with water? Did you say to wait until it boils, and how would the clerk know it has boiled? Most people find, within five steps, that their simple job needs twenty instructions and still has gaps. That feeling, that slight dizziness at how much a person carries in their head without noticing, is the true beginning of understanding programs. Programs are what you get when someone does this work completely, closing every gap, for jobs thousands or millions of steps long. Lesson four is devoted to that, and you will be ready for it, because you will have felt in your own kitchen why it is hard.

There is a famous saying in computing that belongs here, and it is short enough to remember whole. Garbage in, garbage out. It means that if the instructions or the numbers given to the machine are wrong, the answers coming out will be wrong too, no matter how fast or expensive the machine is. The clerk does not launder bad work into good work. He amplifies whatever he is given, wisdom or folly, at three billion steps a second. When a computer produces a wrong bill, a wrong date, a wrong total for your business, the machine is almost never lying and almost never broken. Somewhere upstream, a human wrote an instruction that said the wrong thing, or fed it a wrong number, and the clerk, faithful as ever, executed it exactly. Blaming the clerk is like blaming a mirror for a stain on your shirt. The mirror's whole virtue is that it shows what is in front of it, and the clerk's whole virtue is the same.

Now, one more piece of the picture before we recap, because modern processors have a trick that the single-clerk picture does not show. One clerk, however fast, can only be in one place in the instruction row at a time. So modern chips put several clerks on the same desk. Each complete clerk inside a processor is called a core. A core is a full working processor inside the processor, able to fetch, decode, and execute its own stream of instructions. Many phones today have 8 cores. That means eight clerks, working side by side on different parts of the job at the same moment. One core draws your screen while another handles your music and a third watches for your messages arriving. When a machine is described as having more cores, it is not a faster clerk. It is more clerks. Speed of each worker and number of workers are two different kinds of power, and advertisements love to blur them. You will not blur them, because you have the picture. Ticks per second tell you how fast each clerk works. Cores tell you how many clerks there are.

And where do the instructions and numbers come from, at that speed? The clerk's desk, his registers, holds only a handful of numbers. Everything else, the programs and the data they work on, lives in memory and storage, the desk and the filing cabinet of lesson three. The clerk spends his life fetching from that memory, and how far away the memory sits is one of the great practical questions in all of computing. Hold that thought. It is literally the next lesson.

Let me also place the clerk honestly among his colleagues, because you will hear other chip names and I do not want them to blur together. The processor we have described, the general clerk, is good at following any instructions in order. There is a cousin chip that specialises in drawing pictures, doing the same small calculation on millions of pixels at once, and it matters greatly for games and for the AI work later in this path. Its name can wait. For today, the lesson is the clerk himself, the general worker, the one who runs the show, fetches every instruction, and answers for everything the machine does. In most machines, most of the time, if something happened, the clerk did it, following orders.

Before we recap, let me show you the clerk handling the one job that makes him more than a calculator, because it is the job lesson one's worked answer promised you. Conditional branching. Branching means the clerk does not always march to the very next instruction. Sometimes an instruction tells him, if a condition holds, jump ahead or jump back to a different part of the program. Picture the program counter again, the clerk's note of what comes next. A branch is an instruction that rewrites that note. Instead of moving to the next pigeonhole, the clerk crosses out his note and writes a new address, and his next fetch goes there instead.

Here is a small, complete example you can follow on your fingers. Suppose a program is counting down from three, the way a timer does. An instruction subtracts one from a number in a register. The next instruction says, if the register is not yet zero, jump back and subtract again. The next instruction after that says the counting is finished. Now run the clerk through it. The register starts at three. Subtract, and it holds two. Is it zero? No. Jump back. Subtract, and it holds one. Is it zero? No. Jump back. Subtract, and it holds zero. Is it zero? Yes. Do not jump. Move on to the finished instruction. That tiny loop, subtract and test and jump, is the seed of almost everything interesting a computer does. Searching a list is a loop. Drawing a photograph is loops inside loops, one for every row of pixels. Waiting for you to tap the screen is a loop that tests, and tests, and tests, until the test finally says yes. When the clerk jumps, he is not being clever. The instruction told him exactly when to jump and exactly where to land. But the effect of all that faithful jumping is a machine that can handle situations its programmer never counted by hand, because the counting, the testing, and the choosing were built into the instructions themselves.

Notice, too, what the branch is made of. The decision rests on a comparison, and a comparison is one of the clerk's ordinary jobs from his instruction set. Is this number bigger than that one? Are these two numbers equal? Is this register zero yet? The clerk answers with a simple result, in effect yes or no, one bit of truth, and the branch instruction spends that bit immediately. Lesson one's humble bit, the single choice between two values, turns out to steer the whole machine. Bits store the numbers. Bits store the instructions. And a single bit, freshly minted by a comparison, decides which road the program walks next. That is a satisfying piece of architecture, and it is worth pausing on, because nothing in this course is wasted. Everything you learn here returns later, wearing a bigger coat.

One honest warning grows straight out of the branch, and every programmer learns it the hard way. If the test in a loop can never come out the way the instruction expects, the clerk will loop forever. He will subtract, test, jump, subtract, test, jump, three billion times a second, patient as stone, waiting for a condition that the instructions themselves made impossible. The machine has not crashed in the sense of breaking. It is doing exactly what it was told, forever. When your phone freezes on one screen and will not answer, very often some clerk inside it is running a faithful, endless errand of exactly this kind. The cure people reach for, turning it off and on again, works because it wipes the clerk's note and makes him start from the very first instruction of a known program. Keep that in mind for your own machines. A frozen device is usually not a dead device. It is a literal clerk, trapped by literal instructions, waiting for someone to hand him a fresh start.

Two questions follow naturally, and a good student asks both, so let us take them before we close. The first is, if speed is so wonderful, why not simply make the clock tick faster and faster forever? Because the clerk works in the physical world, and the physical world charges for speed. Every tick moves electricity through tiny paths, and moving electricity makes heat. Tick twice as fast and you make roughly twice the heat in the same small space. A chip is smaller than your thumbnail, and at modern speeds it can produce more heat for its size than a stove element. That is why laptops have fans, why phones grow warm in your hand when they work hard, and why the clerk sometimes slows himself down on purpose when he gets hot, a behaviour called throttling. Your phone is not being lazy when it slows on a hot day. Its clerk is protecting himself from cooking. Speed in computing is always bought with heat and battery, and the art of the engineer is spending both wisely.

The second question is closer to your own work. If the clerk is this fast and this faithful, why do programs still feel slow, and why do computers still make mistakes with your business files and your messages? Because the clerk is rarely the slow part. He waits. He waits for memory to hand him the next instruction, waits for storage to deliver a photograph, waits for the network to bring a message from across the country. A clerk who can work in a billionth of a second, waiting on deliveries that take a thousand times longer, spends most of his life waiting, and lessons three and five are about those two waits. And the mistakes, as the sandwich taught us, are almost never his. They live in the instructions, written by people, in a hurry, with assumptions they never noticed they were making. Understanding the clerk teaches you where to look when a machine misbehaves. Not at the faithful worker. At the orders he was handed, and at the numbers he was fed. That habit of mind, looking upstream for the real cause, is worth more than any single fact in this lesson, and it will serve you in every lesson that follows and in a good deal of life outside this classroom as well.

Let us recap, in order. First, the processor, or CPU, is the part of the computer that follows instructions, one after another, exactly as they are written. Second, his whole life is a cycle of 3 steps, fetch, decode, and execute, repeated without pause. Third, fetch uses the program counter, the clerk's note of which instruction comes next. Fourth, decode reads the instruction as a number and matches its code against the instruction set, the fixed list of jobs he knows. Fifth, execute does the job, and the numbers being worked on sit in registers, the tiny, fastest storage on the clerk's own desk. Sixth, he is very fast. His clock ticks about 3000000000 times a second, at around 3 gigahertz, and that speed turns simple steps into enormous power. Seventh, he is very literal. He does what the instruction says, never what it meant, which is why garbage in means garbage out. And eighth, modern processors hold several clerks at once, each one called a core, and many phones have 8 cores working side by side.
