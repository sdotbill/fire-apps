# Test data: the exam the model has never seen

Welcome back for the last time in this class, and in, in, take the good seat. I am Professor Sana Rahal, and this is Machine Learning One, lesson six, Test data, the exam the model has never seen. Six lessons ago you could not have said what machine learning was. Today you stand with the whole machine in your hands. Examples in, rules out. Features on the left, label on the right. A loss that says wrong, this much. A walk downhill in the dark. And a disease, memorising instead of learning, that no training score can detect. This final lesson builds the institution that holds all of it honest. The examination. Test data is data set aside, untouched by training, used once at the end to grade the finished model on ground it has never walked. By the end of this lesson you will know how to split data into its working sets and what job each set does, why the final test must be touched only once, how to read a test score like a professional, including its uncertainty, and how the six lessons assemble into one complete, honest workflow you could run a real small business project through tomorrow. Then I will close the course, properly, because you have earned a proper closing.

Draw the final diagram with me. Picture your whole dataset as a pie, every labelled example you own. Before any training begins, before the model sees a single row, you slice the pie. The largest slice, say seventy percent of the pie, is the training set. That is the slice the model learns from, the examples whose labels drive the dials downhill. A second, smaller slice, say fifteen percent, is the validation set. Validation is a word meaning checking, and this slice is your practice exam. The model never trains on it, but you may grade on it repeatedly while you work, choosing features, choosing the learning rate, deciding when to stop, comparing one recipe against another. The last slice, the remaining fifteen percent, is the test set. The model never trains on it, and you do not grade on it during the work at all. It sits in a sealed envelope, in effect, until the model is completely finished and every decision has been made. Then, once, you open the envelope, grade the finished model on the test set, and that score is the honest estimate of how the model will perform on the real world it has not yet met. Training set for learning. Validation set for steering. Test set for the final, untouched judgment. Three slices, three jobs, and the discipline is in never letting the slices swap jobs. The moment test data steers a decision, it has become validation data, and you no longer own a final exam. You own another rehearsal and a false sense of security.

Let me put the slicing into the small numbers you can check in your head, using the marketing table from lesson two, which held two hundred labelled people. Seventy percent of two hundred is one hundred and forty. So the training set holds one hundred and forty people. Fifteen percent of two hundred is thirty. So the validation set holds thirty people, and the test set holds the remaining thirty people. One hundred and forty plus thirty plus thirty is two hundred. The whole pie, accounted for, no person in two slices, because a person appearing in both training and test would be a leak in the examination itself, the model graded partly on faces it had already memorised. Slicing is done at random, shuffled like a deck of cards, so each slice resembles the whole, the same rough mix of referrals and adverts, buyers and non-buyers, in each. And slicing is done once, at the start, before training, for a reason that should now feel obvious. If you slice after peeking at results, you will, even honestly, even unconsciously, choose slices that flatter. The envelope must be sealed before the work begins, or it was never sealed at all. Professionals write the split down, sometimes even lock it with a fixed random seed, a number that makes the shuffle reproducible, so that months later anyone can verify that the exam was the exam, untouched. That is what rigour looks like in practice. Not genius. Bookkeeping, done in the right order, defended like a contract.

Now the question every thoughtful student asks, and it deserves a full answer. Why is touching the test set more than once so corrupting? Surely grading twice cannot change the model. You are right that the dials do not move when you grade. What moves is you. Suppose you grade ten different models on the test set and ship the one with the highest test score. You have not trained on the test set, but you have selected on it, and selection is a quiet form of fitting. Among ten decent models, the highest test score belongs partly to skill and partly to luck, the luck of matching this particular test slice's quirks. Ship on that score and the real world will grade lower, because the luck does not ship with the model. Worse, suppose you grade, adjust a feature, grade again, adjust the learning rate, grade again, twenty rounds of it. Each adjustment that survives did so because it pleased the test set. Round by round, you have tuned the model to the exam, by hand, through the side door. You have become the memorising student, one decision at a time, while telling yourself you never trained on the test data. The rule protects you from yourself. Test once, at the end, and report what you get, whether it flatters or stings. If the sting sends you back to work, then the honest consequence follows. That test set is spent. For the next final judgment you need fresh test data, or you must demote the old test set to validation and carve a new sealed slice from new examples. Examinations, like in any school worth attending, cannot be retaken in secret until the grade improves. The grade is the grade. It goes on the record.

Let me work a complete grading in numbers, the way you will do it and the way you should demand it be done for you. Our finished marketing model faces its sealed test set, thirty people it has never seen. It predicts client or not client for each. It gets twenty-four right and six wrong. Twenty-four out of thirty is eighty percent. That eighty percent is the headline, and a professional immediately asks the next question. How much could luck move a score built on only thirty people? Quite a lot, and honesty requires saying so. With thirty test cases, each single person is worth about three percentage points, because one thirtieth is roughly three percent. Flip three people's outcomes by pure chance and the score swings by ten points. So the honest report is not eighty percent, full stop. It is eighty percent on a test of thirty, which means the true skill is probably somewhere in a band around that number, perhaps seventy to ninety, and more test data would narrow the band. Compare that with a test of three thousand people, where each person is worth a thirtieth of a percent and the band is narrow and the number can be leaned on. This is why test set size is a professional obsession and why small test sets produce big arguments. The score is an estimate, not a measurement from nature's own ledger. Report the size beside the score, always, at the same size, in the same sentence. Eighty percent, on thirty unseen cases. A vendor who reports the percentage and hides the count is hiding the band, and you now know to ask for it. How many cases was that graded on? That question, added to last lesson's question, what does it score on data it has never seen, completes your two-question audit of any machine learning claim on earth.

Our guided think-it moment today is a small courtroom, and you are the judge. Three claims are brought before you. Claim one, our model scored ninety-nine percent, graded on the data it was trained on, after training for a very long time with many features. Claim two, our model scored eighty-four percent on validation, and we then adjusted features eleven times, each time keeping the change only if the test score rose, finishing at ninety-one percent on test. Claim three, our model scored eighty percent on a sealed test set of thirty cases, touched once, after all decisions were made using validation. Rule on each claim, in your head, before I rule. Take your time, this is the whole course in one exercise. My rulings. Claim one is worthless as evidence, however impressive it sounds. Grading on training data cannot detect memorising, and a long training with many features is precisely the recipe for it. Claim two is the subtle fraud, self-deception division. The test set was used eleven times as a steering wheel, so the ninety-one percent is a validation score wearing a test costume, inflated by selection, and the true unseen performance is unknown and certainly lower. Claim three is honest evidence, modest and properly reported, score and size together, from an exam that could have failed and was allowed to speak once. Eighty, honestly earned, is worth more than ninety-nine in a costume, because eighty can be planned on, priced on, and promised on. If your rulings matched mine, you do not merely know this course. You can practise it, and more importantly, you can audit it, which in a world filling with claims is the rarer and more valuable skill.

Now let me assemble the whole course into one workflow, spoken as a single story, because this is the artifact I most want you to carry out of the university gates. A small business owner, let us take the tree service one last time, decides to predict job hours. Step one, define the label exactly. Hours from arrival on site to departure, including waiting on site, written down like a contract. Step two, choose features with the availability test and the leakage test. Height, condition, distance to the house, crane, power lines, each knowable at quote time, none containing the answer. Step three, collect and label honest examples, every job for a season, no cherry-picking the easy ones. Step four, slice the pie before training. Seventy percent to learn from, fifteen to steer by, fifteen sealed for the final exam, shuffled, written down. Step five, choose a loss that matches the business's real costs, squared error if big misses hurt disproportionately, weighted if underquoting hurts more than overquoting. Step six, train by gradient descent, batches and epochs, a sane learning rate, watching validation performance, stopping early when validation stops improving, before the dials start learning the noise. Step seven, compare recipes honestly on validation, simpler against fancier, and let the unseen-ish steering set, not the training score, pick the winner. Step eight, open the envelope once. Grade the frozen, finished model on the test set, report the score with its size, and decide, on that evidence, whether the tool is good enough to price real jobs with. Step nine, in real use, keep collecting new labelled examples, because the world drifts, trees, prices and customers change, and periodically the whole honest cycle runs again on fresher data with a fresher sealed exam. Nine steps. Every lesson you took is in there, in its place, doing its job. Examples in, rules out, but only inside a discipline that can tell learning from memorising and a score from a story. That discipline, not any single technique, is what this class actually taught you. Techniques will date. The discipline will not.

A last word on what to do when the honest exam returns a disappointing grade, because it will, in your work as in everyone's, and how a practitioner behaves in that moment is the true final examination of character in this field. You will be tempted to grade again on a different slice and hope. To adjust one small thing and re-open the envelope. To report the validation number instead, in larger type. Resist, in the specific way this course taught you. Report the grade at full size. Diagnose with the tools you own. Was the gap wide, suggesting memorising, or was performance uniformly weak, suggesting underfitting or starved features? Was the test set small, its band wide, its verdict genuinely uncertain? Was the label noisy, the features missing the real driver, the world itself drifted since the examples were collected? Each diagnosis has a remedy, more data, simpler model, better features, cleaner labels, and every remedy is worked on validation ground, with a new sealed exam prepared for the next final judgment. A disappointing honest grade is not a failure of the method. It is the method working, telling you the truth early, cheaply, in private, instead of letting your customers tell you later, expensively, in public. The exam the model has never seen is the kindest critic it will ever have. Treat its verdicts with gratitude and its envelope with respect, and it will keep you honest for an entire career.

Before the recap, two refinements that separate careful practitioners from merely enthusiastic ones, because you are ready for them now. The first is about how the slicing respects time. Shuffling works beautifully when examples are independent, like separate website visitors. But when examples arrive in time order and the future genuinely differs from the past, a shuffled test set can flatter you, because it lets the model be graded partly on the past it effectively grew up in. The careful alternative is to slice by time. Train on the older examples, test on the newest ones, so the exam mimics the real deployment, where the model always faces a future it has never seen. A tree service grading itself this way trains on spring and summer jobs and sits its exam on autumn jobs, with autumn weather, autumn light and autumn customers. If it passes that exam, it has passed something resembling the life it will actually live. Ask, of every test set you meet, not only was it unseen, but was it unseen in the way the future is unseen. That question is advanced, and you are now entitled to ask it. The second refinement concerns a technique called cross-validation, named here so the term never intimidates you. When data is scarce, one validation slice of thirty may be too small to steer by reliably, so practitioners rotate, training and validating several times with different slices held out in turn, and averaging the verdicts. It is the same discipline, the exam idea, repeated fairly, never a licence to peek at the final test set, which stays sealed through all of it. Rotation for steering, one sealed envelope for judgment. Hold those two apart and you can use the refinement without corrupting the institution it serves.

And a practical word about records, because examinations without records decay into folklore within a year. For every model you ship, write down, in one place, the label definition, the feature list with its availability reasoning, the split sizes and how they were made, the loss chosen and why, the validation scores of the recipes you compared, and the final test score with its size and its date. Six lines of bookkeeping, ten minutes of writing, and a year later you, or a colleague, or an auditor, can reconstruct exactly what the score meant and what ground it was earned on. Without those six lines, all that survives is a percentage, floating free of its meaning, ready to be quoted in ways its earners never intended. I have watched more damage done by unrecorded good scores than by bad models. The bad model gets fixed. The floating percentage gets believed, budgeted and promised, long after the world it described has drifted away. Your six lines are the anchor. Write them every time, especially when the score is good, especially when everyone is in a hurry, especially when nobody asks you to. That is what it means, in this trade, to be the adult in the room, and it is a role this course has been quietly training you for since the first diagram.

One more worked slice, quickly, in a second size of pie, so the percentages become reflexes rather than recipes. Suppose a hospital-adjacent project, no, let us stay with our own people, suppose the bakery has collected four hundred labelled mornings by now. Seventy percent of four hundred is two hundred and eighty mornings for training. Fifteen percent of four hundred is sixty mornings for validation, and sixty mornings sealed for test. Two hundred and eighty plus sixty plus sixty is four hundred, the whole pie again, and notice what the larger pie buys. A test set of sixty halves the luck-band of a test set of thirty, roughly speaking, because each single morning now weighs about one and two-thirds percent instead of about three. More data does not just train better models. It grades them more certainly, and certainty about a modest model is often worth more to a business than a brilliant model nobody can verify. When you advise an owner where to spend the next pound or dollar, remember that examples buy both learning and the ability to prove learning, a double return no fancier algorithm offers. Collect honestly, label exactly, slice before training, and the rest of this course follows almost by itself.

Let me also leave you the failure etiquette for the sealed envelope, spelled out as rules you can tape to a wall, because etiquette stated plainly survives pressure better than principles remembered fondly. Rule one, nobody opens the envelope early, not for a deadline, not for a sales meeting, not for a curious executive. Rule two, nobody grades twice and reports the better number. The first grade is the record, even if a second grading was run by accident, and the accident itself gets written down. Rule three, if the envelope is compromised, if test data leaked into training or steering in any way, you say so in the same sentence as any score you quote, or you do not quote a score at all. Rule four, the test set ages. A sealed exam carved from last year's customers grades last year's world, and as the world drifts the honest move is a new exam from new data, not louder quoting of the old one. Four rules, no exceptions for importance, because importance is exactly when exceptions get proposed. Institutions are not what we do when it is easy. Institutions are what still holds when someone senior is in a hurry. You are the person in the room who will know these rules. Some days that will make you unpopular for about ten minutes. Then the honest number will do its quiet work, and the room will be glad you were in it.

Recap, in order, for the last time in this class. First, before training, data is sliced into three sets with three jobs. The training set teaches the dials, the validation set steers the work, and the test set, sealed until the end, delivers the final judgment on ground the model has never walked. Second, in our worked split, two hundred examples became one hundred and forty for training, thirty for validation and thirty for test, shuffled, disjoint, and fixed before any training began. Third, the test set must be touched only once, because repeated grading turns selection into fitting, and the examiner into the memorising student, one kept adjustment at a time. Fourth, a test score is an estimate whose trustworthiness depends on the test size, so score and size are reported together, like eighty percent on thirty unseen cases, with the honest band that size implies. Fifth, claims are judged by their grading ground. Training scores prove nothing, repeatedly used test scores are validation in costume, and only a sealed, once-touched exam produces evidence you can plan on. Sixth, the whole course assembles into a nine-step workflow, from an exactly defined label to periodic honest re-examination as the world drifts, and that workflow, whole, is the real content of Machine Learning One.

Keep the sealed envelope sealed, keep the record written, keep the score and its size in the same sentence forever, and keep grading your own understanding on fresh ground as faithfully as you grade any model's, because the examiner's discipline, practised inward, is what turns a student of this subject into a practitioner other people can safely trust with real decisions, real money, and real customers' mornings.

That, and the habit of slicing before you train and sealing before you steer, is a complete professional inheritance from six lessons, and it cost you nothing but attention, honesty, and a few pages of arithmetic done in your own head at walking pace.

Carry the two audit questions out of here printed on your mind at full size. What does it score on data it has never seen, and how many cases was that graded on? Two questions, twenty words between them, and they will protect every budget, every promise and every customer you ever attach to a learning machine, for the whole of your working life, long after the details of any particular model have been forgotten by everyone, including, on some mornings, by me.
