# Features and labels: what the machine is shown

Welcome back. In, in, take your seat. I am Professor Sana Rahal, and this is Machine Learning One, lesson two, Features and labels, what the machine is shown. Last time we flipped programming on its head. Examples in, rules out. Today we open up one example and look inside it, because an example is not one solid lump. It has parts, and the parts have different jobs. Some parts are the clues the machine is shown. Some part is the answer we want it to learn to predict. If you mix those jobs up, learning fails before it starts, quietly and expensively. If you get them right, even a simple model can do honest, useful work. By the end of this lesson you will know what a feature is, what a label is, how to choose features with care, how to spot a cheating feature that secretly contains the answer, and how to turn messy real life, like words, categories and missing entries, into numbers a machine can actually use. We draw the diagram before we name the thing, as always, so let us draw.

Picture one example as a row in a table, like a row in a notebook or a spreadsheet. The row has several columns, and then one special column at the end, set apart. The columns before the special one are the features. A feature is one measurable clue about the case, one fact the machine is allowed to look at when it makes its guess. The special column at the end is the label. The label is the correct answer for that row, the thing we want the machine to learn to predict for new rows where we will not know it yet. That is the whole diagram. Features on the left, label on the right, one row per example, many rows making the training data. During training, the machine sees both sides, the features and the label, so it can compare its guess with the truth and nudge its dials. Later, in real use, the machine sees only the features of a new case, and it must produce the label on its own. That difference, seeing the answer during training and not seeing it in real use, is the single most important fact in this lesson, so say it with me in plain words. In training, the machine sees the features and the label. In real use, the machine sees only the features and must guess the label. Hold onto that. Almost every mistake in this field is a way of forgetting it.

Let us fill the table with a real example from a small business, slowly, row by row. Imagine you run a tree service. Homeowners call you for quotes, and you want to predict how long a job will take, so you can price it fairly and plan your crew's day. Each past job becomes one row. What could the features be? The height of the tree, in feet, is a feature. Whether the tree is dead or alive is a feature. How close the tree stands to the house, in feet, is a feature. Whether you need a crane, yes or no, is a feature. The label is how many hours the job actually took. Notice the shape of that. The features are things you can know before the job starts, when you stand in the yard and look up. The label is the thing you only know after the job is done. That timing is no accident, and it is a test you can apply to any feature anyone proposes. Can I know this before I need the prediction? If yes, it can be a feature. If I only know it afterwards, it cannot help me predict in real use, however interesting it is. A feature must be available at prediction time. That sentence deserves its place on the board. A feature must be available at prediction time. The hours taken fail that test for the next job, which is exactly why hours taken are the label and not a feature. We want to predict them precisely because we do not have them yet.

Now a tiny worked example with numbers small enough to follow in your head, because features earn their keep only when you see them do work. Suppose your notebook holds three past jobs. Job one, a tree thirty feet tall, alive, twenty feet from the house, no crane, and the job took two hours. Job two, a tree sixty feet tall, dead, ten feet from the house, crane needed, and the job took six hours. Job three, a tree forty feet tall, alive, fifteen feet from the house, no crane, and the job took three hours. Three rows, four features each, one label each. Now a new call comes in. A tree fifty feet tall, alive, twelve feet from the house, no crane. How long might it take? Your eye, and a simple model, would reason the same way. It is taller than jobs one and three, so longer than their two and three hours. It needs no crane and is alive, unlike job two, so shorter than six hours. A guess of about four hours sits sensibly in the middle. That guess used the features and nothing else. It did not peek at the answer, because for the new job there is no answer yet. And notice which feature did the heavy lifting. Height moved the guess most, crane need moved it a lot when present, and aliveness and distance refined it. In real models, features differ in how much they matter, and part of training is the machine discovering those amounts for itself, as dial settings, from many rows. With three rows it is a hunch. With three hundred rows it becomes a tool you can price with. The structure, though, is identical at both sizes. Features in view, label to predict, rows to learn from.

Features come in kinds, and the kind decides how the machine reads them, so let us name the kinds plainly. A numeric feature is a number on a scale, like height in feet, distance in feet, temperature, or price in dollars. Numbers are the machine's favourite food, because arithmetic works on them directly. A category feature is a choice from a fixed list, like alive or dead, sunny or rainy, referral or advert or walk-in. Categories are not numbers, and treating them as if they were can mislead. If you code sunny as one, rainy as two and cloudy as three, the machine may think cloudy is three times sunny, which is nonsense that you accidentally taught it. The honest fix, which you will meet properly in later courses, is to give each category its own yes-or-no column. Sunny, yes or no. Rainy, yes or no. Cloudy, yes or no. Three honest columns instead of one dishonest number. A text feature is words, like the message a customer wrote on your contact form. Machines cannot eat raw words, so text gets counted and weighed first, how many times certain telling words appear, for example, before it can join the table. And a yes-or-no feature, like crane needed, is the simplest of all, one for yes and zero for no. You do not need to master the machinery of any of this today. You need the instinct. Every feature must end up as honest numbers that mean what they claim to mean, or the patterns the machine finds will be patterns in your coding mistakes, not in the world.

Now the shadow side, and I will spend real time here because this is where beginners, and frankly some professionals, lose the most money. The first shadow is the cheating feature, known in the field as leakage, a word worth learning properly. Leakage means information from the label, or from after the moment of prediction, has leaked into the features. The model then looks brilliant in training and collapses in real use, because in real use that leaked information will not be there. Here is a clean example. Suppose you want to predict, when a quote is requested, whether the homeowner will accept your price. One of your columns is days until the deposit was paid. For customers who accepted, that column holds a small number. For customers who refused, it is blank forever. A model trained with that column will learn the rule, if deposit days exist, the answer is yes, and it will score nearly perfectly on your old rows. It has learned nothing about customers. It has learned to read the answer off the back of the envelope. On a new quote, where nobody has paid anything yet, the column is always blank, and the model is helpless. The tell-tale sign of leakage is a model that looks too good. If your first model is right ninety-nine times out of one hundred on a fuzzy human question, do not celebrate. Suspect leakage, and go hunting for the feature that already knows the answer. Perfect scores on fuzzy problems are not a triumph. They are an alarm bell. Write that down exactly as I said it. Perfect scores on fuzzy problems are an alarm bell.

The second shadow is the missing feature, the clue you never gave the machine. A model can only use the features in its table. If the real driver of the label is not among them, no amount of training will invent it. Imagine predicting which website visitors become clients, using only the colour scheme of the page they landed on and the hour of the day, while never recording how they found you or whether they opened your follow-up email. The model will squeeze those two weak features dry and still guess poorly, and the poor result will not mean machine learning failed. It will mean the table was starved. The fix is not a fancier model. The fix is better features, chosen by someone who understands the real business. This is why the person who knows the work matters so much in machine learning. Choosing features is where human knowledge enters the table. A tree surgeon knows that lean, rot and power lines matter before any data scientist does. A marketer knows that referral source matters. The machine finds the pattern, but you choose what it is allowed to look at, and that choice is a skilled, human, billable act. Never let anyone tell you the machine does it all. The table is yours. The features are your expertise, written down in columns.

The third shadow is quieter. A feature can be present, honest, available in time, and still be unfair or unlawful to use, and a professional has to catch that before training, not after a complaint. If your table includes a feature that stands in for a protected characteristic of a person, the model's rules may quietly discriminate even if you never intended them to. The lesson for today is simple and firm. Features are choices, and choices carry responsibility. Ask of every column not only does it help, and is it available in time, but also should it be used at all. That question belongs to the human at the table, and no dial setting can answer it for you. I raise it now, early in your education, so it becomes a habit while your habits are forming, not a patch you bolt on later.

Let me give you a second worked example, from marketing this time, with the label as a yes-or-no answer, because labels are not always numbers. A number label, like hours or jars sold, is called a regression label in the field, from an old statistical word, and a yes-or-no or category label, like became a client or did not, is called a classification label, because the model is sorting cases into classes. You do not need to love those terms, but you will hear them, so now they are unpacked. Your form records two hundred people. For each person, the features are these. How they found you, referral, advert or search. The size of their business, as a number of staff. Whether they opened the follow-up email, yes or no. The label is whether they became a paying client within a month, yes or no. Let us count together, tiny numbers again. Of the two hundred people, fifty came by referral, and thirty of those fifty became clients. Thirty out of fifty is sixty percent, a strong rate. One hundred came by advert, and only ten of those became clients. Ten out of one hundred is ten percent. Already, with one feature alone, the table is speaking. Referral predicts far better than advert. Now add the email feature inside the referral group. Of the fifty referrals, forty opened the email, and twenty-eight of those forty became clients. Twenty-eight out of forty is seventy percent. The ten referrals who never opened it produced only two clients, twenty percent. Each honest feature sharpens the picture a little more. That sharpening, done systematically over all the features at once, is exactly what training automates. You are not learning a new kind of thinking today. You are learning to see the thinking you already do with counts and rates, laid out as a table the machine can work on at scale.

Time for our guided think-it moment. Take the tree service table in your mind and test three proposed new columns. Column A, the customer's star rating of your company after the job. Column B, whether power lines run through the tree's branches, noted at quote time. Column C, the final invoice total. For each column, ask the two questions from this lesson. Can I know it before I need the prediction? And does it secretly contain the answer? Take a breath and judge them one by one. Here are my judgments. Column A fails. A rating given after the job is not available when you price the job, and worse, a happy rating leaks the fact that the job went well. Column B passes cleanly. Power lines are visible at quote time, they genuinely make work slower, and they contain no hint of the label. That is a fine feature, and a tree surgeon's knowledge put it there, not a computer's. Column C fails twice over. The invoice is written after the job, and since you price by hours, the invoice is practically the label wearing a coat. Using it would be leakage in its purest form. If you judged B as the only keeper, you have the instinct this lesson exists to build. Guard it. It will save you from the most common and most embarrassing failure in applied machine learning.

One more practical piece before we recap, and it is about scale, a small thing that bites hard. Features live on different scales. Height in feet runs from ten to one hundred. Crane needed runs from zero to one. Distance in feet runs from five to fifty. If you feed those raw numbers to some models, the big-numbered feature shouts and the small-numbered feature whispers, even when the whisper matters more. The common fix is to rescale features so they sit on comparable footing, for example squeezing each one into a range from zero to one. You will do this properly in later work. Today, just file the idea. Numbers that look bigger are not automatically more important, and honest modelling makes sure the machine is not fooled by the size of the units somebody happened to measure in. Feet versus metres should never change what a model concludes about a tree, and rescaling is how we keep that promise.

Let me walk one complete table past you from a different trade, so the structure proves it is not about trees. Imagine a small bakery deciding how many loaves to bake each morning, the same bakery shape you met last lesson, now with its table drawn properly. Each past morning is a row. The features are the day of the week, whether school is in, whether roadworks are active on the main road, and the forecast, sunny or rainy. Four features, two of them categories, one of them yes-or-no, one of them a day name. The label is the number of loaves sold by ten o'clock. Now say the sentence that must always be sayable. In training, the baker's notebook shows the machine the features and the label together, sixty mornings of them. In real use, tomorrow morning, the baker knows the day, the school calendar, the roadworks and the forecast, all features, all available before the ovens are lit, and the machine must guess the label, loaves sold, which nobody knows until ten o'clock arrives. If a proposed fifth feature appears, say the length of the queue at nine o'clock, you already know how to judge it. It sounds predictive, and it is, but at baking time, hours before nine, it does not exist yet. It fails the availability test, and including it would train a model the baker can never actually use at the moment of decision. Timing is not a detail in this work. Timing is the difference between a model that helps at the moment of decision and a model that only explains yesterday beautifully. Businesses do not need beautiful explanations of yesterday nearly as much as they need decent guesses before today is spent.

While we are here, let me settle a confusion about labels that I hear every year, because clearing it now saves real pain. The label must be defined exactly, in words a stranger could apply the same way you do. Became a client sounds clear until you ask, within what time, a week, a month, a year, and paid how much, anything at all, or a full package? Job took long sounds clear until you ask whether travel and waiting for a crane count in the hours. Every vague label teaches the machine a vague target, and the machine will hit that vague target with great precision and no usefulness. Professionals write the label definition down before training starts, in one sentence, and they defend that sentence like a contract. Ours for the tree service was, hours from arrival on site to departure, including waiting time on site. Ours for the marketing table was, paid at least one full invoice within thirty days of the form. Notice how each definition settles arguments before they start. If two people labelling the same cases would often disagree, the label is not ready, and no model built on it can be trusted, however impressive its score looks. Clean labels are quiet, unglamorous, decisive work. They are also, hour for hour, some of the highest-value work in the entire field, because every dial downstream learns from them.

And one word about having too many features, since beginners often assume more columns must mean more learning. More features can help when each one adds a genuinely new clue. But every added column is also another chance for leakage to sneak in, another column to collect reliably forever, and another way for the model to find a fluke pattern in a small table that will not repeat in the world. With two hundred rows and three hundred features, a model can practically memorise, a sin we will dissect properly in lesson five under its name, overfitting. The craft is not collecting the most features. The craft is choosing a lean set of honest, available, meaningful features and defending each one's place in the table. If you cannot say, in one plain sentence, why a feature might genuinely relate to the label, that feature is a guest you have not vetted. Vetting guests is the job. A short table you understand beats a wide table you merely hope in, every single time, in every trade I have ever seen this applied to.

Let me do one more slow count together, because reading a table fluently is a skill and skills grow by doing. Back to the tree service, with a fresh little notebook of four jobs where the only feature we will track is whether a crane was needed, and the label is hours taken. Job one, no crane, two hours. Job two, no crane, three hours. Job three, crane needed, six hours. Job four, crane needed, seven hours. Four rows. Now squeeze the table the way your eye already wants to. The no-crane jobs average two and a half hours, because two and three average to two and a half. The crane jobs average six and a half hours, because six and seven average to six and a half. The gap between the groups is four hours. That gap is the feature earning its keep in plain sight. If a fifth job arrives needing a crane, your table-trained guess starts near six and a half hours, then refines with height and distance when those columns join. If a job arrives with no crane, you start near two and a half. One honest feature has already sorted the future into two sensible piles. That is all a model ever does, however large it grows. It sorts the future into sensible piles using the features, weighted by what the labelled past says each feature is worth. When someone shows you a model you cannot interrogate, bring it back to this. Show me the table. Show me the features. Show me the label definition. Show me a count like the one we just did, where one feature visibly moves the label. If the table cannot be shown, the magic is probably bookkeeping. You now own the questions that separate the two, and you learned them from four tree jobs and a bakery. That is how foundations are supposed to work.

One final habit to install before the recap, and then we really will recap. Whenever you meet a new dataset, read it column by column and say each column's job out loud. Feature, feature, feature, label. Then read it row by row and ask whether the row is honest, complete enough to learn from, and labelled by the definition you wrote down. Column by column for jobs, row by row for honesty. That two-direction reading takes ten minutes on a small table and catches leakage, starved features, vague labels and miscoded categories while they are still cheap to fix. Every expensive failure I have watched in this field was visible in that ten-minute reading to anyone willing to do it slowly. Slow reading is not the opposite of progress here. Slow reading is what makes the fast machinery safe to trust.

Recap, in order, as always. First, every example is a row, features on the left, label on the right. Features are the clues the machine is shown, and the label is the correct answer it must learn to predict. Second, in training the machine sees features and label together, but in real use it sees only the features, so every feature must be available at prediction time. Third, features come in kinds, numeric, category, text, and yes-or-no, and each kind must be turned into honest numbers that mean what they claim to mean. Fourth, leakage is the cheating shadow, answer information hiding in the features, and its warning sign is a score that looks too perfect on a fuzzy problem. Fifth, missing features starve the table, and choosing features is where human expertise enters, which makes it skilled and responsible work. Sixth, number labels and category labels lead to the two great families of task, predicting an amount and sorting into classes, and you have now seen honest counts for both.

Carry one sentence out of the room with you and the lesson has done its work even if details fade. The machine can only learn from what it is shown, so showing it well, honest features on the left and an exactly defined label on the right, is not preparation for the real work. It is the real work, and everything clever that follows stands on it.

That closes lesson two. You can now look at any proposed machine learning project and ask the questions that matter. What is the label, exactly? What are the features, exactly? Can each feature be known in time, and does any of them already know the answer? Those questions will expose most bad projects in five minutes flat.
