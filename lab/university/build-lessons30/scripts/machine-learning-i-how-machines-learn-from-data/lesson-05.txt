# Overfitting: memorising instead of learning

Welcome back. In, in, come in and close the door gently, because today's lesson is about a quiet failure, the kind that arrives dressed as success. I am Professor Sana Rahal, and this is Machine Learning One, lesson five, Overfitting, memorising instead of learning. Overfitting is the most important word in this lesson, so let me unpack it before anything else. To fit means to match the examples. To overfit means to match them too closely, capturing their accidents and noise as if they were the pattern, until the model knows the training examples by heart and understands nothing beyond them. For four lessons we have built an engine that drives loss down. Guess, compare, nudge, repeat, downhill all the way. Today we ask the dangerous question. Can the loss go down for the wrong reason? Can a model score beautifully on everything it was trained on and still be useless, even harmful, on the next real case that walks through the door? The answer is yes, it happens constantly, it has a name, and by the end of this lesson you will be able to spot it, explain it with a story about a student and an exam, measure the gap that reveals it with small numbers, and name the honest cures. This lesson and the next one are a pair. Today, the disease. Next time, the examination system that keeps it honest.

Let me give you the class's set problem first, worked in full, exactly as this class teaches it, because it carries the whole lesson in three sentences. The problem is this. Explain overfitting to a friend using a student and an exam. Two or three sentences, no jargon you cannot unpack. Here is the worked answer. Overfitting is the student who memorised last year's exam answers instead of learning the subject: perfect on the questions already seen, helpless on new ones. The cure is the same in both cases — hold back questions the learner has never seen, and grade on those. Sit with that student for a moment, because you have met him. He worked hard, in a way. He learned that question four's answer is B, that the essay question wants the phrase about supply and demand in the second paragraph, that the long calculation ends in forty-two. Ask him last year's paper and he is flawless. Change one number in the calculation, ask the idea from a new angle, and he collapses, because nothing underneath the memorised answers was ever built. He optimised the wrong target. He minimised his loss on the seen questions, perfectly, and learned the subject not at all. A model can do precisely this, with dials instead of flashcards, and it can do it at a scale and speed that makes the failure hard to notice until real customers meet it. Memorising is not learning. That sentence is the lesson. Everything else is evidence and remedy.

Now let me show you memorising happening to numbers, slowly, with a table small enough to hold in your head. Imagine we track jam sales against temperature, and our notebook has three Saturdays. At ten degrees we sold ten jars. At twenty degrees we sold twenty jars. At thirty degrees we sold thirty jars. A simple pattern fairly shouts from those rows. Sales equal temperature, roughly, ten at ten, twenty at twenty. A simple model that learns sales are about equal to temperature will guess fifteen jars at fifteen degrees and twenty-five at twenty-five, and those guesses will be good, because the pattern is real. Now imagine a second model, a complicated one with many dials, that refuses the simple pattern and instead memorises the three rows exactly, weaving an intricate rule that passes through all three points perfectly and also wiggles wildly between them. Asked about ten, twenty or thirty degrees, it answers perfectly, loss zero, a triumph on the training data. Asked about fifteen degrees, a temperature it never saw, its wiggly rule might say four jars, or forty, because between the memorised points its behaviour is arbitrary, governed by the wiggles, not by any pattern. Zero loss. Useless model. Both facts at once. That is overfitting in its purest form, and notice what caused it. Too much flexibility, too many dials, chasing too little data too exactly. The model had enough freedom to memorise, and gradient descent, doing its honest job, used that freedom to drive the loss to zero by the shortest route available, which was memorisation. The walk downhill does not care whether the valley it finds is wisdom or a photograph of the training set. Caring is our job, and the tools for caring are coming in this lesson and the next.

Here is the same disease in a small business body, because you will meet it wearing overalls more often than wearing a gown. A tree service builds a model to predict job hours from its last forty jobs. Forty rows is a small table. The model is given thirty features, everything anyone could think of, including the customer's street name, the day of the month, and the colour of the crew's truck that day. With that much freedom and that little data, the model finds a rule that predicts all forty past jobs almost perfectly. It has learned, in effect, that jobs on Maple Street on Tuesdays with the blue truck take five hours, because in forty jobs that combination happened twice and both took five hours by coincidence. Next week brings a Maple Street job on a Wednesday with the white truck, a combination never seen, and the model's guess is nonsense, because its knowledge is a scrapbook, not a trade. The owner, who was shown a near-perfect score during training, prices the job from that guess and loses money, and concludes that machine learning is a fraud. It is not a fraud. It is a mirror. It showed him a memoriser and he mistook it for a learner, because nobody taught him the test that tells them apart. You are being taught that test today, and it costs nothing, and it is this. Never judge a model only on the examples it trained on. Memorising aces those by definition. Judge it on examples it has never seen. The gap between those two judgments is the disease made visible, and we will put numbers on the gap in a moment.

First, let me name the opposite failure too, because the cure for one disease is often an overdose that causes the other, and professionals steer between them. Underfitting is the failure of learning too little. An underfit model is too simple, or trained too briefly, to capture even the real pattern. It is the student who read one page and guesses vaguely at everything, old questions and new alike. If our jam model ignored temperature entirely and always guessed twenty jars, its loss would be mediocre on the training rows and mediocre on new rows, no gap, just uniform weakness. So picture a spectrum. On the left, underfitting, too simple, high loss everywhere, the pattern missed. In the middle, good fitting, the pattern captured, loss low on training rows and nearly as low on new rows. On the right, overfitting, too flexible or trained too long on too little data, loss near zero on training rows and poor on new rows, the examples memorised. The middle is not found by luck. It is found by controlling flexibility, by having enough honest data, and above all by measuring on unseen examples while you steer, so you can watch the gap open or close with your own eyes. A model is a student. You are the teacher. Teachers who only ever re-ask the practice questions cannot tell a learner from a parrot, and neither can you, until you hold some questions back.

Let us put the gap into numbers now, tiny ones, calculated in the open, because a gap you can calculate is a gap you can manage. Suppose we train a model to predict whether website visitors become clients, and we measure its accuracy, the share of guesses it gets right. On the two hundred training examples, the ones it learned from, it gets one hundred and ninety-six right. One hundred and ninety-six out of two hundred is ninety-eight percent. Beautiful. Now we test it on one hundred fresh visitors it never saw during training, people who filled the form after training ended. It gets sixty-one right. Sixty-one out of one hundred is sixty-one percent. Barely better than a coin flip dressed in statistics. The gap is ninety-eight minus sixty-one, which is thirty-seven percentage points. That gap is the overfitting, measured. A healthy model might score ninety percent in training and eighty-six on fresh cases, a gap of four, the small honest cost of facing the new. A gap of thirty-seven is a confession. The model memorised the two hundred, their quirks, their coincidences, the one referral who happened to buy on a rainy Tuesday, and it carried almost none of that profitably into the future. Memorise those benchmark numbers as a story, not as law. Ninety-eight in training, sixty-one unseen, gap thirty-seven, disease. Ninety in training, eighty-six unseen, gap four, health. When a vendor shows you only the first number of either pair, you now know exactly which question restores the truth. What does it score on data it has never seen? Ask it every time. Ask it in writing.

Our guided think-it moment today asks you to play teacher to three students, which is to say, three models, and diagnose each from its two scores. Student A scores one hundred percent on the practice paper and forty percent on a fresh paper, worse than guessing on some questions. Student B scores seventy percent on practice and sixty-eight percent on fresh. Student C scores forty-five percent on practice and forty-four percent on fresh. Take a breath and name each condition before I do. Here are my diagnoses. Student A is the pure memoriser, severely overfit, perfect on the seen, helpless on the new, the worked example from the start of this lesson wearing a number. Student B is the healthy learner, a small gap of two points, real knowledge that travels. Student C is underfit, no gap worth mentioning, but weak everywhere, the pattern never captured in the first place, needing more learning, more capacity or better features, not less. Notice that B, the best student, has neither the highest practice score nor any score near perfection. Health in this field rarely looks like perfection. It looks like consistency between the seen and the unseen. If you diagnosed all three, you can now read the most important pair of numbers in applied machine learning, and you will never again be dazzled by a lone training score, which makes this one of the most valuable lessons in the course, pound for pound.

Now the cures, honestly stated, with their limits, because a lesson that names a disease without its treatments is just anxiety with a syllabus. Cure one, more data. Memorising works when examples are few and flexibility is vast. Add honest examples and memorisation gets harder while real patterns get clearer, because the coincidences stop repeating while the truth keeps repeating. Doubling a small dataset often does more for a model than any cleverness applied to the dials. Cure two, less flexibility. Use a simpler model, fewer dials, fewer exotic features, when data is scarce. A simple model cannot memorise much. It is forced to find the broad pattern or fail openly, and open failure is far safer than disguised failure. Cure three, stop training earlier. Overfitting often grows with time, the model first learns the pattern, then, with epochs to spare, starts learning the noise. Watching unseen-example performance during training and stopping when it stops improving, a practice called early stopping, catches the model at its wisest moment. Cure four, the family of techniques that deliberately restrain the dials during training, keeping them small and smooth so wild wiggles cost extra loss, has a name you will meet later, and its spirit is already yours. Penalise wiggliness. Reward smoothness. Prefer the simpler explanation when two explanations fit the seen data equally. That preference has an old philosophical name, Occam's razor, the idea that the simpler account should be chosen, and in machine learning it is not philosophy at all. It is survival equipment. And cure zero, underneath all the others, the discipline that makes the rest verifiable. Hold back examples the model never trains on, and grade on those. That is lesson six, entire, and it is the cure the worked answer named. Hold back questions the learner has never seen, and grade on those. Everything else is assistance. That one is the judge.

One more angle, the human one, because this disease is not really about machines. Memorising instead of learning is the most human failure in education, in business and in life. The student crams last year's paper. The business copies last year's campaign without asking what changed. The professional recites the certification answers and freezes on the first real client whose problem is shaped slightly differently. Machine learning did not invent this failure. It inherited it from us, automated it, and, in doing so, gave us a gift. It made the failure measurable. A human memoriser can bluff a conversation. A model cannot bluff a held-back test set. Its gap prints as a number, thirty-seven points wide, undeniable. There is something almost moral in that honesty, and it is why I love teaching this lesson. The machine, judged properly, cannot pretend to have learned. It can only show you, on unseen ground, exactly what it actually has. Demand the same standard of every claim made in this field, including your own future claims, especially your own future claims, and you will be rarer and more valuable than any particular technique can make you.

Before the recap, one caution about noise, because it completes the theory of why memorising is even possible. Real data contains noise, meaning the random wobble of the world that no pattern explains. One sunny Saturday, a festival in the next town empties your stall. One referral client was always going to buy because his brother-in-law insisted. Those wobbles live in the labels. A flexible enough model, trained long enough, will bend its dials to explain the wobbles too, inventing rules from festivals and brothers-in-law that will never recur. That bending is the mechanism of overfitting at the deepest level. The model cannot tell pattern from noise by staring at the training set alone, because in the training set both look identical, both are just numbers to fit. Only unseen data can separate them, because patterns repeat in the unseen data and noise, by definition, does not. There it is, the whole lesson in one logical chain. Noise is indistinguishable from pattern on seen data. Flexible models fit both. Therefore seen-data scores cannot detect overfitting. Therefore grading must happen on unseen data. Four steps, no gaps, and you can now reproduce that chain for any sceptic, which is what understanding actually means. Not nodding at the story. Rebuilding the argument from its foundations, alone, at midnight, for a friend who doubts it. You are ready to do that. Let us recap and then close.

Let me work one more gap calculation in a different trade, so the skill transfers and is not welded to websites. A bakery model predicts loaves sold each morning. Measured as average miss, its error on the sixty mornings it trained on is two loaves. Lovely, bake by it tomorrow. Measured on the next thirty mornings, mornings it never saw, its average miss is eleven loaves. The gap is nine loaves, eleven minus two. In relative terms the unseen error is more than five times the training error. Diagnosis before remedy, always. A gap that wide, on a model with many features fitted to only sixty rows, reads as memorisation until proven innocent. The remedies in order of honesty and cheapness. First, hold the model's score in public to the eleven, never the two, because eleven is what the future will actually experience. Second, simplify, drop the exotic features, the street festival flag that fired once, the day-of-month column, until the model has only the clues a baker herself believes in. Third, gather more mornings, because a hundred and eighty rows will discipline dials that sixty rows let run wild. Fourth, re-measure the gap after each change, on fresh unseen mornings, and keep only changes that narrow it. Notice what this workflow is. It is the scientific habit wearing an apron. Claim, test on new ground, revise, retest. Overfitting is what happens when the second step is skipped and the claim is graded on the ground it was built from. Every field that skips that step, from bakeries to banks, eventually bakes eleven loaves wrong while reporting two, and calls the difference bad luck. It is not bad luck. It is a skipped examination, and now you know its name and its arithmetic.

And let me answer the sharp question a good student always asks at this point, because if it is forming in your mind, ask it proudly. If unseen data is the judge, why not train on all the data at the very end, once the judging is done, to squeeze the most from every example? The honest answer is, sometimes professionals do exactly that, retraining the final chosen recipe on the full dataset before shipping, and it is legitimate precisely because the judging already happened and the recipe was chosen by unseen scores. The sin is not training on lots of data. The sin is choosing, tuning and boasting based on scores from data the model trained on, and calling that evidence. Order is everything. Judge first, on unseen ground, with the recipe frozen. Then, if you retrain on everything, you ship in the knowledge that your evidence came from the honest examination, not from the rehearsal. Keep that ordering bright and you can use advanced practices safely. Blur it, and even advanced practices become sophisticated self-deception. The difference between a science and a ritual is mostly this, whether the test could have failed. A test on training data cannot fail a memoriser. It is a ritual. A test on unseen data can fail anyone. It is a science. Choose science, in this field and, if I may say so as your teacher, in most of the others you will ever practise.

One last story to carry the feeling, not just the facts. Two stalls stand side by side at a market. Both keep notebooks. The first stallholder studies his notebook until he can recite it, every Saturday, every number, and feels magnificently prepared. The second stallholder studies hers differently. She covers the last column with her hand and guesses it, row after row, checking herself only after guessing, practising on answers she has hidden from herself even though she wrote them. On the first rainy Saturday of the new season, a day neither notebook contains, the second stallholder packs sensibly and sells well. The first packs for a memorised Saturday that is not this one, and takes most of it home. Same notebook. Same effort, honestly counted. Different relationship to the unseen. One treated the past as scripture to recite. The other treated it as practice for a future that would not repeat it exactly. Machine learning, done right, is the second stallholder's discipline, mechanised. Done wrong, it is the first stallholder with a faster memory and a bigger notebook. Your job, in every project you ever touch, is to be the second stallholder's advocate in the room, asking the unfashionable question while everyone admires the recitation. What did it score on the Saturday it had never seen?

A word about how overfitting hides in language, so you can hear it in meetings before you ever see it in numbers. Listen for the phrase, the model achieves ninety-nine percent accuracy, with no second number following it. Listen for training results presented with the word results, unqualified, as if the rehearsal were the performance. Listen for a demonstration performed entirely on the examples in the brochure, the same houses, the same emails, the same customers the system was built from. Each of those is a memoriser's tell, the equivalent of a student offering to be examined only on last year's paper, only on the questions he has already recited to you twice. And listen, in yourself, for the warm feeling that a perfect score brings, and learn to distrust that warmth professionally. In this field, a perfect score on seen data is not the summit. It is the trailhead of suspicion. The summit is a good score, honestly a little lower, earned on ground the model had never walked, reported with its gap printed beside it at the same size. Train your ear for the missing second number and you will hear overfitting coming down the corridor before it enters the room, which is, in the end, what expertise mostly is. Hearing what is absent, and refusing to be comforted by what is present, until the absent thing is supplied.

Recap, in order. First, overfitting is memorising instead of learning. The model matches the training examples too closely, capturing noise and coincidence as if they were pattern, and fails on new cases. Second, the worked image is the student who memorised last year's exam answers, perfect on the questions already seen, helpless on new ones. Third, the revealing measure is the gap between performance on training examples and performance on unseen examples, and in our numbers a gap of thirty-seven points confessed the disease while a gap of four showed health. Fourth, the spectrum runs underfitting, good fitting, overfitting, too little learning, the pattern captured, the examples memorised, and health looks like consistency, not perfection. Fifth, the cures are more data, less flexibility, earlier stopping, restraining the dials toward smoothness, and beneath them all, grading on held-back examples the learner never saw. Sixth, noise is why it happens. Pattern and noise look identical on seen data, and only unseen data can tell them apart, because patterns repeat and noise does not.

One more repetition of the central pair, because pairs are remembered where singles fade. Seen, flattering. Unseen, honest. Seen, rehearsal. Unseen, performance. Seen, the notebook recited. Unseen, the rainy Saturday survived.

Grade yourself the way this lesson teaches, as we finish. Do not ask whether today's ideas feel familiar, because familiarity is the training score of the mind, flattering and cheap. Ask whether you could rebuild them tomorrow, on a fresh example, in a trade you have never worked in, for a listener who doubts you. That is your unseen test, and by the evidence of the thinking you did in the guided moment, you are closer to passing it than you were an hour ago.

You have now stood where every honest practitioner stands, between admiration for what the dials can do and suspicion of what the scores claim, and that balanced stance, admiration with suspicion, evidence with humility, is not a mood. It is a method, and methods can be practised until they become character, which is the quiet ambition underneath this entire course.

And if you remember the term early stopping from the cures, keep it warm for the next lesson, because the held-back set that tells you when to stop is the same set that will grow up into the full examination system of lesson six, with its own name, its own rules, and its own inviolable etiquette about being touched once and only once at the end.

Take the student story with you and use it kindly but firmly wherever this field is discussed. It fits in two sentences, it needs no jargon, and it has ended more bad pitches than any equation I know. A model, like a student, is only ever as good as its performance on questions it has never seen, and anyone unwilling to be graded that way is telling you, in advance, exactly how they learned.
