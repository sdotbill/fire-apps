# Gradient descent: walking downhill in the dark

Welcome back. In, in, settle in. I am Professor Sana Rahal, and this is Machine Learning One, lesson four, Gradient descent, walking downhill in the dark. Last lesson we built the funnel. A guess and a truth go in, and a loss comes out, one number that says wrong, this much. Today we answer the question that funnel leaves hanging. The machine knows how wrong it is. How does it know which way to move? A real model may have thousands, millions, even billions of dials. Twisting them at random until the loss falls would take longer than the age of the universe. Yet training works, and it works by a method so elegant that once you see it, you will recognise it everywhere for the rest of your life. That method is called gradient descent. Gradient is a word we will unpack until it is friendly, and descent just means going down. By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain, in plain words and with small numbers, how feeling the slope under your feet lets you walk downhill in total darkness, what the learning rate is and why it can ruin everything at both extremes, why training takes many small steps instead of one giant leap, and what it means when the walk gets stuck in a dip that is not the bottom. This is the cleverest lesson in the course. Take it slowly. Stuck is a place, not a person, and today we walk out of the dark one step at a time, which is, not coincidentally, exactly the method itself.

Draw the diagram with me first, as always. Picture a hill in cross-section, like a slice through a landscape, drawn as a curving line on the board. The horizontal direction is the setting of one dial. Move right, the dial increases. Move left, it decreases. The vertical direction is the loss. High up means large loss, badly wrong. Low down means small loss, nearly right. The curve dips and rises because as you turn the dial, the loss changes. Somewhere on that curve is a lowest point, the valley floor, where this dial is set as well as it can be. Now picture yourself standing on that hillside at night, in total darkness, with the dial as your position. You cannot see the valley. You cannot see the curve. All you can do is feel, through your feet, which way the ground slopes where you stand, and how steeply. Gradient descent is the simple, disciplined walk that follows from that situation. Feel the slope. Take a step downhill. Feel the slope again. Take another step downhill. Repeat until the ground under your feet is flat, because flat, at the bottom, means the loss has stopped falling, and you have arrived. That is the whole method. Everything else in this lesson is the careful working-out of feel, step, and repeat.

Now unpack the word gradient, because it does a precise job and deserves a precise, friendly definition. The gradient is the slope of the loss, measured right where you are standing, together with the direction in which the loss increases fastest. In one dial, that is simple. The gradient is just how steeply the loss climbs as the dial increases, and its sign tells you the story. If turning the dial up makes the loss rise, the gradient is positive, and downhill is the other way, turn the dial down. If turning the dial up makes the loss fall, the gradient is negative, and downhill is forward, keep turning up. Feel the slope means compute the gradient. Step downhill means move the dial a little against the gradient, against the direction of increase, because the opposite of uphill is where the valley lives. With many dials, the gradient is that same slope-feeling done for every dial at once, a list of slopes, one per dial, each saying how the loss would respond to that dial alone. The mathematics that computes all those slopes efficiently is a later course's pleasure. The idea is fully yours today. Gradient means slope, felt dial by dial, and descent means stepping against it, downhill, toward smaller loss.

Let us walk with tiny numbers, one dial, full steps spoken aloud, because this method must live in your fingers and not just your ears. Our dial is the sunny-day jam prediction from before. The truth, averaged from the notebook, is that sunny days sell twenty-one jars. The loss for a single guess, we will keep simple today, is the squared error, guess minus truth, squared. Start the dial at ten jars, a poor guess. Error is ten minus twenty-one, which is minus eleven in sign and eleven in size. Squared loss is eleven times eleven, one hundred and twenty-one. A big loss, and the slope here is steep, because we are far from the truth. Which way is downhill? Our guess is too low, the loss falls as the dial rises toward twenty-one, so downhill is up the dial numbers, toward larger guesses. Take a step. Not all the way, for a reason I will explain in a moment, but a healthy step, say five jars. Dial now fifteen. Loss is fifteen minus twenty-one, six in size, squared, thirty-six. Down from one hundred and twenty-one to thirty-six. The method is working in public. Step again, smaller now, because the slope is gentler nearer the bottom, say three jars. Dial eighteen. Loss, three squared, nine. Step again, two jars. Dial twenty. Loss, one squared, one. Step again, one jar. Dial twenty-one. Loss zero. The ground is flat. Stop. Read the walk back. Losses of one hundred and twenty-one, thirty-six, nine, one, zero. Steps of five, three, two, one. Notice the marriage between them. Big slope, big step. Small slope, small step. Flat ground, no step at all. That marriage is not a coincidence or a courtesy. It is built into the method, and the knob that controls it has its own name, coming next.

That knob is the learning rate. The learning rate is the number that turns slope into step size. In words, each step equals the learning rate multiplied by the slope. A small learning rate makes cautious, tiny steps even on steep ground. A large learning rate makes bold, leaping steps. And here is the tragedy and comedy of this knob. Both extremes fail, in opposite, memorable ways. Learning rate too small, and the walk takes forever. Imagine steps of a tenth of a jar on our hill. From ten jars you would need over a hundred steps to crawl to twenty-one, and on a real model with millions of examples, that slowness costs real money in computing time, and training may be stopped long before it arrives anywhere good. Learning rate too large, and something worse happens. Overshooting. Imagine a step so big that from fifteen jars you leap clean over the valley to thirty jars. Loss at thirty is nine squared, eighty-one, worse than where you stood. The next slope, now on the far wall, points back, and you leap again, to five jars, worse still. The walk does not settle, it ricochets, and the loss can grow instead of shrinking, a failure with the honest name divergence, the walk flying apart instead of descending. Between the crawl and the ricochet sits the craft. A good learning rate makes the loss fall steadily, step after step, fast enough to afford, calm enough to settle. Practitioners often start a little bold and shrink the rate as training proceeds, big strides on the open hillside, small careful steps near the valley floor, like a walker who runs the easy slope and tiptoes across the flat stones at the bottom. When a colleague says training is unstable, or training is crawling, your first question from today onward is always the same. What is the learning rate? It is the usual suspect, and it is usually guilty.

A question should be forming in your mind, and it is the right question. In my jam walk, I seemed to know the truth, twenty-one, to compute the loss. In real training, where does the slope come from, if the machine cannot see the whole hill? The answer is the quiet miracle of this subject, and I will state it plainly. The machine does not need to see the hill. For the examples in front of it, it can compute, exactly, how the loss would change if each dial moved a tiny amount. That computation is arithmetic on the model's own insides, using the guess, the truth from the label, and the way the dials connect to the guess. No peeking at the future, no map of the landscape, just local feeling, done exactly, for every dial at once. That is why the darkness metaphor is not a weakness of the method but its genius. A walker who can feel the slope exactly, at every step, does not need daylight. She needs patience and a sane learning rate. Real training therefore looks like this, at scale. Show the model a batch of examples, a batch being just a group of examples handled together, perhaps a few dozen or a few hundred rows. Compute the average loss on that batch. Compute the gradient, the slope for every dial, on that batch. Step every dial a learning-rate-sized amount downhill. Repeat with the next batch. Thousands of repetitions later, the average loss has walked down from embarrassing to useful, and the dials hold the pattern of the data. One pass through the whole training set is called an epoch, another word worth owning, and training usually runs for many epochs, the walk circling the dataset again and again, descending a little more each pass, until the loss flattens and further walking buys nothing worth its cost.

Now the honest complications, because a teacher who only shows you the sunny version of an idea has taught you a brochure, not a subject. First complication, the landscape is not one smooth bowl. With many dials, the loss landscape can have dips that are not the deepest valley, called local minima, where local means nearby and minimum means a low point. A local minimum is a dip where every small step leads uphill, so the slope-feeling walker stops, believing she has arrived, while a deeper valley exists elsewhere, beyond a ridge she never crossed. In our one-dial jam world the landscape was a single bowl and this could not happen. In real models it can, though in very large models it turns out to be less of a trap than early researchers feared, because with millions of dials there are usually side paths out of shallow dips. Still, the concept matters for your thinking. Gradient descent guarantees a downhill walk. It does not guarantee the deepest valley on earth. It guarantees improvement, patiently, locally, which in practice is usually enough, and in theory is a limitation you should be able to state in one sentence, as you now can. Second complication, flat regions and steep cliffs both exist. On a plateau, the slope is nearly zero while the loss is still poor, and the walk crawls, not from a small learning rate but from flat ground. Down a cliff, the slope is enormous, and one step can hurl the dials into nonsense. These are managed with refinements you will meet later, all of them variations on the same theme. Feel slope, step carefully, repeat, and adjust the stepping rules when the terrain misbehaves. The theme never changes. Only the footwear improves.

Our guided think-it moment today is a walk you take yourself, with numbers I will give you and one decision that is yours. A dial predicts the hours for a job type. The true average is eight hours. The dial starts at two hours. Squared loss at the start is six squared, thirty-six, a steep hillside, since two is far from eight. You may choose a first step of one hour, three hours, or twelve hours. Choose, and defend your choice in your head before I give mine. Take a breath. Here is the defence I hope you built. A step of twelve hours lands the dial at fourteen, past the truth, with a loss of six squared again, thirty-six, no progress at all and a wasted move, overshooting in its purest form. A step of one hour lands at three, loss twenty-five, progress, but slow, five more such steps at least, the crawl. A step of three hours lands at five, loss nine, strong progress with no drama, and the next slope, gentler now, invites a step of two, landing at seven, loss one, then a step of one, landing at eight, loss zero. Three well-sized steps did what twelve-hour leaping could not do at all. If you chose three hours, your instinct for learning rates is already forming. If you chose otherwise, you have felt, cheaply and in your head, exactly the two failure modes that cost real projects real money. Either way the lesson has done its work, because gradient descent is not a formula to memorise. It is a walker's judgment, and judgment grows by walking, even imaginary hills, especially imaginary hills where the falls are free.

Let me tie this lesson back to the small business owner whose life has carried our examples, because gradient descent is happening inside tools she already rents, whether she names it or not. When her marketing platform says it is optimising her advert delivery, dials inside are being walked downhill against a loss that counts wasted spend and missed customers in some proportion somebody chose. When her quoting tool improves after a season of jobs, its dials descended on her own labelled rows, hours predicted against hours taken, loss falling epoch by epoch in a data centre she will never see. She does not need to run this walk herself. She needs to know it exists, that it is local and patient rather than magical and instant, and that when the tool behaves strangely, the grown-up questions are the ones from this course. What loss is it descending? At what learning rate, in effect, how boldly does it change its mind? On which examples, whose labels, measured honestly how? A tool whose owner can answer those is a tool being run by adults. A tool whose owner cannot is a hillside walked blindfold, and the darkness in our title is friendly only when the walker can feel the slope. Ignorance of the slope is the only true darkness in this subject. You are now several lessons clear of it.

One more image before the recap, the one I personally keep, because it corrects the most common misunderstanding of all. People imagine training as the machine studying, like a student reading a book once and understanding it. Gradient descent is not reading. It is tuning, like a musician tuning a string by ear. Pluck, listen, the note is flat, tighten a fraction, pluck again, listen again, closer, tighten less, until the note rings true. Nobody would say the musician learned the song by tuning the guitar, and nobody should say the model understands the world because its dials descended well. Tuning is real skill and real value. A tuned instrument plays beautifully in trained hands. But the tuning process itself knows nothing of music. It knows slope and loss, error and step. Keeping that boundary bright, between the power of the method and the absence of understanding inside it, is the mark of someone who actually knows this field, as opposed to someone who has merely been impressed by it. You are becoming the first kind of person. That is the entire point of this university.

Let me give you one more full walk, slower, with a second dial joining the first, because real models never tune one dial alone and the interaction between dials is where beginners get dizzy. Imagine a model predicting delivery time in minutes using two dials. Dial A is the minutes it assumes for loading the van. Dial B is the minutes it assumes per mile of driving. The truth in the world, hidden from the model, is loading takes ten minutes and driving takes three minutes per mile. A delivery of five miles should therefore take ten plus fifteen, twenty-five minutes. Start both dials wrong. Dial A says four minutes, Dial B says one minute per mile. Prediction for the five-mile delivery, four plus five, nine minutes. Truth twenty-five. Error sixteen, a steep hillside indeed. Now feel the slopes separately, which is the gradient's whole job. If Dial A rises by one minute, the prediction rises by one minute, error shrinks, so Dial A's slope says climb the dial. If Dial B rises by one minute per mile, the prediction rises by five minutes, because the mile charge applies five times, error shrinks five times faster, so Dial B's slope is stronger. A good step therefore moves both dials up, but moves Dial B more boldly in effect, because its slope is steeper. Say Dial A steps to seven and Dial B steps to two. Prediction, seven plus ten, seventeen minutes. Error eight, halved. Another round. Dial A to nine, Dial B to two and a half. Prediction, nine plus twelve and a half, twenty-one and a half. Error three and a half. Another round, smaller steps as the slope gentles. Dial A to ten, Dial B to three. Prediction twenty-five. Error zero. Three rounds, two dials, one walk. Multiply that picture by a million dials and a million deliveries and you have industrial training, with no new idea added, only scale. The gradient is simply the discipline of asking, for every dial at once, if I move you a little, does the loss rise or fall, and by how much. Asked a million times a second, that humble question tunes the largest machines on earth.

While that picture settles, notice something about batches that protects the walk from a single strange example. Suppose one delivery in a batch was freakish, a road closed, the van waited an hour, the label says eighty-five minutes for a trip the dials rightly predict at twenty-five. If training stepped on that example alone, the dials would lurch toward nonsense, chasing an accident. Averaged inside a batch of, say, fifty deliveries, that one freak contributes one fiftieth of the slope, a tug, not a lurch. Batches are the walker's common sense. Do not change your whole route because of one strange stone. Feel the average slope of the ground you are crossing. This is also why shuffling examples matters, mixing the batches so each one represents the whole territory fairly, city deliveries beside rural ones, Mondays beside Saturdays. A walk fed only on one neighbourhood's slopes will tune beautifully for that neighbourhood and wander lost in the next. Representativeness, a plain word for a fair mix, is as much a part of descent as the arithmetic, because the slope you feel is only ever the slope of the examples you just stood on.

And a word about stopping, since a walk that never ends is as useless as a walk that ends too soon. Training stops, in honest practice, for one of three reasons. The loss has flattened, the ground is level, further steps buy nothing. The budget has run out, because computing costs money and a good-enough model today beats a perfect model next quarter. Or, the subtlest reason, performance on held-back examples has stopped improving even while training loss still falls, which is the fingerprint of the memorising disease we dissect next lesson. That third stopping signal is so important that professionals give the held-back set its own name and watch it like a hawk during every walk. Hold that thought, unnamed for one more lesson, and let it make you curious. The walk downhill is powerful, patient and blind in specific ways, and knowing exactly where its blindness lies is what separates the engineer from the enthusiast. You are collecting those blindnesses lesson by lesson, deliberately, like a walker memorising the cliff edges of a beloved route in daylight, so she can cross it safely in the dark forever after.

Let me leave you with the arithmetic of patience, because it explains why this field exploded when computers got fast rather than when the idea was born, the idea being far older than the machines that made it practical. Suppose a modest model has one hundred thousand dials, trains on one hundred thousand examples, in batches of one hundred, for twenty epochs. Count the walks. One hundred thousand examples divided by one hundred per batch is one thousand batches per epoch. Twenty epochs makes twenty thousand steps of the walk. At each step, the gradient must be felt for all one hundred thousand dials, so the total slope-feelings number twenty thousand times one hundred thousand, which is two thousand million, two billion small arithmetic acts, before the model is done. No human team does that by hand in a lifetime. A modern computer does it between breakfast and lunch without warming up. Gradient descent did not change when the computers arrived. The number of affordable steps changed, and with it, the size of hill that patience could cross. When you hear that a giant model trained for months on a warehouse of machines, translate it with today's lesson. A very long walk, a very large hillside, a learning rate chosen by people who have ricocheted off enough valleys to respect the knob. There is no step in that story you have not now taken yourself, on foot, with jam jars, at walking pace, which is exactly the pace understanding prefers.

Recap, in order. First, gradient descent is the disciplined walk of training. Feel the slope, step downhill, repeat, until the ground is flat and the loss stops falling. Second, the gradient is the slope of the loss where you stand, dial by dial, and stepping against the gradient, against the direction of increase, is stepping downhill. Third, in our jam walk the losses fell from one hundred and twenty-one to thirty-six to nine to one to zero, with steps shrinking as the slope gentled, exactly as the method intends. Fourth, the learning rate turns slope into step size, and it fails at both extremes, crawling when too small and overshooting or diverging when too large. Fifth, training runs in batches and epochs, many small descents averaged over many examples, not one leap from one example. Sixth, the walk guarantees improvement, not perfection. Local minima, plateaus and cliffs are real terrain, managed by refinements, and the method tunes dials brilliantly while understanding nothing, which you must never forget.

One sentence to sleep on, and it is the sentence practitioners mutter at misbehaving training runs in real laboratories. If the loss is not falling, the walk is telling you something about the slope, the step, or the ground itself, and shouting at the walker has never yet flattened a hill.

Before you go, test yourself silently on the delivery dials one final time, because fluency is the goal and fluency only comes from one more repetition done alone. Loading dial too low and miles dial too low together made a prediction of nine against a truth of twenty-five, and both slopes pointed up, and the miles dial pointed up harder because its effect repeats on every mile of the journey. If you can still see why the second dial deserved the bolder step, you own the gradient idea in a form no forgetting curve will easily take from you, and you are ready for anything the rest of this course builds on top of it.

Walk out of this room with the walker's creed in your pocket. Feel the slope honestly, step against it sanely, repeat without drama, and distrust any stride so large it promises the valley in one leap. That creed tunes models, and frankly it learns most human skills too, which is why this lesson tends to follow students home.
