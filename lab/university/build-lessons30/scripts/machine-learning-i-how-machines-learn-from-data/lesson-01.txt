# Examples in, rules out: the inversion of programming

In, in. Come on in and sit down. I am Professor Sana Rahal, and this is Machine Learning One, How Machines Learn From Data. This is our very first lesson together, and its title tells you almost the whole secret in five words. Examples in, rules out, the inversion of programming. Inversion is a plain word once we unpack it. It means a turning upside down, a flipping of the order you expected. In this lesson you will learn what ordinary programming does, what machine learning does instead, why that flip matters, and how to spot which approach fits which job. By the end you will be able to explain the difference to a friend in your own words, with no jargon left unpacked, and you will see why this one flip changed what computers can do for a small business, for a family, and for the world. My office door carries a little hand-written sign that says stuck is a place, not a person. If you feel stuck at any point in this lesson, that is fine. Stuck is just where we start. We draw the diagram first, and then we name the thing. That is how I teach, and that is how we will go today.

Let me draw the first diagram in words, slowly. Picture two boxes on a whiteboard. In the first box, on the left, I write the word rules. In the second box, in the middle, I write the word computer. An arrow goes from rules into the computer, and another arrow comes out of the computer on the right into a third box that says answers. That is ordinary programming. A person thinks hard, writes down exact rules, feeds those rules into the computer, and the computer follows the rules to produce answers. Now picture a second diagram underneath it. In the first box this time I write the word examples. In the middle box I still write computer, but I add underneath it the words learning program. An arrow goes from examples into that learning program, and the arrow coming out on the right does not go to answers this time. It goes to a box that says rules. That is machine learning. A person gathers many examples, feeds those examples into a learning program, and the program works out rules of its own. Same computer. Opposite direction. Rules in, answers out is the old way. Examples in, rules out is the new way. Hold onto that picture, because everything else in this whole class is just that picture getting more detailed.

Let me make the old way completely concrete, because you already know it from everyday life even if you have never written a line of code. Code is just instructions written in a language a computer can follow exactly. A program is a set of those instructions. Suppose you run a small shop and you want a simple program to work out a discount. You might tell the computer, in plain English first, if a customer spends more than one hundred dollars, take ten percent off, otherwise charge the full price. That is a rule. You thought of it. You decided the number one hundred and the number ten percent. The computer did not decide anything. It just obeys, quickly and without getting tired. Or suppose you want a program to add up a till at the end of the day. You tell it, take every sale, add them one by one, and print the total. Again, you supplied the method. The computer supplied the speed. For jobs like that, ordinary programming is perfect, because you already know the rule and the rule does not change. A calculator is the purest case. You press seven plus five, and deep inside, rules about addition turn your input into the answer twelve. Nobody needs the calculator to learn addition from examples. The rule for addition is known, exact, and settled. When the rule is known and exact, write the rule. That is lesson zero, and it never stops being true.

Now feel where the old way starts to strain. Suppose you want a program that looks at an email and decides whether it is junk mail or real mail. Try to write the rules yourself. You might start with, if the email says free money, mark it as junk. That catches some junk. Then the junk senders stop writing free money and start writing free m0ney with a zero instead of the letter o. So you add another rule. Then they change again. You add a rule about links, a rule about capital letters, a rule about certain senders, a rule about spelling mistakes. After a month you have five hundred rules, after a year you have five thousand, and the junk still slips through, because the other side keeps changing and because real mail sometimes looks a little bit like junk too. A note from your bank about a free service is real. A note from a stranger about free money is not. The difference is real, but it is fuzzy, and fuzzy differences are very hard to write as exact rules by hand. The same strain shows up with pictures. Write a rule for telling a cat from a dog in a photograph. Furry, four legs, tail — that describes both. Pointy ears — some dogs have those too. You can feel the pattern when you see a hundred cats and a hundred dogs, but turning that feeling into exact written rules defeats almost everyone. The pattern is in the examples, not in a rule you can state in advance. That gap, between a pattern you can recognise in examples and a rule you cannot write by hand, is exactly the gap machine learning was built to cross.

So here is the new way, step by step, with nothing hidden. First, you collect examples. An example is one case where you already know the answer. For the junk mail job, an example is one email together with its correct label, junk or not junk. Label is our second new word, and it just means the correct answer attached to an example. A pile of examples with their labels is called training data. Training is the word for the learning process itself, and training data is the pile the machine learns from. Second, you choose a kind of flexible pattern-finder, called a model. A model is a mathematical shape with adjustable settings inside it, a bit like a radio with many dials. At the start the dials are set almost at random, so the model is useless. Third, you show the model the examples one by one. For each example, the model makes a guess, you compare the guess with the true label, and you nudge the dials a tiny bit in the direction that would have made the guess better. We will spend whole lessons later on how that nudging works, so for now just hold the picture of dials being nudged, example after example. Fourth, after seeing many examples, sometimes thousands, sometimes millions, the dial settings settle into a pattern that works well on the examples. Those settled dial settings are, in effect, the rules the machine found. Nobody wrote them. They grew out of the examples. Fifth and last, you use the model on new cases it has never seen, and if it learned well, it guesses well there too. Examples in, rules out. That five-step story is the whole of machine learning in miniature, and every lesson in this class zooms into one part of it.

I want to slow down on the word rules, because it can mislead you if we are not careful. When I say the machine finds rules, I do not mean it writes sentences like if the email says free money, mark it as junk. Sometimes the rules it finds can be read almost like sentences, and later courses look at those cases. But usually the rules are numbers, dial settings, weights — we will meet that word properly in the next lessons — that work together in a way no human would read like a story. The rules are real, in the sense that they decide what the model does, but they live as numbers inside the model, not as English on a page. That is both the power and the price of the flip. The power is that the machine can capture patterns too fuzzy and too tangled for us to write down. The price is that we cannot always explain, in plain sentences, exactly why the model gave the answer it gave. We can test whether it works. We can measure how often it is right. But reading its mind is harder than reading a rule a person wrote. A good practitioner never forgets that trade. You gain reach, and you give up some transparency, and you manage that trade with honest testing, which is where this class will end, in lesson six, with the exam the model has never seen.

Let me give you a small worked example you can follow entirely in your head, with tiny numbers, because numbers make the flip vivid. Imagine you sell jam at a market stall, and you want to predict how many jars you will sell on a Saturday based on the weather. You have no rule for that. Nobody does. But you have your notebook from the last four Saturdays. Saturday one was sunny and you sold twenty jars. Saturday two was sunny and you sold twenty-two jars. Saturday three was rainy and you sold ten jars. Saturday four was rainy and you sold twelve jars. Those four lines in your notebook are training data. Each line is an example. The weather is the input, what you are shown, and the number of jars is the label, the correct answer for that day. Now, what rule would a human eye find in those four lines? Sunny days average twenty-one jars, because twenty and twenty-two average to twenty-one. Rainy days average eleven jars, because ten and twelve average to eleven. So the rule the examples suggest is simple. If it is sunny, expect about twenty-one jars. If it is rainy, expect about eleven jars. Next Saturday the forecast says sunny, so you pack twenty-one jars, maybe a couple extra to be safe. Notice what just happened. You did not start with a rule about jam and weather handed down by an expert. You started with examples and the rule came out of them. You just did, by hand and in miniature, exactly what a learning program does at scale. The machine would do the same averaging in spirit, though with far more examples, far more inputs than just weather, and dials nudged by a careful procedure instead of by your eye. If the numbers had been messier, say sunny days selling eighteen, twenty-five, nineteen and thirty, your eye would still find roughly the middle, and so would the machine. Learning, at its heart, is finding the pattern that sits in the middle of messy examples. That is worth saying twice. Learning is finding the pattern that sits in the middle of messy examples.

Now let us test the flip on a second everyday job, a little bigger, still from a small business owner's real life. Imagine you run a small marketing service. Clients come to you because they want more customers. Every week, people fill in a form on your website. Some of those people become paying clients, and most do not. After a year you have records on, let us say, two hundred people who filled in the form. For each person you know a few facts, how they found you, how big their business is, whether they opened your follow-up email, and crucially, whether they became a client or not. That last fact is the label. Could you write exact rules by hand for who will become a client? You could try. People with bigger businesses, maybe. People who open the email, maybe. But the real pattern is a blend. Perhaps people from referrals who open the email and run a business with five to twenty staff become clients quite often, while people from a cold advert who never open anything almost never do, with dozens of shades in between. Writing that blend as exact hand rules is the junk mail problem all over again. But feeding the two hundred examples to a learning program is natural. The program nudges its dials until its guesses line up with what actually happened across those two hundred cases. Then, when a new person fills in the form next week, the model gives you a score, a sense of how likely this person is to become a client, and you spend your limited calling time on the most promising people first. You have not replaced your judgment. You have aimed it. That is a sentence I want you to keep for the whole course. A good model does not replace your judgment, it aims it. And notice, the model only knows what the examples taught it. If your two hundred records all came from one town, the model may misjudge people from a very different town. The examples set the limits of the rules. Garbage examples in, garbage rules out, is the old warning, and it is exactly right. The flip does not create wisdom from nothing. It extracts the pattern that is actually in the data you gave it, no more and no less.

Here is a third angle on the same flip, and it is the one that helps most people feel it in their bones. Think about how a child learns the word dog. Nobody hands a two-year-old a written definition with rules about ears and tails and then tests her on the definition. Instead, adults point. That is a dog. That is a dog too. That is a cat, not a dog. Example after example, with labels spoken aloud, and slowly the child builds an inner pattern that lets her point at a dog she has never seen before and say dog, correctly, with delight. She cannot state her rules. Ask her why that one is a dog and she will say because it just is. Her rules live in her, as tuned connections, not as sentences. Machine learning copies that human route on purpose. It learns the way children learn, from labelled examples, because for fuzzy patterns in a messy world, that route works where the definition route fails. And it fails the way children fail too. Show a child only tiny dogs and she may call a horse a big dog. Show a model only one narrow kind of example and it will make the matching mistake. The cure in both cases is the same, broader and better examples, honestly labelled. When you hear someone say the machine learned, this is the honest meaning underneath the phrase. Nobody programmed the answer. The pattern was grown from examples, the way a child's pattern grows from pointing and naming.

We should also be precise about what the flip is not, because loose talk causes real confusion. Machine learning is not magic, and the model does not understand the world the way you do. Your jam model does not know what rain is. It knows that in your notebook, the rainy lines carried smaller numbers. Machine learning is not the same as ordinary statistics disappearing, either. Averages, like our twenty-one and eleven, are part of its toolkit, not a rival to it. And machine learning is not always the right tool. If the rule is known and exact, write the rule. Adding up a till, working out tax at a fixed rate, checking whether a password matches the stored password — these want written rules, exact and auditable, and a learning model would only add fog. A professional chooses. Known rule, stable world, exact answer needed — write the program. Fuzzy pattern, plenty of examples, a good guess is valuable — teach the model. Some real systems mix both, written rules guarding the edges while a model handles the fuzzy middle. Knowing which half of the diagram you are in, rules in or examples in, is the first skill of this whole field, and you now have it.

One more worked pass, because I want the dials picture to be solid before lesson two builds on it. Imagine a model as a single dial and a single job, guessing jam sales on sunny days only. The dial is the model's current guess for a sunny day, one number. You start the dial at zero, a silly guess. First sunny example, you sold twenty jars. The guess of zero is too low by twenty, so you nudge the dial up, but only part of the way, say to ten. Second sunny example, you sold twenty-two jars. The guess of ten is too low by twelve, so you nudge up again, say to sixteen. Third sunny example, twenty jars again. The guess of sixteen is low by four, nudge to eighteen. You can feel the dial homing in, step by smaller step, toward the neighbourhood of twenty-one, the average we found earlier. That homing-in, done patiently over many examples, is training. The final dial position is the learned rule for sunny days, in the form of a number. Real models have thousands or millions of dials and a far cleverer nudging method, which lesson four will teach you under its proper name, gradient descent. But the soul of it is already here in this little dial story. Guess, compare with the truth, nudge, repeat. Guess, compare, nudge, repeat. If you remember nothing else from today, remember those four beats, because they beat underneath every machine learning system ever built, from a jam stall notebook to the largest systems on earth.

Let me also name the people in this story, because in real work these roles matter. The person who collects and labels the examples is doing data work, and sloppy labels poison everything downstream. The person who chooses the model shape and runs the training is doing modelling work. The person who will use the model's guesses, like you deciding whom to call first, is the decision maker, and the model serves that person's decision, not the other way round. In a small business, one person may wear all three hats in a single afternoon. In a hospital or a bank, whole teams split the hats. Either way, the flip stays the same at the centre. Somewhere, examples went in, and rules came out, and a decision got better aimed because of it. When a vendor tells you their system uses artificial intelligence, the grown-up follow-up questions are now yours to ask. What examples did it learn from? How many, and how honestly labelled? How do you test it on cases it has never seen? A vendor who can answer those clearly has a real system. A vendor who cannot is selling fog. This class will give you, lesson by lesson, the knowledge to tell the difference for yourself.

Before we recap, let me answer the three questions every beginner asks at this point, because you may be asking them quietly right now. First question, does the machine think up its rules from nothing, like an inventor? No, and this matters. The person still chooses the shape of the model, chooses which examples to collect, chooses what counts as the label, and chooses how success will be measured. The machine searches inside the space the person built. It is a powerful search, and it finds patterns no person spotted, but it searches where it was pointed. Human judgment frames the job at both ends, before training and after it. Second question, how many examples does learning need? It depends on how fuzzy the pattern is and how much the examples vary. Our jam rule settled with four examples because the pattern was simple, one input, weather, and two clear groups. Junk mail needs tens of thousands of examples because senders vary endlessly and real mail varies too. A useful rule of thumb is this, the fuzzier the pattern and the more ways the world can vary, the more examples you need before you should trust the rules that come out. Third question, can the learned rules ever beat the person who collected the examples? At the narrow job they were trained on, yes, often, and that is not spooky. You can recognise thousands of faces yet be unable to write the rule for even one. A model trained on enough labelled faces can sort new faces more reliably than you can, while knowing nothing about birthdays, jokes, or friendship. Narrow skill grown from examples can exceed the teacher on that narrow skill, while general understanding stays entirely with the person. Holding both halves of that sentence at once, impressed by the narrow skill and clear-eyed about the missing understanding, is the mature attitude this whole class is trying to build in you.

One last story, and it is a true shape of story even though the stall is mine, about the cost of choosing the wrong side of the flip. A shopkeeper I imagine, let us call her Ana, runs a bakery and wants to know which mornings will be busy so she can bake the right amount and waste less. Ana first tries the rules route. She writes rules with her nephew. Rainy mornings are quiet, except when they are not, because the school run still happens. Sunny Fridays are busy, except in the holidays. She ends the month with a notebook full of exceptions, each rule patched with an unless, and the waste barely moves. Then Ana tries the examples route. For sixty mornings she simply records what happened. The day, the weather, whether school was in, whether the roadworks were active, and the label, how many loaves she sold by ten o'clock. Sixty honest examples. The pattern that comes out is one she could feel but never state. Roadworks plus school plus rain is her busiest combination, because drivers stuck in traffic buy coffee and bread while they wait. No hand-written rule of hers had ever joined those three facts together. Her waste falls, not to zero, because the world keeps some randomness, but enough to matter every single week. Ana did not become a programmer. She became a good collector of examples, and she asked the grown-up questions about testing before she trusted the scores. That is the realistic promise of this field for a small business. Not robots, not magic, just patterns you already half know, made solid by examples you already half have, once you flip which side of the diagram you start from.

Time to recap, in order, the way we will always recap. First, ordinary programming is rules in, answers out. A person writes exact rules, and the computer follows them. Second, machine learning flips that order. It is examples in, rules out. A learning program studies labelled examples and finds its own rules, held as dial settings inside a model. Third, an example is one case with a known answer, that known answer is its label, and the pile of examples is the training data. Fourth, training follows four beats, guess, compare with the truth, nudge the dials, repeat, until the guesses line up with the examples. Fifth, the flip wins where patterns are fuzzy and examples are plentiful, like junk mail, pictures, and likely clients, and it loses where rules are known and exact, like addition, discounts, and tax, where you should simply write the rule. Sixth, the learned rules are only as good as the examples, because the pattern in the data is all the machine can extract. Examples in, rules out, but honest examples, or the rules will mislead you with a straight face.
