'Could the Stars Alone Do It? Self-Gravity Dispersions and Dark Fractions for Eleven Ultra-Faint Dwarfs'. Its stated result: Stars alone underpredict the observed dispersions of the ultra-faint sample by a median factor given in the abstract, implying median dark fractions above ninety percent within the half-light radii. Listen carefully here — this piece carries weight in the questions later.

'Counting Too-Big-To-Fail: Circular Velocities of Milky Way Satellites Against Subhalo Tallies'. Its stated result: The observed satellite system, weighed with one estimator, contains fewer high-circular-velocity objects than published subhalo tallies predict, with the deficit at the 25 kilometre per second threshold stated numerically in the abstract.

We take the pieces in order, because each one stands on the last.

'From Survey Yields to a Galactic Population: Scaling the Dark Energy Survey Ultra-Faint Counts to the Whole Sky'. Now my worked example — this class's set problem, worked in full. The problem: Take the paper “Concentration Proxies for Twenty Satellite Halos Against the Mass-Concentration Relation at Its Low-Mass End”. Its stated result is: Concentration proxies for the twenty satellites sit systematically above the extrapolated mass-concentration relation, by the median residual reported in the abstract, an offset traced to the stated scale-radius mapping rather than to the galaxies themselves. (a) Restate the claim in your own words. (b) Name one observation or computation that could prove it wrong. The worked answer: A strong answer restates the claim without borrowing the paper’s wording, then names a test the paper itself would accept — here, the natural test is to recompute or re-measure the headline quantity in the stated result and check it against the paper’s own number, under the paper’s own stated assumptions.
