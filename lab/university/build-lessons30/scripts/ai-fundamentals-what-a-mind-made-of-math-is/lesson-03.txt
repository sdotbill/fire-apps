# What a model is — a function with a memory of examples

Come in. I am Professor David Kim, and this is AI Fundamentals, lesson three. In lesson one we named the thing: programs that learn patterns from examples. In lesson two we counted its ingredients on our fingers: data, parameters, training — from, adjusted, adjusting. Today we slow right down on the object those ingredients produce, the model itself, because the word model is the word you will hear most often in this field and the word most often left undefined by the people using it. Our title today says what a model is: a function with a memory of examples. By the end of the lesson you will know what a function is in plain words, what kind of memory a model has and what kind it does not have, and you will be able to answer this class's set problem in your own words, because we are going to work it together, in full, before you ever face it alone. That is why this lesson matters. The model is the product. It is the thing being sold, rented, guarded, and argued about. If you know exactly what it is, nobody can sell you a fog sculpture and call it a model.

First, the word function, because it frightens people unnecessarily and it should not. A function is a machine for turning inputs into outputs. That is the whole definition, and everything else is decoration. An input goes in, the function does its work, an output comes out. A coffee machine is a function: beans and water in, coffee out. A recipe is a function: ingredients in, dinner out. A vending machine is a function: coins and a button press in, a packet of crisps out. In mathematics the word is used with more precision, but the picture never changes: something goes in, something comes out, and the function is the thing in the middle that decides what the out will be for any given in. When I say a model is a function, I mean exactly this and nothing more mystical. A trained spam filter is a function: an email goes in, a verdict comes out, junk or not junk. A trained pricing tool is a function: the details of a job go in, a suggested price comes out. A trained chatbot is a function: the conversation so far goes in, the next words come out. Inputs, outputs, and the deciding thing in the middle. If you hold that picture, you hold the concept, and the rest of this lesson is about what makes the deciding thing in the middle different from a coffee machine.

The difference is where the deciding came from. The coffee machine's behaviour was designed: an engineer decided what it would do, part by part. The model's behaviour was grown. Remember the recipe picture from our earliest work together. A recipe is a function: ingredients in, dinner out. Now picture a recipe nobody wrote. Instead it was adjusted a million times — a little more salt, a little less — tasting after every try, until the dinners came out right. That adjusted recipe is a model: a function shaped by examples until its output — its guess — is usually good. Nobody sat down and designed the final recipe. It arrived at its final form by being corrected, over and over, against real dinners. That is the grown function, and every model you will ever meet is one: a function whose deciding was not designed rule by rule, but shaped, nudge by nudge, by the examples it was trained on. Lesson two gave you the mechanism: the parameters are the dials, training is the turning, data is what the turning answers to. The model is what you have when the turning stops: the function, with its dials set, ready to take new inputs and produce its guesses.

Now the second half of our title, the memory of examples, and here I must be careful, because this phrase is a good servant and a bad master. It is true that a model behaves as if it remembers its training. Show a model a new enquiry that resembles the enquiries it was trained on, and it answers the way those enquiries taught it to answer. Its past is present in every guess it makes. In that sense, memory is the right word, and I chose it for the title deliberately. But the memory is not a filing cabinet. The model does not contain its examples. The photographs it trained on are not stored inside it as photographs. The emails are not in there as emails. What is inside is the settings, the parameters, the vast list of numbers, and the examples survive only as the shape those numbers were bent into. Think of a riverbed. The riverbed does not contain last year's floods. But its shape is the memory of every flood that ever ran through it, and the next flood will follow that shape. Think of your own hands after years of work: they do not contain the jobs, but the skill is in them, and the skill is a memory of the jobs in everything but name. A model is like that. It has a memory of examples the way hands have a memory of work: not stored, but embodied. Settled into the settings. Present in every output, retrievable as no particular example at all.

This distinction does real work, so let me press on it with two consequences you will actually use. Consequence one: because the examples are not stored, a model can generalise, which is the field's word for handling a case it has never seen. Your spam filter will correctly judge an email that has never existed before, written this morning by a scammer inventing fresh wording, because the filter is not matching the message against stored messages. It is running the message through settings shaped by the pattern of all messages, and new messages carry old patterns. Generalising is the whole point of the enterprise. A system that could only handle cases it had literally seen would be a lookup table, and lookup tables do not need training, or parameters, or this course. Consequence two, and it cuts the other way: because the examples are not stored, a model cannot show you its evidence. Ask a person why they judged a message junk and they can point: this phrase, that sender, the last three like it. Ask the model and there is nothing to point at. There are only the settings, all of them contributing a little, none of them containing the reason in a form you could read. The model has conclusions without a filing cabinet, judgement without a notebook. That is not a flaw anyone is hiding from you; it is the direct price of the design, and knowing it will keep you from asking these systems for the one thing they structurally cannot give, which is a receipt. Later classes in this department will talk about what can be done around that limit. For today, let it stand plainly: the memory is real, and it is a memory of shape, not of items.

Let me walk one model through its whole life, slowly, so the abstraction gets a face. Take a lead scorer for your company, a small, unglamorous, genuinely useful model: it reads a new enquiry and predicts whether this person is likely to become a paying client, so you can answer the promising ones first. Before training, this function exists but its dials are random, and its predictions are coin flips wearing a suit. Then comes the data: your past enquiries, hundreds of them, each labelled with what actually happened — became a client, or went quiet, or haggled for a month and vanished. Then training: enquiry after enquiry run through the dials, guess compared with what actually happened, nudge, next, until the guesses line up with reality often enough to trust. What patterns did it find? Perhaps that enquiries naming a deadline became clients, that enquiries asking only about price on the first line usually did not, that messages from business owners who mentioned their town by name were serious in a way the others were not. Nobody told it those things. Nobody wrote those rules. They are in the settings now, as shape. And here is the part I want you to sit with: you, the owner, could not have written those rules either, not because you lack the knowledge, but because your knowledge of your own clients lives in exactly the same form as the model's — as shape, as feel, as the judgement in your hands after years of enquiries. The model is a second pair of trained hands. That is all it is, and that is not a small thing to have. But notice: it predicts who will become a client. It does not understand why anyone buys anything, and on the day a strange enquiry arrives carrying none of the old patterns, it will guess with the same confidence and a fraction of the grounds. A function with a memory of examples is only ever as worldly as its examples.

And now we arrive at the set problem of this class, and we work it in full, together, the way I promised. Here is the problem, exactly as this course sets it. In your own words, one paragraph: what is a model, and what did training do to it? Use one example from your own life where prediction — not understanding — was enough. Let me work the answer the way I would want to see it worked, and then we will take it apart bone by bone so you can build your own. The worked answer: a model is a function — a machine for turning inputs into outputs — whose internal settings were adjusted, over millions of examples, until its predictions matched reality often enough to be useful. Training is that adjustment. A spam filter never understood a word of your email; prediction was enough, and knowing the difference between the two is the whole first lesson. Now look at the shape of that answer, because the shape is a scaffold you are welcome to steal. It stands on six bones: function, inputs, outputs, parameters, examples, predictions. Function names the kind of thing it is. Inputs and outputs name what flows through it. Parameters name what is inside it. Examples name what shaped it. Predictions name what it produces. Build your own answer on the same bones, in your own words, with your own example, and it will stand up. Leave a bone out and it wobbles: describe a model without examples and you have described a designed program; describe it without predictions and you have described a storage box. Six bones. Function, inputs, outputs, parameters, examples, predictions.

For your own example, choose from your real life, and the test of a good choice is that prediction really was enough and understanding really was absent. The spam filter is the cleanest case and you are allowed to use it, but your own week offers others. The map predicting your arrival time: you did not need it to understand traffic, you needed to know whether to leave now. The platform predicting who will linger on your post: it understands nothing about your work, and its prediction still shapes your reach. The tool predicting which past client is likely to need you again: you do not need it to understand loyalty, you need a short list on Monday morning. Pick one, name what went in, name what came out, and say plainly that the prediction was enough. That last move, saying it plainly, is the soul of the answer. It shows you know the difference between the two things, and knowing the difference, as I have said since lesson one, is most of this subject.

Now our guided try-it, because an answer like this should be built once with company before it is ever built alone. I will start a paragraph, out loud, and I want you to finish it in your head, or better, under your breath, in your own words. A model is a function. It takes in — and here you name an input from your own business, an enquiry, a photograph of a finished job, a month's invoices — and it gives out — and here you name the output, a prediction: who will buy, what it should cost, which words will draw the call. Its settings were adjusted — and here you name the examples, your examples, the labelled past — until its predictions matched reality often enough to be useful. And it does not understand — name the thing it does not understand — and it does not need to, because for this job, prediction is enough. Run that shape twice. Once for the spam filter, once for something in your company. By the second run you will feel the paragraph become yours, and that feeling is the lesson landing. This is how every hard idea in this course will be handled: I work it in full, you work it with me, then it is yours.

Two confusions remain that I want to burn off before the recap, because they attach themselves to the word model like burrs. The first confusion is between the model and the product. The model is the function, the frozen settings. The product is the model plus everything wrapped around it: the screen you type into, the company that hosts it, the monthly fee, the cheerful name. When a product delights you or fails you, it is worth asking which layer you are actually judging, because products fail for product reasons, a bad screen, a missing feature, every day, while the model inside is fine, and models fail quietly inside products whose screens are beautiful. Keep the layers apart and your judgements get sharper and your complaints get addressed to the right address. The second confusion is between the model and the machine it runs on. The model is the settings; the machine is the computer that runs inputs through them. The same model can run on a warehouse of machines or, for small models, on the phone in your pocket, and it is the same model either way, the way the same recipe is the same recipe in a big kitchen or a small one. Size of kitchen, quality of dinner: related, but not the same question. Model, product, machine. Three layers. Most muddled arguments about this technology are two of these layers being traded mid-sentence, and now you can hear it happen in real time, which is a small superpower I am happy to give you.

Before the recap, let me deepen the function picture, because you will lean on it in every later class and I want it load-bearing. Functions can be chained, and chaining is how modest models become impressive systems without any new magic entering the room. The output of one function becomes the input of the next. Your phone does this when you dictate a message: one function turns sound into words, another turns words into a tidied sentence, a third turns the sentence into a prediction about what you will say next. Three functions, chained, and from the outside it looks like one smooth act of understanding. It is not one act. It is three guessers handing their guesses along a line, and if the first one mishears a word, the others build faithfully on the error, because each function trusts the input it is handed. Remember that when a system fails in a way that seems stupid: often one link guessed wrong early and every later link did its job perfectly on a broken input. Chains also explain why these systems can be repaired in pieces. A builder can retrain one function in the chain, the one that sorts, say, and leave the others untouched, the way you would replace one worker on a line without retraining the whole crew. When a vendor announces that their system is new and improved, the fog-clearing question is: which function in the chain changed, and what was it retrained on? Sometimes the answer is one small link. Sometimes the answer is a poem. You now know which answer is which.

And let me deepen the memory picture too, with the contrast that finally made it click for a student of mine some years ago. Compare a photograph album with a pair of hands. The album stores: every picture is in there, findable, pointable, and if a picture was never taken, the album has nothing to say about that day. The hands embody: no job is stored in them, yet everything about how they move was written by the jobs, and they can handle a job they have never done before by drawing on the shape of all the jobs they have. A model is hands, not album. I keep returning to this because nearly every wrong expectation I correct in this field is an album expectation brought to a hands system. People expect recall and get judgement. They expect evidence and get conclusions. They expect the system to know what it has seen and admit what it has not, and the system can do neither, because nothing in it is stored as a seen thing. Expect hands, and the behaviour makes sense: brilliant on familiar ground, inventive on nearby ground, quietly unreliable on ground its experience never touched, and never, ever able to show you the album. There is no album. There was never an album. There are only the settings, shaped by the weather of the examples.

One more walk through the title, then, with both halves lit. A function with a memory of examples. The function half tells you the mechanics: inputs in, outputs out, the settings deciding. The memory half tells you the biography: those settings carry the shape of every example the function was trained on, the way your own judgement carries the shape of every client you have ever served. And the two halves together tell you the limit: the function can only output what its memory of examples has prepared it to output. It has no other resources. It cannot consult the world, it cannot reason its way back to first principles, it cannot notice that today is a strange day unlike its examples, unless strangeness itself was in the examples with labels attached. Everything a model does, it does out of its past. That is why the past you feed a system, if you ever feed one, is the most consequential decision in the whole arrangement, and why lesson two's warning about garbage will echo through this entire department. The model is its training, remembered as shape. There is nothing else in the box. I keep saying there is nothing else in the box, and I will keep saying it, because the day that sentence feels obvious to you is the day the fog has fully left the room.

Let me also say a word about size, since model sizes are quoted at buyers like engine sizes at car lots, and the comparison deserves an honest frame. A larger model, more parameters, more examples, longer training, can hold finer distinctions and more of them, the way a bigger workshop with more specialised tools can take on finer work. That is real, and for some jobs it is decisive. But size is not a synonym for fit. A vast model trained on the general internet knows the general shape of business writing; a small model trained on your two years of sent emails knows you. For the job of sounding like you, the small one can win, and it will cost less, run faster, and fail in ways you can predict. The fog move is to sell size as quality itself. The clear move is to ask: fit for which job, trained on whose examples, at what cost per use. A model is a function with a memory of examples, and the first question about any memory is never how big it is. It is what it is a memory of.

One last illustration, and I choose it from the biggest member of the family, because if the definition holds for the biggest member it holds for all of them. Consider the chatbot, the system that drafts, answers, summarises, and converses, the one most likely to be described to you as a mind. Run it through today's lens and watch what survives. Is it a function? Yes, precisely: the conversation so far goes in, and the next words come out, and it does this one stretch of words at a time, each new stretch a fresh run of the same function on a longer input. Are its settings a memory of examples? Yes: it was trained on an enormous body of text, and everything fluent in it, every rhythm of explanation, every shape of an apology or a sales letter or a condolence note, is the shape of that text settled into numbers. Can it show you its evidence? No: it can produce more words, some of which will sound like evidence, and the difference between evidence and words that sound like evidence is a difference you must supply, because the function cannot. Can it generalise? Beautifully, within the world its examples described, and that world is wide, which is why the system feels wide. And can it step outside that world? No more than your hands can perform a job no job prepared them for. That is the largest, most impressive system in the field, described completely by a sentence from a fundamentals course: a function with a memory of examples. If the sentence feels too small for the system, sit with that feeling a moment. The feeling is the fog leaving. The system did not shrink. Your description of it became exact, and exact descriptions always feel smaller than foggy ones, the way a map feels smaller than the mystery of an unmapped coast. The coast is the same size it always was. Now you can sail it.

And a short word about trust, since a definition that cannot guide trust is only a slogan. You now know what kind of trust a model has earned when it has earned any: the trust you give a trained pair of hands. You trust them on the work they were trained on, you watch them on work near the edge of it, and you do not hand them work from a different trade entirely and act surprised. You also know how such trust is checked: not by asking the hands to explain themselves, which they cannot do, but by watching their outputs on cases where you already know the answer. Every serious evaluation of a model works exactly like that, whatever it is called in the brochure: held-back examples, known answers, counted errors. When you test a tool on twenty of your own past enquiries before letting it near a live one, you are not doing a crude version of the science. You are doing the science, at the scale your business needs. The definition told you how. That is what definitions are for.

And if you remember nothing else from today, remember the posture this definition gives you in a negotiation. When someone presents a model to you now, you are no longer looking at a marvel or a threat. You are looking at a function, and you can ask function questions: what goes in, what comes out, what was it trained on, where does its memory of examples run out. Four questions, all of them answerable by an honest seller, none of them answerable by fog. That is the practical cash value of a definition, and it compounds across every purchase, every hire, and every headline from here on.

Recap, in order. First: a function is a machine for turning inputs into outputs, and a model is a function, nothing more mystical and nothing less useful. Second: what makes a model different from a designed function is that its behaviour was grown, not written: its parameters were adjusted over examples until its guesses became good. Third: its memory of examples is embodied, not stored: the examples survive as the shape of the settings, the way a riverbed remembers floods and hands remember work. Fourth: because the memory is shape rather than items, a model can generalise to cases it has never seen, and for the same reason it cannot show you its evidence or hand you a receipt. Fifth: this class's set problem stands on six bones — function, inputs, outputs, parameters, examples, predictions — plus one honest example from your own life where prediction, not understanding, was enough. Sixth: keep the layers apart — model, product, and machine are three different things, and judging them separately is what clear thinking looks like in this field.

What comes next is the questions on this lesson, with the full text open beside you, as always; going back to read is learning, not cheating, and the set problem's shape is worth a second look before you answer. When you are done, lesson four takes the output side of everything we have built and slows down on it: prediction as the core act, the one move every member of the family is making, in the workshop and in the wild. You came in today with ingredients. You leave with the thing itself. That was the whole point of lesson three.
