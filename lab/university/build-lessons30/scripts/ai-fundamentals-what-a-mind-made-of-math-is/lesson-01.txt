# What 'artificial intelligence' actually names

Come in. I am Professor David Kim, and this is AI Fundamentals. This is lesson one, and its title is a question hiding inside a phrase: what does artificial intelligence actually name? That is what we are going to settle today, slowly and completely, because almost everything confusing about this subject comes from people using those two words without ever saying what they point at. By the end of this lesson you will be able to point at the thing yourself. You will know what the words name, what they do not name, and why the difference matters to you in your actual working life. And it does matter to you. You run a marketing company. Every week somebody tries to sell you a tool, pitch you a service, or scare you with a headline, and all three of those people are counting on the fog around these two words. Fog is profitable for the person selling and expensive for the person buying. So in my room we clear fog first. That is the whole job of lesson one.

Let me give you the whole lesson in one sentence right at the start, the way I like to work, and then we will spend our time together earning it. Here is the sentence. Artificial intelligence is not magic, and it is not a mind in a box. It is machinery, mathematics running at enormous scale on data. Hold onto that. Everything today is an unpacking of that one sentence, word by word, until you can say it yourself and mean every part of it.

Start with the word artificial, because it is the honest word in the pair and nobody argues about it. Artificial means made by people. A wooden chair is artificial. A spreadsheet is artificial. Nobody hears the word artificial and expects the chair to have grown in a forest. So far, so plain. The trouble lives entirely in the second word, intelligence, because intelligence is a word we normally use for people and animals, for minds that understand things. When you put the two words together, the phrase starts whispering a promise it never actually made. It whispers that inside the machine there is something like a mind, something that understands the way you understand, something that knows what it is doing and why. That whisper is not in the machinery. That whisper is in the words. And a great deal of money and a great deal of fear both live inside that whisper.

So here is the first clearing. When engineers and researchers say artificial intelligence, the thing they are actually naming is a family of computer programs that do tasks by finding patterns in examples, instead of by following a list of rules a person wrote out by hand. That is the real definition, and I want you to hear how unglamorous it is, because unglamorous is what true usually sounds like. A family of programs. Tasks done by finding patterns in examples. No mind promised. No understanding promised. Just programs, patterns, and examples.

Let me make that contrast concrete, because the contrast is where the understanding lives. Think about the older kind of program, the kind the world ran on for decades, and still runs on in many places. In that older kind, a person sits down and writes rules. If this happens, do that. If the customer owes money and the date is past the fifteenth, send the reminder. If the word invoice appears in the subject line, file the message under billing. Every behaviour in the program exists because a human being thought of it in advance and wrote it down as an instruction. That kind of program is powerful, and it is honest in a particular way: it can never surprise you with anything its writer did not, in some sense, put there. But it has a hard limit. It can only handle the cases somebody imagined ahead of time. The moment the world shows it a case nobody wrote a rule for, it has nothing to say.

Now think about your inbox, and the junk folder inside it, because that is where most people met the newer kind of program without ever being introduced. Nobody on earth could write rules that catch junk mail. The people sending it change their wording every day. They misspell words on purpose. They write like a bank one morning and like a long lost cousin the next. A rule writer would be chasing them forever, always one step behind. So the spam filter was not built from rules. It was built from examples. It was shown enormous numbers of messages, some labelled junk and some labelled real, and it found the patterns that tend to separate the two. Nobody told it which patterns. Nobody wrote down that a message shouting about a prize in capital letters is suspicious. It found that shape in the examples, along with thousands of other shapes no person ever named. That is the newer kind of program. That is the kind this whole class is about. The rules were not written. The rules, if you can even call them that, were found, by the machine, in the examples.

So if I had to name the thing freshly, with no history attached, I would not call it artificial intelligence at all. I would call it pattern machinery, or learning from examples. But names are not chosen fresh. Names have history, and this name has about seventy years of it. The phrase artificial intelligence was put forward in the nineteen fifties, when the field was young and its founders were reaching, the way young fields do, for the biggest word they could find. They were asking a real and interesting question, which is whether a machine could ever do the things we use the word thinking for. That question is still interesting. But the name outran the work, and it has been outrunning the work ever since. When you hear the phrase, I want you to perform a small translation in your head, every time, until it becomes habit. Translate artificial intelligence into this: programs that learn patterns from examples. The sentence gets less exciting. It also gets true. And true is what lets you make good decisions with it.

Now let me walk you around the family, because it is a family, and the members look very different from one another, and part of the fog is treating them as one creature. Member one: the classifiers, the sorters. Your spam filter is one. A program that looks at a photo and sorts it into faces or no faces is one. A program that reads a customer review and sorts it into happy or unhappy is one. Sorting things into labelled piles, that is the job. Member two: the predictors, the guessers of the next thing. The program that finishes your sentence when you type a text message is one. The program that guesses which of your past clients is most likely to need you again this quarter is one. Member three: the generators, the makers. These produce new text, new images, new sound, built out of the patterns in the examples they were shown. The chatbot that drafts a first version of an advertisement for you is one of these. Member four: the recommenders. The program that decides which post, which video, which product to put in front of a person next. If you advertise on social platforms, you pay money to one of these every time you run a campaign, whether anyone told you so or not. Four members, one family, and the family resemblance is always the same: none of them was handed rules for its job. All of them were shown examples until their guesses became good.

Notice what is not on that list. There is no member called the understander. There is no member called the knower. Every member is doing some version of the same act, which is taking something in and producing a guess coming out, a sorting, a prediction, a draft, a recommendation. Later in this course, in lesson four, we will slow down on that act and give it its proper name, prediction, and we will see that it really is the core act underneath everything. For today, just notice the shape. In, and out. Something goes in, a guess comes out. The guess is often very good. Good enough to be genuinely useful, good enough to build a business service on, good enough that I would tell you to take it seriously. But the shape of the act never changes, and the shape is not the shape of a mind thinking. It is the shape of a very well trained guess.

I want to spend a few minutes on the two directions of fog, because I refuse mystique in both directions, and I mean that as a rule of this room. Fog direction one says the machine is magic. This is the fog of the salesman and the breathless headline. In this fog, the program understands your customers better than you do, it knows things, it creates like an artist creates, it is basically a mind and maybe more than a mind. People speak this way because wonder sells. Wonder opens wallets. When a tool is presented as magic, you cannot ask sensible questions about it, like what examples was it trained on, or how often is it wrong, or wrong in which direction. Magic has no error rate. That is exactly why the word is used. Fog direction two says the machine is a menace, a mind waking up, a rival, something that wants things and schemes for them. This fog sells too, fear sells newspapers the way wonder sells software, and it rests on the same mistake as the first fog, just with the feeling reversed. Both fogs assume there is a mind in the box. Both fogs are wrong in the same place. There is machinery in the box. Mathematics, running at enormous scale, on data. A thing can be powerful without being a mind, the way a river is powerful without wanting anything. Keep both fogs out of your head and you will see the actual machine, which is interesting enough on its own, I promise you. It does not need the costume.

Here is a place where this lands in your real week, and I want to use your real week a lot in this class, because ideas that do not survive contact with a Tuesday afternoon are not worth much. Imagine a salesman, a perfectly pleasant fellow, showing you a platform that will, in his words, let artificial intelligence run your advertising. He shows you a dashboard. The numbers on it are green. He says the system knows your audience. Now, with lesson one in your pocket, translate. The system does not know your audience. The system was shown examples, very likely a great many examples of advertisements and what people clicked on, and it found patterns in them, and now it guesses, advertisement by advertisement and person by person, what is likely to get clicked. That guess might be excellent. It might beat your own guessing, because it has seen more examples than you will see in your lifetime, and there is no shame in that, a calculator beats me at arithmetic too. But the moment you translate knows into guesses from patterns in examples, you know what questions to ask. How often is it wrong. What does it do with a kind of customer it has never seen. What happens to my money while it is learning. Those are business questions, and you are good at business questions. The fog was the only thing stopping you from asking them. That is what this lesson is for. Not to make you a programmer. To make you un-foggable.

Let me also say plainly what this machinery is genuinely good for in a small company like yours, because clearing fog cuts both ways, and I do not want you leaving today thinking the answer is that it is all theatre. It is not theatre. A program that sorts your incoming enquiries into urgent and routine, by pattern, can save a person an hour a day. A program that drafts the first version of a routine email, which you then fix in your own voice, can give you back your evenings. A program that looks at two years of your invoices and finds the pattern of which clients pay late, that is real money found by real machinery. The honest sentence is not that these tools are nothing. The honest sentence is that they are something specific: fast, tireless guessers, trained on examples, brilliant at the shape of what they have seen, and with limits we will map carefully in lesson five. Specific is more useful than either magic or menace. Specific is what you can build on.

There is one more distinction I want to plant today, and we will grow it all course long. It is the distinction between prediction and understanding, and it might be the single most valuable idea in this class. Your spam filter predicts, superbly, that a message is junk. It does not understand the message. It does not know what a prize is, or why a stranger would lie to you about one, or what money means. It has patterns where understanding would go. Now here is the part people find surprising: for the job it does, prediction is enough. The filter does not need to understand a word of your email to protect your inbox, and it protects it well. A great deal of useful work in the world is like that. Knowing which customers will probably call again does not require understanding why they call. Knowing which words in an advertisement probably draw clicks does not require understanding longing or humour or trust. Prediction without understanding is not a defect in these systems. It is their nature, and on the right jobs it is plenty. The trouble only starts when we hand them a job where understanding was the thing we actually needed, and we did not notice the swap, because the prediction was so fluent we mistook it for the other thing. Hold that thought. It comes back in lesson three, in lesson five, and honestly in every working decision you will make with these tools for the rest of your life.

Now, before the recap, our guided moment, because in this class we do not just listen, we practise the move while it is fresh. Here is a small exercise I want you to do with me, right now, in your head or out loud if nobody is listening. I am going to give you three sentences of the kind you will meet in the wild, and your job is to translate each one into the plain machinery underneath. Sentence one: the platform's artificial intelligence understands your brand. Translate it. The platform's program was shown examples of your material and of material like it, and it guesses, from the patterns, what fits your brand. Understands became was shown examples and guesses. Sentence two: our system knows the perfect moment to post. Translate. The system was shown examples of posts and their results, and it predicts the posting times that have tended to do well. Knows became predicts from examples. Sentence three, and this one is my favourite because it sounds so warm: the chatbot cares about your customers. Translate. The chatbot produces responses whose patterns resemble caring responses, because caring responses were in its examples. Cares became produces patterns that resemble. Now notice how you feel after translating. The tools did not shrink. A good predictor of posting times is still worth money. But the fog is gone, and what is left is a thing you can evaluate, price, and supervise. Do this translation every time the words understand, know, or care are used about a program, and you will be ahead of most of the conversation in this country. That is the try-it for today, and I mean for you to keep it. It is a tool, not a test.

Before we recap, I want to walk the family once more, slowly, through a single ordinary day in your business, because a day is the right size for seeing how much of this machinery you already use without anyone naming it for you. Your day starts with email, and the sorting has already happened before you woke up: junk in one folder, real enquiries in your inbox, and that sorting was pattern machinery, member one of the family, working all night without a single written rule about what junk looks like this week. Then you drive to meet a client, and the map on your phone tells you the trip will take twenty-three minutes, and that number is member two, a predictor, guessing from patterns in millions of past trips, at this hour, on this day, on these roads. It does not understand traffic. It has never sat in a jam feeling its morning slip away. It predicts, and its prediction is good enough that you plan your departure around it, and you are right to. Mid-morning, you dictate a note into your phone and it turns your voice into text. That is pattern machinery again, matching the shapes of sound to the shapes of words, and when it gets a word wrong it gets it wrong in a revealing way: it guesses a word that sounds right and fits the pattern, because guessing is the only move it has. In the afternoon you write a post for your company page, and the platform you post it on decides, person by person, who sees it. That decision is member four, the recommender, and it is worth pausing on, because this one touches your livelihood directly. The recommender was shown examples of what each person lingered on, clicked on, shared, or scrolled past, and it predicts, for every post, who will probably linger. Your post lives or dies by a prediction about attention. You do not have to like that. But you should know it is a prediction, made by machinery, from examples, because a person who knows that writes differently, tests differently, and spends differently from a person who thinks a mind somewhere is judging their work. And in the evening, perhaps, you open a chatbot and ask it to draft a follow-up message to a client who went quiet. That is member three, the maker, producing new text from the patterns of a great many examples of how people write follow-up messages. Four members, one day, and not one of them understood you, your business, or your client. Every one of them predicted. And your day ran better because of it. That is the honest picture, and it is not a small picture. It is just not a picture of a mind.

One more idea belongs in lesson one, and it is the idea that will keep you calm when the headlines get loud, so I want it planted today. It is the difference between the narrow and the general. Every member of the family I have described is narrow. Each one does its own job and no other. Your spam filter is magnificent at spam and would be helpless at driving directions. The map's predictor knows nothing about email. The chatbot that drafts your message cannot sort your photographs. Each system is a single, narrow skill, trained on examples of that skill. When people imagine a mind in the box, what they are imagining is something general, one thing that can do all of it, the way you can sort mail and drive and write and also learn something entirely new by Friday. Nothing in the family is like that. Bundles of narrow systems can be arranged together so the bundle looks broad from the outside, and later classes in this department will show you exactly how that arranging is done, and it is clever, genuinely clever. But clever arranging is not a mind arriving. It is narrow pieces, side by side, each still guessing at its own job. When a headline tells you a system can now do twenty things, the translation is that twenty narrow guessers are now sharing one front door. Useful, yes. Worth your attention, yes. A mind, no. Remember the shape: in, and out, one job at a time, however many doors the building has.

And since we are clearing fog, let me clear the fog around one small word you will meet constantly from here on, the word smart, as in smart tools, smart systems, a smart platform. Smart is doing the same work as intelligence in the big phrase. It is a compliment dressed as a description. When a tool is called smart, ask the only question that matters: smart at what, exactly, and shown which examples, and wrong how often. A spam filter is smart at spam. A pricing tool is smart at the prices in its examples. The word smart tries to widen a narrow skill into a general glow, and your job, as the person whose money is on the table, is to narrow it back down. Narrowing words back down to the specific thing they name is, honestly, most of what this entire class is. You are learning a vocabulary, but really you are learning a habit: never let a big word stand where a specific description belongs.

I will add one honest warning before we close the teaching, because a fog-clearer owes you the whole weather, not just the sunny part. The translation habit I am teaching you can be overdone. There is a version of it that curdles into dismissal, where a person waves at every new system and says it is just patterns, just guessing, nothing here matters. That is fog too, the third fog, the fog of the unimpressed, and it will cost you money just as surely as the other two, because while you are waving, a competitor is quietly using a good predictor to answer enquiries in five minutes while yours wait until morning. Just guessing is doing a lot of work in that sentence. A guess trained on millions of examples is not a coin flip. It is the distilled shape of all that experience, available in a second, for pennies. Respect the machinery for exactly what it is, no more and no less, and you will neither overpay for it nor sleep on it. That balance, neither magic nor menace nor shrug, is the professional stance, and it is where I intend to leave you standing at the end of this course.

One last small thing, and then we recap. People sometimes ask me, if it is only machinery, why does talking to it feel so much like talking to someone? That feeling is real, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a shrug. The feeling comes from you, and it is one of the finest things about you. Human beings are built to find minds. We find faces in clouds and intentions in weather. When a program produces fluent sentences, in the right rhythm, answering in context, the part of you that recognises people fires, because fluency is one of the signals it uses. The signal can be present without the mind being present, the way a recording of laughter can make you smile in an empty room. Knowing that does not make you cold. It makes you accurate, and accuracy about what is in front of you is a form of respect, for the machine, for yourself, and for the actual people whose words taught it its patterns. So feel the feeling, and then do the translation. Both. That is the whole discipline in one line.

Let me recap, in order, the way we will always recap. First: artificial intelligence names a family of programs that do tasks by finding patterns in examples, not by following rules a person wrote by hand, and not by thinking the way a mind thinks. Second: the machinery underneath the name is mathematics running at enormous scale on data, and the whole secret fits in one sentence: a model is a function that was shown so many examples that its guesses became good, and training is the showing. Third: the family has recognisable members, sorters, predictors, makers, and recommenders, and every one of them shares the same shape, something goes in and a guess comes out. Fourth: there are two fogs, magic and menace, they both assume a mind in the box, and in this room we refuse both. Fifth: prediction is not understanding, prediction is often enough, and knowing the difference between the two is the whole first lesson and half of every lesson after it.

What comes next is the questions on this lesson, and I want you to take them seriously and take them easy at the same time. They are there to fix the ideas in place, not to catch you out. The full text of this lesson stays open beside the questions, and going back to read is learning, not cheating. I would rather you look something up and understand it than guess and move on carrying the fog with you. When you are done with them, lesson two takes the one sentence from today and pulls it apart into its three ingredients, data, parameters, and training, and we count them on our fingers until they are yours. Come back for that. You did well today. The fog is thinner than it was an hour ago, and that was the entire point.
