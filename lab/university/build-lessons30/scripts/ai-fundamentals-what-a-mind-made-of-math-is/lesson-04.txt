# Prediction as the core act

Come in. I am Professor David Kim, and this is AI Fundamentals, lesson four. We have built the machine piece by piece: the name cleared in lesson one, the three ingredients counted in lesson two, the model itself defined in lesson three as a function with a memory of examples. Today we watch the machine do its one and only trick, and we give that trick its proper name. The name is prediction, and my claim for this lesson, which I will spend our time together proving, is that prediction is the core act: the single move underneath every member of the family, from the spam filter to the chatbot to the thing that decides who sees your advertisements. Two acts, and the whole class turns on keeping them apart. Training is the showing: before the system ever meets you, its settings are adjusted over millions of examples. Prediction is the act: afterwards, one new case at a time, the system produces its guess. Training happens in the workshop. Prediction happens in the wild. By the end of this lesson you will be able to take any AI system doing anything at all and find the prediction inside it, the way you can find the engine inside any car once someone has shown you what an engine looks like. That skill is worth money to you, because a person who can find the prediction can ask how good the prediction is, and that question, asked calmly and repeatedly, is the most powerful question a buyer owns.

Let us define prediction the way this room defines everything: plainly, and smaller than the fog wants it. A prediction is a guess about something not yet known, produced from patterns in things already known. Notice the parts. There is a guess, which means it can be wrong, and any system whose output cannot be wrong is not predicting, it is looking something up. There is something not yet known: the future, most obviously, but also the present you have not checked and the past nobody recorded. Your spam filter predicts something about the present: this message, which no human has judged yet, belongs in the junk folder. That is a prediction even though nothing about it is in the future, because the answer is unknown at the moment the guess is made and the guess is made from patterns, not from a lookup. And there is the basis: patterns in things already known, the examples, the memory of examples from lesson three. A prediction is the memory of examples, pointed at a new case. That is the whole act. Everything else is costume.

Now let me prove the claim by walking the family, the way we did in lesson one, and finding the prediction inside each member with the lights on. The sorter first, because it hides the prediction best. Sorting feels like knowing: the filter does not say this message might be junk, it simply moves it, decisively, the way a clerk files a letter. But the decisiveness is a costume too. Underneath, the filter predicted: shown this message, the pattern of its settings says junk is the likely verdict, likely enough to act on. Sorting is prediction with the uncertainty trimmed off for display, and once you see that, you will ask the sorter the predictor's question: how often is the sort wrong, and wrong in which direction, because a real enquiry filed as junk costs you a client and a junk message filed as real costs you a glance, and those are not the same cost, and a good buyer weighs them differently. The predictor member wears the act openly: the map predicting your arrival, the tool predicting which client will need you again. Nothing to uncover there, except to notice that it is the same act as the sorter's, with the uncertainty left visible. The recommender is a predictor working a crowd: for each person and each post, it predicts attention, will this one linger, and it shows the post where the predicted lingering is highest. Your reach, as a marketer, is a field of other people's predictions about attention, and I do not think that sentence can be repeated too often in a marketing company. And the maker, the generator, the chatbot, the member that feels least like a guesser: it is a predictor running in a loop. It predicts the next word. Then it predicts the next word after that, given the words so far, including its own. Then the next. A paragraph is hundreds of predictions in a row, each one small, each one shaped by the memory of examples, and the fluency that feels like thought is what you get when the small predictions are very good and very fast. The image makers work the same way in their own medium: prediction, in strokes and patches, all the way down. Four members, one act. I told you in lesson one that the shape never changes: something goes in, a guess comes out. Today the guess has its name. It is a prediction, every time, in every member, without exception.

There is a technical word for the act that you will meet in documentation and in sales material, and I want you to own it rather than be impressed by it: inference. When engineers say a model is running inference, they mean the model is being used: a new case is going through the frozen settings and a prediction is coming out. Inference is prediction, wearing its work clothes. The pair is worth fixing side by side: training is the showing, inference is the using. Training happens in the workshop, before the system meets you, at enormous expense, over millions of examples. Inference happens in the wild, after, cheaply, one case at a time, millions of times a day across the world. When a vendor's page boasts about their inference speed or their inference cost, they are talking about how fast and how cheaply the frozen model produces predictions, and now the boast is legible to you as a plain business fact rather than a spell. Words stop being fog the moment you can translate them on sight, and inference is one of the words this course exists to defog.

Now, the timeline, because keeping the two acts apart in time is the practical skill inside this lesson. Lay it on a timeline. On the left, the long before: millions of examples, settings adjusted again and again, the workshop lit for weeks. That is training. On the right, the quick after: one fresh case arrives, and out comes a guess, in a fraction of a second, for a fraction of a cent. That is prediction. Before and after; workshop and wild. The reason I press this so hard is that the two acts are constantly being blurred in conversation, usually in one specific direction: the system is described as learning from you while you use it. For the overwhelming majority of systems you will ever touch, that is not happening. Your corrections, your clever prompts, your frustrated rephrasings: they shape the current conversation, because the conversation is part of the input, but they do not retrain the model. The settings are frozen. The workshop is elsewhere and elsewhen. What feels like the system learning you is the system predicting with more of your words in its input, which is a real and useful thing, and a different thing. Why does the difference matter to a business owner? Because it tells you where improvement comes from. If a tool's predictions about your clients are poor, the fix is not to be patient with it while it learns you. The fix is better examples in its next training, or a different tool, or your own judgement on top. Patience is a virtue in many places. Waiting for a frozen function to thaw itself is not one of them.

Let us talk about how good a prediction is, because prediction has a beautiful property that understanding does not: it can be graded. A prediction is a guess about something that will become known, or can be checked, and when it becomes known you can count. The filter predicted junk; was it junk? The tool predicted this client would return; did she? Count the hits, count the misses, and you have the beginning of knowledge about a system, real knowledge, the kind you can take to a bank. This is why I keep steering you toward prediction language and away from understanding language. Understanding cannot be graded. There is no scoreboard for whether a chatbot truly grasps your brand. But its predictions can be graded, every one of them, and a vendor who believes in his system will help you grade it, and a vendor who steers you back to words like understands and knows is steering you away from the scoreboard. Insist on the scoreboard. Keep your own, in a notebook if you must: what the tool predicted, what actually happened, twenty cases, fifty. You will learn more about any system from fifty graded predictions than from fifty pages of its marketing, and you will learn it in the only currency that matters, which is what happens next in your actual business.

One refinement belongs here, because predictions are not all built the same and the difference shows up in how you should use them. Some predictions come as a verdict: junk, not junk; call her, do not call her. Others come as a likelihood: a seventy percent chance this enquiry becomes a client, a thirty percent chance of rain on the day of the shoot. The likelihood kind is more honest and more useful, because it tells you how to bet your time. A seventy percent enquiry deserves your first call of the morning. A thirty percent enquiry deserves a call too, perhaps, but not the first one, and not at the price of the seventy. Trouble begins when a likelihood is dressed as a verdict, when a tool shows you only its top guess and hides how close the second guess was. Two enquiries can both be labelled promising while one sits at ninety percent and the other at fifty-one, and they should not be worked the same way. So here is a buyer's habit to take from this lesson: whenever a tool gives you a verdict, ask what the likelihood was, and whenever it gives you a likelihood, ask what it was graded against. The systems that can answer both questions are the systems built by people who grade themselves. The others are asking you to grade them as magic, and we settled what to do with magic in lesson one.

Now the guided try-it, because this act of finding the prediction should be practised while I am here to practise it with you. I will name three things tools do in a marketing week, and for each one I want you to say, in a full sentence, what is being predicted, from what patterns, about what unknown. First: the tool drafts your advertisement. Say it with me. The system predicts, word by word, the words most likely to follow the words so far, from patterns in an enormous memory of examples of writing, about the unknown of what text should come next. The draft is a prediction of text. Second: the tool tells you Tuesday at ten is the best time to post. The system predicts the attention your post will receive at each possible time, from patterns in past posts and their results, about the unknown of how this post will do. The schedule is a prediction of attention. Third, and this one is the quietest: the tool summarises a long email thread for you. The system predicts the words of a shorter text that patterns say belong with threads like this one, about the unknown of what a good summary here would say. Even summarising, which feels like pure understanding, is prediction underneath: a guess at the words, checkable against the thread, gradable by you in ten seconds. Run that sentence shape over anything a tool does this week: the system predicts this, from patterns in that, about this unknown. When you can fill in all three blanks, the tool has no fog left to hide in. When you cannot fill in the middle blank, the patterns, you have found the exact question to ask its seller.

Before the recap, let me show you grading done once, in full, on numbers small enough to hold in your head, because I want the scoreboard to be a thing you have seen and not just a metaphor I praise. Imagine you let a tool rank your enquiries for one month, and you write down its top prediction each time: this one will become a client. Say it makes that call on twenty enquiries. The month ends. You count. Fourteen of the twenty became clients. Six did not. Now you know something no brochure could tell you: on your enquiries, in your town, in your trade, this tool's top prediction was right fourteen times out of twenty. And the counting teaches you more the longer you keep it, because you can start asking the second-order questions. Of the six misses, were they all one kind of enquiry, say the ones from outside your area? Then the tool has a blind spot with an address, and you can compensate for it yourself, which is what a good owner does with any employee's weakness. Of the clients you actually won that month, how many did the tool rank low? That is the miss nobody notices, the false alarm's quiet twin, and it matters because a tool that only confirms the obvious is furniture, not help. Notice what made all of this possible: the predictions were written down before the outcomes were known. A prediction remembered afterwards is not a prediction; it is a story, and stories always grade themselves kindly. Write it down, date it, count it later. That humble discipline, applied to machines, is the entire scientific method of this field wearing work boots, and it is available to you tonight with a notebook and a pencil.

Why does prediction work at all? It is worth one honest minute, because the answer explains both the power you have seen and the brittleness waiting in lesson five. Prediction works because the world has habits. People who ask about price in the first line usually keep asking about price. Messages shaped like past junk are usually junk. Attention gathers where attention gathered before. The patterns in the examples are not arbitrary; they are the fingerprints of those habits, and a model is, in the end, a very large collection of the world's habits, distilled into settings. But habits are not laws. Habits shift: a competitor opens across town, a platform changes what it rewards, a phrase that worked all spring tires out by autumn, and the world's new behaviour no longer matches the habits in the frozen settings. The model cannot notice. Noticing was never installed. It will keep predicting the old world with undiminished confidence, and the scoreboard, your scoreboard, is the only instrument that will register the change. This is why grading is not a one-time inspection you perform at purchase. It is a habit of your own, the owner's habit, matching the machine's habits, and the day your counts start slipping is the day you have learned something worth real money: the world moved, and the frozen function did not.

One more subtlety, and it is the subtlest thing in this lesson, so take it slowly. Some predictions change the thing they predict. When the recommender predicts you will linger on a certain kind of post and therefore shows you more of that kind, your lingering is partly the prediction's own doing: the guess helped make itself true. When a tool predicts an enquiry is promising and you therefore answer it within the hour, warmly, with your best attention, while the low-ranked enquiry waits two days for a cooler reply, the outcomes at month's end are partly your doing, downstream of the ranking. The scoreboard still matters, more than ever, but read it with this in mind: a prediction handed to a person who acts on it is not a weather forecast. It is more like a coach's call, entangled with the playing of the game. This is not a reason to distrust the act. It is a reason to keep your own judgement in the loop, visibly, especially where your actions are the mechanism by which predictions come true or false. The owner who knows he is part of the system grades more honestly than the owner who thinks he is only watching it.

And a word about causation, briefly, because prediction's oldest trap lives there. A model finds that enquiries mentioning a deadline become clients. It does not find, and cannot find, whether the deadline causes the seriousness, or whether serious people simply mention deadlines, or whether some third thing produces both. For prediction, the difference does not matter: the pattern pays either way. But the moment you act on a pattern as if it were a cause, the difference can cost you. If you instructed every client to mention a deadline, you would not manufacture seriousness; you would manufacture deadlines. Patterns tell you what travels together. They are silent about what produces what. Keep prediction for prediction's jobs — ranking, sorting, drafting, timing — and bring causes in only where you have evidence of the slower, human kind. That sentence will save you from the most respectable-looking mistake in the whole field, which is steering a business by correlations as if they were levers.

Let me also place prediction beside its neighbours, because three words get traded for one another in sales talk and they are not the same. A lookup finds a stored answer: your calendar lookup does not predict your Thursday appointment, it retrieves it, and it is never wrong in the prediction way, only out of date. A calculation derives an answer from rules: the invoice total is calculated, and if the rules and the inputs are right, the total is right with a certainty no prediction ever earns. A prediction guesses an unknown from patterns, and it carries its error rate with it like a shadow, always, even when the shadow is small. Systems get sold by borrowing the dignity of the neighbours: the prediction is presented with the confidence of a calculation, or the finality of a lookup. Your defence is classification. When a tool speaks, ask which of the three it just did. If it predicted, where is the error rate? If it calculated, what were the rules? If it looked something up, how fresh is the store? Three kinds of answer, three kinds of trust, and a great deal of expensive confusion dissolves the moment you refuse to let one kind wear another's clothes.

And since this lesson is the heart of the course, I will say its heart out loud once more, in the plainest form I own. You do not need these systems to be minds. You need them to be right often enough, on the cases you feed them, at a cost below the value of being right. Prediction is the act that can be measured against that standard, which is why this course keeps returning to it the way a compass needle returns north. Understanding is a beautiful word and a genuine mystery, and it is somebody else's course. This one is about machinery that guesses from examples, and the guessing, graded honestly, is more than enough to build on. Hold the standard — right often enough, on your cases, at the right cost — and every tool you meet from now on walks up to the same measuring wall.

One more scene from your week, to fix the timeline in muscle memory, and then we recap. Picture the morning you first use a new drafting tool. You type a client's situation, and out comes a reply in something like your voice. It feels, irresistibly, as though the tool has come to know you in the last ten minutes. Run the timeline over that feeling. The knowing, such as it is, happened months ago and miles away, in the workshop, when the settings were adjusted over millions of examples of writing, very few of them yours. This morning's event is on the right side of the timeline: one fresh case, your words, run through frozen settings, one prediction out. The feeling of being known is the prediction's fluency wearing the costume of acquaintance. None of this makes the draft worse. It may be an excellent draft. But if you believe the tool has learned you this morning, you will make two mistakes: you will trust tomorrow's draft more than today's evidence warrants, because surely it knows you better now, and you will blame yourself when it errs, as though you had taught it badly. Both mistakes dissolve under the timeline. The settings were frozen before you arrived. Your job is not to teach the tool. Your job is to grade its predictions and to bring your own knowledge, the real, accumulated, understanding kind, to the cases where the pattern runs out. That division of labour, machine predicts and owner judges, is the working heart of every good arrangement I have seen between a business and these systems, and it is the arrangement this whole course is quietly training you to run.

And a final distinction for the road, small in words and large in consequence: the difference between a prediction and a promise. A promise binds the speaker. When you tell a client the work will be done by Friday, you have not merely guessed about Friday; you have committed yourself to making Friday true, and your honour is the mechanism. A model's prediction binds nothing and commits nothing. It is a reading of patterns, offered without stake. The trouble, and I have watched this trouble cost real money, is that fluent predictions are easily heard as promises, by customers, by staff, and by owners at the end of a long day. A delivery date a tool suggested, repeated to a client, becomes the client's expectation and your obligation, while the tool's part in it evaporates. So handle predictions the way a careful person handles a talented but unaccountable adviser's tips: write them down as predictions, grade them as predictions, and convert them into promises only through your own judgement, at the moment you choose, knowing the obligation is yours alone. The machine guesses. The owner promises. Keeping those two verbs with their rightful owners is most of professional maturity in the age of this machinery, and you are nearly trained in it already.

If you take one habit from this lesson into the rest of your education, let it be the habit of writing predictions down before outcomes arrive, for machines and for people alike, including yourself. Your own gut predictions about clients, campaigns, and hires are also guesses from patterns, the patterns of your experience, and they deserve the same honest grading you now give the machine's. Owners who grade their own calls get sharper. Owners who remember their calls kindly stay exactly as sharp as they were. The scoreboard does not care whose prediction it grades, and that impartiality is its kindness.

Carry the timeline in your pocket this week and lay every claim you hear across it, left side or right side, workshop or wild, and you will find that half the confusion in any conversation about this technology is simply the two acts being traded mid-sentence by people who never separated them.

Recap, in order. First: prediction is the core act, a guess about something not yet known, produced from patterns in things already known, and every member of the family, sorter, predictor, recommender, and maker, is performing it. Second: sorting is prediction with the uncertainty trimmed off, and making is prediction running in a loop, one next word at a time. Third: inference is the engineer's word for the same act, the using of the frozen model, as training is the showing that froze it. Fourth: the two acts live on a timeline, training in the workshop, long and expensive, before; prediction in the wild, quick and cheap, after, and most systems do not learn from you while you use them. Fifth: predictions can be graded and understandings cannot, so insist on the scoreboard, count hits and misses on your own cases, and prefer likelihoods you can bet your morning on over verdicts that hide how close the call was.

What comes next is the questions on this lesson, and as always the full text stays open beside them; going back to read is learning, not cheating, and the sentence shape from our try-it is worth keeping in front of you while you answer. When you are done, lesson five takes the graded scoreboard seriously and asks the honest question: where is all this prediction strong, where is it brittle, and why are both true of the same machine at the same time. You now know the one act. Next we map where the act holds and where it cracks.
