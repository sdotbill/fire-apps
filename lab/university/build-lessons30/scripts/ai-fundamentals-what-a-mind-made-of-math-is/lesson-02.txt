# Data, parameters, and training: the three ingredients

Come in. I am Professor David Kim, and this is AI Fundamentals, lesson two. Last time we cleared the fog around the name itself, and we landed on one sentence: a model is a function that was shown so many examples that its guesses became good, and training is the showing. Today we take that sentence apart, piece by piece, because inside it there are exactly three ingredients, and every system in this department, every tool anyone will ever try to sell you, is built from those three and nothing else. The three ingredients are data, parameters, and training. By the end of this lesson you will be able to define each one in plain words, tell them apart without hesitating, and point at each one inside any real system you meet. That sounds like a small thing. It is not a small thing. Most of the confident nonsense spoken about this technology comes from people mixing these three up, usually on purpose, because a customer who cannot tell the ingredients apart cannot ask what is in the food. You will be able to ask. That is why this lesson matters, and it matters in your business this quarter, not in some abstract future.

We will take the ingredients in the order the course names them. First, data: the examples the system learns from. Second, parameters: the internal settings inside the system, the knobs. Third, training: the showing, the long process of adjusting those settings against the examples until the guesses come out right. Count them on your fingers with me, the way we will do it all course. One: what it learns from, the examples, the data. Two: what gets adjusted inside it, the settings, the parameters. Three: the adjusting itself, the showing, the training. From, adjusted, adjusting. Say those three words and you have the skeleton of every system we will ever discuss. Now let us put meat on each bone, one at a time, slowly.

Data first, because everything starts there and most failures end there. Data is the examples. That is the whole definition, and I want to resist every urge to make it fancier. When we say a system learned from data, we mean it was shown examples: examples of emails labelled junk or not junk, examples of photographs labelled with what is in them, examples of questions paired with good answers, examples of advertisements paired with what people did when they saw them. An example is one case, with, very often, a label attached, and the label is the part a human being usually supplied. Somebody, somewhere, said this one is junk, this one is real, this photograph contains a dog, this answer was helpful, this one was not. Underneath every impressive system there is a mountain of those small human judgements, and I want you to remember that when the marketing gets mystical. The mountain was built by people, one label at a time.

Now, the size of the mountain matters, and so does its quality, and the two are not the same thing, and confusing them is expensive. A system shown a million examples has usually found finer patterns than a system shown a thousand, the way a salesman who has made a million calls reads a room faster than one who has made a thousand. Scale is real. But quality is the quiet governor of the whole enterprise. If the examples are wrong, the patterns will be wrong, faithfully, at scale, with total confidence. There is an old saying in computing, older than this field, and it has never stopped being true: garbage in, garbage out. Show a system a mountain of mislabelled email and it will learn, beautifully and precisely, the wrong lesson. Show a pricing tool only your cheapest jobs and it will learn that your work is cheap. The machinery does not know the examples are bad. It cannot know. Judging the examples is our job, the human job, and it stays our job no matter how large the system gets. When a vendor tells you their system learned from billions of examples, the fog-clearing question is never how many. It is: whose examples, labelled by whom, checked how. Billions of the wrong examples is just a faster way to be wrong.

Let me ground this in your own company, because you are sitting on more data than you think, and seeing it changes how you hear every pitch from now on. Your sent emails are data: thousands of examples of how you write to clients, what you promise, how you close. Your invoices are data: examples of jobs, prices, and who paid on time and who needed chasing. Your reviews are data: examples of what delighted people and what annoyed them, in their own words. Your past advertisements, with their results attached, are data of the most valuable kind, because each one is an example with a label money itself supplied: this wording, this picture, this many calls. If you ever pay for a tool that claims it will learn your business, this is what it would have to learn from, and now you can ask the only question that matters: will it learn from my examples, or from everybody's examples averaged together? Both can be useful. They are not the same product, and they should not cost the same money. A system trained on the whole internet knows how people write in general. A system trained on your sent folder knows how you write. When you can say that sentence to a salesman, in those words, the fog in the room gets noticeably thinner.

There is one more thing about data to fix in place today, because it will matter in lesson five when we talk about brittleness. The examples a system was shown draw a boundary around what it can do, and the system does not know where the boundary is. Inside the boundary, where the examples were thick, its guesses are usually good. Outside it, where the examples were thin or absent, its guesses can be wild, and, this is the important part, they arrive in exactly the same confident shape as the good ones. A system shown only summer enquiries will guess strangely about the week before Christmas, and it will not warn you. It cannot warn you. It has no examples of its own ignorance. Hold that thought; we will pick it up again when we map the brittleness properly. For now, the sentence to keep is this: data is the examples, the examples draw the map, and the system has never seen the territory off the edge of its own map.

Parameters second. Parameters are the internal settings inside the system, the knobs. I like the word knobs, and I will use it all course, because it keeps the idea at the right size in your head. Picture a mixing desk in a recording studio, the long board covered in sliders and dials. Each dial is set somewhere. The combination of all those settings is what makes the sound come out one way rather than another. A model is like that board, except the board is made of numbers and it is far, far bigger. A modest model might have thousands of parameters. A working model of the kind you meet in products might have millions. The largest language models have hundreds of billions of parameters, each one a single number, a single dial set to a single position. Nobody should be intimidated by that count, and nobody should be hypnotised by it either. A parameter is not a fact the system knows. It is not a memory of a sentence or a photograph. It is one setting, meaningless on its own, that only does its work in combination with all the others, the way one dial on the mixing board means nothing until the music runs through the whole board at once.

This is the point where I always stop and clear a particular piece of fog, because it is the most common misunderstanding I meet. People hear that a system has billions of parameters and they picture a library: billions of stored facts, billions of memorised pages. That picture is wrong, and it leads to wrong expectations in both directions. The system has not stored the examples. It has been shaped by the examples. The difference is the difference between a student who memorised the textbook and a student whose judgement was formed by working a thousand problems. The second student cannot recite problem four hundred and twelve. But put a new problem in front of her and her settings, so to speak, produce a good approach. That is what parameters are: formed judgement, stored as numbers, with the original examples nowhere inside. It is also why these systems can surprise you by failing at something that looks like simple recall. You are asking the mixing board to recite. Reciting was never the design. The design is: run the new case through the settings, and let the output be whatever the settings make of it.

And here is the sentence about parameters I most want you to keep, because it is the hinge of the whole lesson. Before training, the parameters are set essentially at random, and the system is useless: shown an email, it guesses junk or not junk no better than a coin. After training, the same parameters, the same dials, sit in different positions, and the system is good. Nothing else about the machine changed. The program that runs it is the same program. The examples are gone home. The only difference between the useless machine and the useful one is the positions of the knobs. So when someone asks what training produces, the honest, complete answer is: a set of numbers. A trained model, the valuable thing, the thing companies guard and sell and argue over, is at bottom a very large list of numbers, arrived at by a very long process of adjustment. That is not a dismissal. A symphony is at bottom a list of notes. But it tells you exactly what kind of thing you are dealing with: not a creature, not a library, a list of settings that makes guesses come out right.

Which brings us to the third ingredient, training itself, and now the first two ingredients click together. Training is the showing: the long process of adjusting the parameters against the data until the guesses come out right. Let me walk the loop slowly, because the loop is simple and everything is in it. Step one: the system is shown one example, say an email, and it runs that email through its current settings and produces a guess: junk, or not junk. Step two: the guess is compared with the label, the human judgement attached to that example. The label is the teacher in the room. Step three: if the guess was wrong, the parameters are nudged, a very small adjustment in the direction that would have made the guess a little less wrong. If the guess was right, the settings are left mostly alone, or nudged a hair toward more of the same. Step four: next example. And then the loop repeats, not a hundred times, not a thousand times, but millions of times, billions of times for the large systems, example after example after example, nudge after nudge after nudge. That is all training is. Guess, compare, nudge, repeat, at a scale no human lifetime could match. The grandeur is in the scale and the patience, not in any mystery inside the loop. A child learning to catch does the same loop: reach, miss, adjust, reach again. The machine does it with numbers, without tiring, without sleeping, and without ever understanding what a ball is. Or an email. Or anything.

Two honest details about training belong in a fundamentals class, and I will not skip them just because they are less shiny than the loop itself. The first is that training is expensive in a way using the system is not. The long adjustment process, millions or billions of examples with the settings nudged after each, takes enormous computing power, warehouses of machines running for weeks for the largest systems. That cost is paid once, up front, by whoever builds the system. Afterwards, each single use, one email sorted, one draft produced, costs a tiny fraction by comparison. Remember this shape, expensive to train, cheap to run, because it explains the economics of the whole industry: why a few companies build the large models, why everybody else rents them, and why the tool you subscribe to for a monthly fee can exist at all. The second detail is that training ends. The adjusting stops. At some point the builders freeze the settings, and the system you meet is that frozen list of numbers, no longer learning from what it sees. Most systems you will ever use do not learn from you as you use them. Your corrections today do not retrain the tool tonight. The tool you meet in six months is a new frozen list, trained again, in the workshop, on new examples, by its builders. In lesson four we will separate these two acts formally, training in the workshop and prediction in the wild, and the separation will do a lot of work for us. Today, just hold the shape: there is a long, expensive before, in which the knobs get set, and a long, cheap after, in which the set knobs get used.

Now the guided moment, because these three words only become yours when your hands have sorted something with them. I will give you three scenes from a working week, and for each one I want you to name which ingredient is in the foreground. Scene one: you spend a Friday exporting two years of client emails and marking the ones that turned into paying jobs, so a tool can learn what a promising enquiry looks like. Which ingredient are you handling? You are handling data. You are choosing and labelling examples. Scene two: a vendor's engineer tells you their new version has far more parameters than the old one and should therefore judge tone more finely. Which ingredient is she describing? Parameters, the settings, the size of the mixing board. More dials can hold finer distinctions, though more dials fed bad examples just hold finer nonsense, and now you know to ask about the examples too. Scene three: before a system launches, its builders run it through its examples for three weeks, adjusting it a little after every mistake. Which ingredient is that? Training, the showing itself, the long loop of guess, compare, nudge. Run those three scenes again in your head until the sorting is instant. From, adjusted, adjusting. Data, parameters, training. When a salesman blurs them, and some will, you sort the sentence yourself, silently, and ask about the ingredient he skipped. He always skips one. It is usually the data.

Let me also give you the three ingredients working together on one system from your own world, end to end, so the trio stops being a list and becomes a machine you can see. Take a tool that drafts replies to customer enquiries in your voice. Its data: examples of enquiries, each paired with the reply you actually sent, hundreds of them, your real voice doing real work. Its parameters: millions of settings inside it, initially random, initially useless, producing gibberish in nobody's voice. Its training: the long loop, draft, compare with the reply you sent, nudge, next enquiry, until the drafts come out sounding like you on a good day. And the finished product, the thing you subscribe to, is the frozen list of settings, plus the program that runs new enquiries through them. Notice what is not in that story. Nobody wrote rules for your voice. Nobody could; you could not write them yourself. The voice was in the examples, the examples shaped the settings, and the settings now produce the voice on new cases. Data, parameters, training. Every time. When you meet a system that seems to break this pattern, look again, because you will find the three ingredients wearing a costume, and finding them is most of understanding the thing.

Before the recap, three more things deserve their moment, because each one prevents a specific, expensive misunderstanding I have watched smart business owners make. The first is about labels, the human judgements attached to examples, because labels sound like a technical detail and they are actually the soul of the data. When examples of customer messages are labelled urgent or routine, somebody decided what urgent means. When examples of advertisements are labelled good or bad, somebody decided what good means, and if they decided it meant clicks, the system will learn to chase clicks, including the cheap, curious, never-buying kind. The system learns the label, not your intention. It cannot learn your intention; your intention was never in the examples. So whenever you judge a tool's output and find it strangely faithful to the wrong goal, do not first blame the parameters or the training. Ask about the labels. Ask what counted as a success in the examples, because the machine is a mirror of exactly that decision, polished to a shine. In your own company this is good news disguised as a warning: if you ever build examples for a tool, the labelling is where your judgement enters the machine. Label like it matters, because it is the only place your values get in.

The second thing is a fuller picture of parameters, because the mixing board deserves one more visit. Think about what a single knob can and cannot do. On the mixing board, one dial might govern how much echo the voice carries. Turned one way, the voice sounds close and dry; turned the other, it sounds like a hall. No single position of that dial is the music, and yet the music you hear is partly that dial's doing. Now multiply that by millions, and let some of the dials govern distinctions no human has a name for, distinctions the training process found useful that nobody would have thought to build a dial for. That is the part I find genuinely wonderful, and I say so without any fog at all: the board wires itself, in effect, deciding through the long loop of nudges which distinctions deserve a dial. Nobody designed the dial that notices the particular flavour of urgency in a message that says whenever you get a chance but means today. Training grew it, because examples rewarded noticing. When you hear that a model discovered something its builders did not expect, this is usually what it means: a combination of settings nobody planned, doing a distinction nobody named. It is still settings. It is still numbers. But it is numbers arranged by experience, and experience arranges things in ways blueprints do not. That is why these systems are worth a whole course and not a pamphlet.

The third thing is about training, and it is the human cost hiding inside the word. We say the loop repeats billions of times, and the sentence slides by, but let it land: for the largest systems, training is one of the biggest industrial processes on earth, measured in electricity, in cooling water, in warehouses of specialised machines running hot for weeks. I do not tell you this to make you feel anything in particular about it. I tell you because it explains the landscape you buy tools in. It is why the biggest models come from a handful of organisations that can afford the workshop. It is why smaller companies, including ones that will pitch you, build on top of those models rather than training their own, the way a good local builder builds with lumber he did not mill. And it is why, when a tiny vendor claims to have trained a world-class model from scratch in a garage, your fog training should stir. Workshops at that scale cannot hide. Ask where the training happened and what it ran on, and watch whether the answer is a place or a poem.

One more pass at the trio, from the direction of failure, because you learn a machine fastest by watching its three ways to break. A system can fail from its data: the examples were thin, or stale, or labelled toward the wrong goal, and the guesses inherit all of it. A system can fail from its parameters in the plain sense that there are not enough of them for the fineness of the job, a small board asked to mix an orchestra, though in practice this is the rarest of the three failures for a buyer to meet. And a system can fail from its training: stopped too early, while the guesses were still crude, or run so long on the same examples that it memorised their quirks instead of learning their patterns, the way a student who memorises past exams collapses on a new question. Data, parameters, training: three ingredients, three failure modes, and a diagnosis habit for free. When any tool disappoints you, and they all will sometimes, ask which ingredient you are looking at before you conclude anything about the whole technology. Most disappointments are one ingredient, underfed.

Let me leave you with one small, practical exercise to carry into your week, smaller than a lesson and more useful than most meetings. The next time any tool, any pitch, any article mentions a system doing something impressive, I want you to silently ask three questions, one per ingredient. What were the examples, and who labelled them? How many settings are doing the work, and is the job fine-grained enough to need them? And how long, and on what, was it trained, and by whom? You will not always get answers. That is itself an answer. A seller who can describe his data, his parameters, and his training in plain words is selling machinery. A seller who can only describe feelings, revolutions, and magic is selling fog, and fog, as we established in lesson one, is priced by the pound. Three questions, three ingredients, and you are suddenly the hardest customer in the room to fool, which is exactly the customer I am trying to build.

And one sentence of perspective before we close the teaching, because I do not want the smallness of the parts to hide the size of the whole. Three ingredients is a short list. Flour, water, and heat is also a short list, and it gives you every bread on earth. The shortness of the list is not a measure of the thing built from it; it is a measure of how well we understand the thing. A field that can name its ingredients in three words is a field whose machinery you can reason about, argue about, price, and govern. Compare that with any technology whose sellers cannot name its ingredients at all. You now can name these. Data, parameters, training. Everything else in this department, the chatbots, the image makers, the agents waiting for you in later classes, is those three ideas arranged with increasing cleverness, and arrangements can be learned one at a time, the way we are learning them: plainly, in order, with the fog kept out of the room.

Recap, in order, as always. First: data is the examples the system learns from, usually labelled by people, and the examples draw the boundary of what the system can do, while the system itself never knows where that boundary is. Second: garbage in, garbage out; the quality of the examples governs the quality of the patterns, and no scale of bad examples fixes itself. Third: parameters are the internal settings, the knobs, meaningless one by one and powerful in combination; they are formed judgement stored as numbers, not stored facts and not memorised examples. Fourth: before training the parameters are essentially random and the system is useless; after training the same system is good, and the only thing that changed is the positions of the knobs. Fifth: training is the showing, the loop of guess, compare, nudge, repeated millions or billions of times; it is expensive, it happens in the workshop, and it ends, leaving a frozen list of numbers that the world then uses cheaply, one case at a time.

You now hold the three ingredients, and you hold them in the right order, with the right names, which puts you ahead of most of the noise you will hear this year.
