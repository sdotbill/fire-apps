# Where AI is strong, where it is brittle, and why

Come in. I am Professor David Kim, and this is AI Fundamentals, lesson five. You now know what the machinery is, what it is made of, what a model is, and what act it performs. Today we grade it. The title of the lesson is the whole agenda: where AI is strong, where it is brittle, and why both are true of the same machine at the same time. This is the lesson the salesmen hope you skip, and it is also the lesson the fear merchants hope you skip, because both of them need a one-sided picture and the truth here is two-sided in a very specific, very learnable way. The strengths are real, and I will state them at full size. The brittleness is real, and I will state it at full size too. And the why, the single design fact that produces both lists, is something you already know: a model is a function with a memory of examples, and everything in today's lesson is that sentence, cashed out. By the end you will be able to look at any task in your business and sort it, calmly, into work this machinery should do, work it should do with you watching, and work it should never touch. That sorting is the most valuable skill in this course for a working owner, and it cannot be bought as a feature. It can only be learned. Let us learn it.

The strengths first, at full size, because a fog-clearer does not minimise. Strength one: scale of experience. A trained model carries the distilled shape of more examples than any human being will meet in a lifetime. You have read perhaps a few thousand business emails closely in your life. A writing model has been shaped by more text than a thousand lifetimes of reading. On any task where the pattern lives in volume, the machine starts with an advantage no hiring decision can match. Strength two: speed. A prediction takes a fraction of a second. The model can sort a thousand enquiries, draft a hundred routine replies, and scan a year of reviews for recurring complaints in the time it takes you to drink your coffee, and the thousandth prediction is made with the same care as the first. Strength three, the twin of speed: tirelessness. The model does not get bored at hour six. It does not skim the last fifty invoices because the first fifty were dull. A great deal of expensive human error is fatigue error, and this machinery simply does not have that failure mode. Strength four: consistency. Given the same patterns, it reaches for the same judgements, Monday and Friday, in January and in June, unaffected by mood, weather, or a difficult morning. And strength five, the one I find genuinely remarkable: breadth of pattern. Because the examples span so much, the model sometimes connects things no single specialist would connect, a phrasing from one trade that unlocks a problem in another, a structure from one kind of document that organises a different kind. That breadth is the source of the moments that make people say the word creative about these systems, and while I will keep correcting the word, I will not correct the moment away. The moments are real. On familiar ground, at volume, under time pressure, with patterns that live in examples: this machinery is strong in ways that should change how a small company is run, and a competitor who understands that is dangerous to one who does not.

Now the brittleness, at the same size, in the same plain voice. Brittle point one, and it is the master key to all the others: the model knows only its examples, and it does not know what it does not know. Inside the territory its training covered thickly, its predictions are usually good. Step outside that territory, into the unusual client, the unprecedented situation, the week the world changed, and the predictions can be wild, and they arrive in exactly the same confident shape as the good ones. The system has no edge detector. It cannot feel the ground thinning under its feet, because feeling the ground was never part of the design. Brittle point two: fluency outruns accuracy. The language models in particular produce sentences whose smoothness is not evidence about their truth. A draft can read beautifully and contain a wrong date, an invented discount, a promise you never made, a fact about your own business that is simply not so, stated in your own voice, better than you would have stated it. The field has a word for the system inventing things in this fluent way, and you should know the word because you will meet it: hallucination. Strip the costume and it is exactly what this course would predict. A machine that produces the next likely words will, when the true words are not in its patterns, produce likely words instead, and likely words are sometimes invented ones. It is not lying. Lying requires knowing the truth and choosing otherwise. It is guessing past the edge of its memory and not knowing it has crossed the edge, which in some ways is more dangerous, because there is no malice to detect, only confidence to discount. Brittle point three: no stake and no world. The model does not live with the consequences of its predictions. It does not meet the client whose quote was wrong. It does not know that your town's big employer just announced layoffs, unless that fact is in its input, because it has no window and no ears; its world is its examples and the text you hand it, full stop. And brittle point four: frozenness. The settings were set in the workshop at a moment in the past. Prices change, platforms change, your own offers change, and the model goes on predicting the world as its examples described it, until somebody retrains it. Every one of these four points, notice, is the strength list read from the other side. Tireless consistency is frozenness that cannot adapt. Breadth of pattern is a memory that cannot tell its centre from its edge. Fluency is prediction doing its job so well that it predicts straight past the truth. One design, two faces. That is the why, and if you remember nothing else today, remember that the strengths and the brittleness are not two facts about the machine. They are one fact, seen from two sides.

Let me make the sorting skill concrete with your own week's work, because this is where the lesson earns its keep. Into the first pile, work the machinery should simply do: sorting and filing, first drafts of routine messages, summarising long threads for your eyes only, finding the recurring phrases in a hundred reviews, turning your spoken notes into tidy text, suggesting posting times, ranking enquiries for your attention. What do these share? The patterns are thick, the volume is high, the cost of a single error is small, and you will see the output before the world does. Into the second pile, work it should do with you watching: drafts of anything a client will read under your name, prices suggested but not sent, replies to complaints, anything involving a date, a figure, or a commitment. The machinery drafts, you grade, you send. Your judgement is the edge detector the system does not have, and on this pile your job title is exactly that. Into the third pile, work it should never touch alone: anything where an error is expensive or irreversible, anything legal, medical, or financial in its consequences, anything depending on facts about your town this month that cannot be in its examples, and anything where the person on the other end needs, and deserves, a human being who will live with the outcome. The piles are not about trusting or distrusting the technology. They are about matching the task's shape to the machine's shape: thick patterns and cheap errors on one side, thin patterns and dear errors on the other, and your attention spread accordingly. An owner who sorts this way gets the speed without the scars.

Now our guided try-it, and I want you to actually run it, because sorting is a motor skill as much as an idea. I will name six tasks, quickly, and you call the pile before I do. A tool offers to answer your most common customer questions automatically, from your past answers. Pile two, shading toward one: the patterns are thick, they are your own answers, but the words go out under your name, so you watch until your scoreboard says otherwise. A tool offers to write the first version of your monthly newsletter. Pile one for the draft, pile two for the send, and notice how many real tasks split exactly like that, draft and send in different piles. A tool offers to predict which of your services will grow next year. Pile two at best, and handle with tongs: the patterns are thin, the world moves, and the prediction is a prompt for your thinking, never a substitute for it. A tool offers to handle a furious customer's complaint end to end while you are on holiday. Pile three. The patterns of fury are exactly where a memory of examples is weakest, the cost of a wrong tone is a reputation, and the customer deserves a person. A tool offers to pull every phone number and date out of a stack of documents into a tidy list. Pile one, with a spot check: extraction from thick patterns, errors cheap and visible. And a tool offers to tell you what a competitor across town charged a specific client last month. Pile three, and walk away from the tool as well: that fact is not in any legitimate examples, so whatever it answers is invention wearing a uniform, and now you can see the uniform for what it is. Six tasks, sorted. Run that drill on every new offer you receive and the offers will start sorting themselves.

Let me slow down on the strengths once more, with scenes, because a strength you have watched working is a strength you will actually use, and unused strengths are just brochure copy. Scene one: the Monday morning inbox of a company that has had a busy weekend. Ninety messages: enquiries, invoices, two complaints, a supplier changing terms, a dozen pieces of junk that slipped past the filter, and the one message that matters most, a client ready to sign, buried in the middle. A person needs an hour and a strong coffee to find the shape of that inbox, and by message seventy the skimming has begun and the skimming is where the signing client gets a slow reply. The machinery reads all ninety with the same attention, ranks them by the patterns of your past Mondays, and puts the signing client on top in seconds. That is scale, speed, and tirelessness not as abstractions but as a client answered while he was still warm. Scene two: a year of customer reviews, four hundred of them, which no owner will ever sit down and read end to end. The machinery can, and it finds that one phrase, parking, appears in a third of the unhappy ones, a pattern invisible to anyone reading a review at a time because no single review is about parking. Your business has a parking problem it did not know it had, found by breadth of pattern across volume no human would process. Scene three: the routine acknowledgement messages, the thank-you-for-your-enquiry notes that must go out fast, in your voice, forty times a month. Drafted by the machinery, glanced at by you, sent in minutes. Nobody's genius was needed for those messages. Somebody's hours were being spent on them, though, and now those hours belong to work that actually needs a mind. Hold those three scenes when the brittleness list starts to sour you on the whole enterprise. The strengths pay rent. That is why we bother mapping the cracks at all: not to discard the tool, but to use a rent-paying tool without letting it sign your name to its guesses.

And now the brittleness deserves the same treatment, scenes and all, because a crack you have watched opening is a crack you will spot in the wild. Scene one: the confident quote. An owner lets a tool draft a reply to a price enquiry, and the draft, fluent and warm, quotes a figure from a package the company stopped offering last spring. The settings were frozen before the spring. The tool does not know the offer died, because knowing things like that is not in its design; it predicted the words a reply like this usually contains, and the usual words carried the usual price. Caught by the owner at the watching pile, it is a funny story. Sent unwatched, it is a promise the company must honour or explain, and the explanation sounds exactly as good as it is. Scene two: the invented reference. A tool drafting a proposal mentions, helpfully, a project your company completed for a named business in a neighbouring town. The project does not exist. The business does, which makes it worse. The model needed a reference-shaped sentence, its patterns supplied one, and the sentence was smooth enough to sail past a tired reader. This is hallucination in working clothes: not exotic, not rare, and entirely predictable once you know the machine predicts words rather than checking facts. Scene three: the strange week. A storm, a road closure, a local factory's announcement, and suddenly the enquiries arriving are unlike any in the examples: urgent, confused, asking about things your company has never sold. The machinery ranks them by the old patterns, confidently, wrongly, and the owner who trusts the ranking that week answers the wrong people first. Nothing in the system announced that the world had moved. Systems like this never announce it. The announcement is your job, and it is made of local knowledge no model has: you live in the town the model only has examples of. Three scenes, one moral: the brittleness is not random malfunction. It is the design, meeting the edge of its examples, in a confident voice. Expect it there, specifically there, and it loses most of its power to harm you.

Fairness requires one more scene, on the other side of the ledger, and I will not skip it, because this room deals in full pictures. Human beings are brittle too, in our own well-documented ways. We get tired, we play favourites, we remember the last client and forget the forty before him, we are confident about things we half-read, and we invent, fluently and with total sincerity, when our memory runs out; the word confabulation exists in psychology for a reason. The honest comparison is not machine brittle, human solid. It is two different brittlenesses, with different shapes. Human error tends to be noisy and self-doubting at the edges; we hesitate, we say I am not sure, we go and check, because we live in the world the answer is about and the world pushes back on us. Machine error tends to be smooth and certain at the edges, because nothing pushes back inside the settings. That difference is why the partnership works when it is arranged properly: the machine supplies the scale and the stamina, the human supplies the doubt and the world. Arrange it the other way, human doing volume until fatigue makes the errors, machine supplying unexamined certainty on top, and you have built the worst of both. The three piles from earlier are exactly this partnership, written as a work rota. That is all they ever were.

So the why, one final time, stated as a single chain you can recite. The model is a function. Its settings are a memory of examples. Prediction is running new cases through that memory. Therefore the model is strong wherever the new cases resemble the remembered ones, because the memory is vast, fast, and tireless. Therefore it is brittle wherever the new cases depart from the remembered ones, because there is nothing else in the box to consult: no world, no doubt, no stake, no clock. Strength and brittleness are not a balance the engineers struck. They are the same property, resemblance, helping you on one side of the line and leaving you on the other. And the line itself is invisible to the machine, which is the single most important sentence in this lesson, and I will repeat it in different words so it has two chances to stay with you: the system cannot tell you where its own competence ends. You are the boundary detector. In every arrangement you build with this technology, for the rest of your working life, that is the job that remains yours, and no release note will ever retire you from it.

A few working habits grow directly out of today's map, and I want them named before we close, because a map nobody acts on is decoration. Habit one: spot-check the strong pile. Even work the machinery does alone gets sampled by your eyes, a few outputs a week, the way a good owner walks a floor he trusts. The sampling is not distrust. It is how you notice the world moving before the errors get expensive. Habit two: keep the scoreboard from lesson four running on the watched pile, and let it promote and demote tasks between piles. A drafting tool that grades well for three months on your cases earns more freedom. One that invents a second date earns less. The piles are not commandments; they are a living rota, revised by evidence, which is the most this-course sentence I can offer you. Habit three: never let a fluent output skip the check on its checkable parts. Dates, figures, names, prices, and promises are the five things to verify in any draft before it leaves, because those are the places where a predicted word and a true word part company most expensively. Read the warmth of the draft for tone if you like; then read those five things like an auditor. Habit four: write down the strange weeks. When the world moves, a storm, a closure, a sudden change in your market, note it in the same notebook as your scoreboard, because a dip in the machine's grading that matches a note in your book is not a mystery; it is the boundary, found and marked, and marked boundaries are how an owner learns the true shape of a tool's territory faster than any manual could teach it.

And let me close the teaching by returning the lesson to its proper emotional temperature, because I have heard this material land two wrong ways and I want neither for you. It should not land as fear: nothing in the brittleness list is a reason to refuse the strengths, any more than a car's ability to leave the road is a reason to walk everywhere. It should not land as complacency either: nothing in the strengths list licenses an unwatched signature. It should land as professionalism, the calm of a person who knows a tool's working range the way a carpenter knows a saw's. Every powerful tool has a range. Inside it, the work sings. Outside it, the same power that made the work sing makes the damage fast. Knowing the range is not a limitation on your ambition. It is what separates the owner who will still be profiting from this machinery in ten years from the one who will be telling a story about the winter it quoted the dead package to his best client. You are being trained for the ten-year version. That is the whole design of this course, and today was its most practical day.

One more distinction sharpens the whole map, and then we are done with the teaching. It is the difference between a mistake and a fabrication, because owners punish them as one thing and they are two. A mistake is a wrong prediction on a case inside the territory: the ranking put a weak enquiry above a strong one, the draft chose the second-best wording. Mistakes are the cost of prediction, they are counted by the scoreboard, and a good rate of them is the price of the speed. A fabrication is a prediction that sailed off the territory entirely and brought back an invented fact in a confident voice: the dead package's price, the project that never happened. Fabrications are not a worse grade of mistake; they are a different event, the boundary being crossed without a signal. The management differs accordingly. Mistakes you budget for. Fabrications you design against: the five-point check, the watched pile, the rule that names, dates, figures, prices, and promises are verified by a person before they travel. When you can say which of the two just happened in your business, and respond to each in its own way, you are no longer a user of this technology. You are its manager, which is the rank this course has been quietly promoting you to since lesson one.

Let me leave you a single question to carry out of this lesson, because it compresses the whole map into something you can ask in a doorway, in a sales meeting, in your own head at midnight. The question is: what would this look like if it were wrong? Ask it of a ranking and you remember to glance at the bottom of the list as well as the top. Ask it of a draft and your eyes go to the dates and the figures. Ask it of a confident summary and you open the original thread for the one paragraph that mattered. The question works because it points your attention at the boundary, which is where, as we have established at some length, all the interesting failures live and where the machine itself will never point. Tools that survive the question earn more of your business. Tools whose sellers flinch at it have told you which pile they belong in, and it is the third one, the one with the door that stays closed.

Let me also give you the buyer's version of this entire lesson, compressed into the three questions that belong in every demonstration you ever sit through, because a demonstration is a performance on the performer's home ground and the map tells you exactly how to take it off that ground. Question one: show me it failing. An honest builder can show you the kinds of cases where the system is weak, because he has graded them; a seller who claims there are none is telling you his system does not know what it does not know, and neither does he. Question two: show me it on my cases. Your enquiries, your reviews, your town, your spring prices — the territory is yours, and performance on the builder's territory is evidence about the builder's territory only. Question three: what happens when the world changes? The honest answer involves retraining, updating, and your own scoreboard catching the drift; the fog answer involves the system adapting, learning, and keeping up, verbs we have spent four lessons learning to translate. Three questions, each one a direct application of today's map, and together they will do more for your purchasing than any feature comparison ever printed. A tool is not strong or brittle in the abstract. It is strong or brittle on a territory, for a task, at a cost of error — and you are the only person in the room who knows your territory, your tasks, and your costs. That is why this lesson could not be replaced by a rating website. The map is general. The territory is yours.

Recap, in order. First: the strengths are scale of experience, speed, tirelessness, consistency, and breadth of pattern, and they are real and full-sized. Second: the brittleness is just as real: the model does not know what it does not know, its fluency outruns its accuracy and can invent in a confident voice, it has no stake and no window on the world, and its settings are frozen in the past. Third: the two lists are one fact, the memory of examples, seen from two sides, which is why no update will ever give you the strengths with the brittleness removed. Fourth: the owner's skill is sorting tasks into three piles, machinery alone, machinery watched, and machinery never, by asking two questions: how thick are the patterns, and how dear is an error.

What comes next is the questions on this lesson, with the full text open beside you, as always; going back to read is learning, not cheating. When you are done, lesson six, our last, gathers the whole course into the honest vocabulary: the words to use, the words to refuse, and the set problem one final time, in your own words, standing on everything we have built. You came in today with a machine. You leave knowing where it stands firm and where it cracks, and why. That is most of wisdom about any tool, and you have it now.
