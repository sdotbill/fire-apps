# The honest vocabulary: no magic, no menace

Come in. I am Professor David Kim, and this is AI Fundamentals, lesson six, our last lesson together. Its title is the rule of this room, stated one final time: the honest vocabulary — no magic, no menace. Across five lessons we built the machinery in plain words. We named the thing in lesson one: programs that learn patterns from examples. We counted the ingredients in lesson two: data, parameters, training. We defined the product in lesson three: a model, a function with a memory of examples. We found the core act in lesson four: prediction, in the workshop and in the wild. And in lesson five we graded the whole arrangement: strong where the new cases resemble the remembered ones, brittle where they depart, one design with two faces. Today we do the thing all of that was for. We fix the words. Because a vocabulary is not decoration on knowledge; a vocabulary is where knowledge lives when it leaves the classroom and goes to work in your week. You will forget, in time, some of my sentences. You will not forget the words you reach for when a salesman smiles, a headline shouts, or a tool does something uncanny at midnight. If those words are honest ones, the knowledge is still working. If they are fog words, the knowledge is gone no matter how well you once understood it. That is why the last lesson of a fundamentals course is a lesson about language, and why I ask you to take it as seriously as any lesson before it. It is the lesson that keeps the other five.

Here is the honest vocabulary, the whole of it, laid out on the table the way we built it, one word at a time, with its plain meaning attached. Data: the examples the system learns from. Parameters: the internal settings inside it, the knobs. Training: the showing, the long process of adjusting those settings against the examples until the guesses come out right. Model: a function, a machine for turning inputs into outputs, whose settings were adjusted over examples until its predictions matched reality often enough to be useful. Prediction: the act itself, a guess about something not yet known, produced from patterns in things already known, one new case at a time. Inference: the same act in its work clothes, the using of the frozen model. And around those six, the honest adjectives: trained, frozen, graded, likely, brittle. A trained system, not a taught child. Frozen settings, not a growing mind. A graded prediction, with a scoreboard, not a trusted oracle. A likely outcome, with the likelihood stated, not a certain one. A brittle edge, named and watched, not a mysterious failure. Six nouns and a handful of honest adjectives: that is the entire vocabulary of this course, and I will tell you a secret about it. It is enough. Every true thing that can be said about the systems in this department can be said with those words, and every sentence about them that cannot survive translation into those words was never true to begin with. That is the test, and today we practise it until it is reflex.

Now the other list, the words to refuse, or at least to translate on sight, because each of them smuggles a mind into the box. The system knows. It does not know; it predicts from patterns, and the difference decides what questions you ask next. The system understands your brand. It does not understand; it was shown examples and its settings carry their shape, and the day your brand does something its examples never contained, the understanding will be nowhere to be found, because it was never there. The system thinks, decides, believes, wants, cares, creates. Each of those words names something a person does with a mind, and each of them, applied to the machinery, performs the same small theft: it moves your trust from the scoreboard, where it can be earned, to a character, where it can only be felt. I am not asking you to become a pedant, correcting your friends at dinner. I am asking you to run the translation in your own head, in the half second before you decide what something is worth. Knows becomes predicts from patterns in examples. Understands becomes was shaped by examples of. Decides becomes outputs the highest-ranked guess. Creates becomes generates, by predicting, in a loop, what usually comes next. The translation takes no time once it is habit, and it changes the temperature of every room it happens in, because fog cannot survive being translated out loud twice in a row.

And the two master fog words themselves, the pair in our title. Magic first. Magic is the word for a result whose workings we are not going to discuss. It is a curtain with a price tag. Whenever the workings of a system are hidden behind wonder, somebody is being protected from your questions, and it is not you. The honest vocabulary has no use for magic because the honest vocabulary is the workings: examples, settings, adjusting, guessing, grading. A thing explained is not diminished. The adjusted recipe is not less remarkable for our knowing it was adjusted. If anything the machinery is more impressive stated plainly, the way a bridge is more impressive when you understand the loads it carries than when you merely gasp at it. Then menace. Menace is the word for a result whose intentions we are invited to imagine. But the machinery has no intentions, and imagining them is the same curtain, hung from the other side. The real risks of these systems are not intentions and never were: they are the risks we mapped in lesson five, confident errors at the edge of the examples, invented facts in a fluent voice, frozen settings in a moving world, and owners who stopped grading because the outputs felt like a colleague's. Those risks are serious, manageable, and boring to headline, which is precisely why the headline prefers menace. No magic, no menace: not a slogan, a discipline. It means every claim about this technology gets translated into machinery before it gets believed, priced, or feared.

Let us now run the discipline at full speed, because our guided try-it today is the one I most want you to keep for the rest of your life: the headline drill. I will give you three headlines of the kind that will cross your phone this year, and we translate each one together, out loud, into the honest vocabulary, and then we judge what is left. Headline one: artificial intelligence now understands your customers better than you do. Translate. A model, trained on examples of customer behaviour, predicts some customer behaviour, on the cases resembling its examples, more accurately than an unaided owner. What is left after translation? Possibly a useful tool, and two questions: which behaviour, and graded how, on whose customers. The fog said better than you, full stop, a rival for your own knowledge of your own people. The translation says a predictor with a territory, which you can test this month for the price of a notebook. Headline two: the machine is learning, and soon it will not need us. Translate. The builders are training new versions of the model on new examples, in the workshop, as they have always done, and the frozen version in your hands is not learning anything tonight. What is left? A release schedule, dressed as an uprising. Headline three, my favourite, because it sounds so reasonable: our platform's intelligence makes the decisions, so you can relax. Translate. Our model ranks options by prediction and acts on the top-ranked guess without showing you the likelihood, the territory, or the scoreboard. What is left? An invitation to stop being the boundary detector, which lesson five identified as the one job that remains yours. Relax is the most expensive word in that headline. Three headlines, three translations, and notice: in every case the machinery underneath was worth taking seriously, and in every case the fog was the part asking for your money or your fear. The drill is the course. Everything before today was learning to do the drill.

And now, to close the course the way it deserves, we work the set problem one final time, in full, standing on everything we have built. The problem, as this course sets it: in your own words, one paragraph: what is a model, and what did training do to it? Use one example from your own life where prediction, not understanding, was enough. You heard my worked answer in lesson three, and you built your own with me there. Hear it once more, now with five lessons inside your ears, and notice how much more of it you can hear. A model is a function, a machine for turning inputs into outputs, whose internal settings were adjusted, over millions of examples, until its predictions matched reality often enough to be useful. Training is that adjustment. A spam filter never understood a word of your email; prediction was enough, and knowing the difference between the two is the whole first lesson. Six bones, as before: function, inputs, outputs, parameters, examples, predictions. But now you know what each bone carries. Function carries the shape of the act. Parameters carry the memory of examples, embodied, not stored. Training carries the workshop, the cost, the frozenness. Predictions carry the scoreboard, the likelihoods, the boundary you must detect. And the example carries the philosophy of the whole course: that prediction without understanding is not a scandal or a trick, but a working arrangement, powerful inside its territory and honest about what it is, as long as the human beside it keeps the vocabulary honest. When you write your own paragraph after this lesson, and I hope you will, write it as the owner you have become: machinery in plain words, an example from your own ground, and not one fog word standing.

There is a citizen's skill underneath all of this, and I named it in lesson one and I will name it again at the door: you will leave able to read any headline about AI and know, within a sentence or two, whether it is reporting or theatre. That skill now has a population of one more, and it matters beyond your purchasing, though it will save you money. It matters because this technology will be argued about, in public, by people selling wonder and people selling fear, for the rest of your life, and the arguments will be won by whoever controls the words. A person who says model, examples, settings, prediction, graded, brittle, in the right places, is a person whose neighbours can think clearly around them. Fog is contagious, and so is clarity. I have done my best to make you a carrier of the second. The rest of this department's classes, the ones about how machines learn in depth, about data and where it lives, about agents that act in the world, all of them assume the vocabulary you now own, and all of them will go faster for you because the fog cannot get a foothold. That is what a fundamentals course is for: not to finish your education, but to make the rest of it possible at speed.

Before the recap, let me hold a small clinic on the remaining everyday words, the ones too ordinary to have made the first list, because fog lives in ordinary words most comfortably of all. The system learns. This one is half honest, which makes it the most dangerous of the set. Training is a kind of learning, and I use the word myself about the workshop. But in the wild, at your desk, the frozen system is not learning, and the sentence the system learns from you, said by a seller, quietly promises that today's errors are tuition toward tomorrow's excellence. They are not. They are just today's errors. Translate: the system was trained, past tense, in the workshop, and the next version may be trained again. The system remembers. It carries a memory of examples, embodied in settings, and it remembers nothing about you, your last conversation, or Tuesday, unless those things are placed in its input again. Translate: the settings carry the shape of the training examples. The system sees your logo, reads your reviews, writes your posts. Sees, reads, writes: each word borrows a human act. The honest versions are longer and better: it processes an image and predicts what it depicts; it takes in text and predicts patterns in it; it generates text by predicting what usually comes next. Longer, yes. Fog is always shorter than honesty, which is exactly its commercial advantage, and why honesty has to be a practised discipline rather than a preference. And the word smart, which we met in lesson one: smart is a compliment wearing a lab coat. Translate it every time into the question it is dodging: good at what, on whose examples, graded how. A vocabulary is a set of questions as much as a set of answers, and the honest words are the ones that open questions while the fog words close them. Knows closes. Predicts opens: how often, on what, at whose expense. That difference, open versus closed, is the surest field test I can give you. When a word about this technology makes you feel that everything is settled, be suspicious. When it makes you ask the next question, keep it.

Now let me show you the vocabulary doing a full day's work, because words are only yours when you have watched them earn. Imagine you are writing the page on your own website that describes how your company uses these tools, and imagine writing it twice. First, the fog version, the version a thousand companies published this year: our intelligent platform understands your needs, learns your brand, and creates content that connects, while our smart assistant takes care of your customers around the clock. Every verb in that paragraph is borrowed, and a customer who has taken this course can translate it in seconds and trust it accordingly, which is to say, not much. Now the honest version, same facts, honest words: we use trained software to draft first versions of your content from examples of work that performed well. A person here grades every draft, checks every date and figure, and sends only what passes. The software predicts; we judge; you get the speed of the first and the responsibility of the second. Read those two paragraphs side by side and ask which company you would hand money to. The honest version is not weaker. It is stronger in the only way that survives contact with reality, because every claim in it can be checked, and the checking will go well. That is the commercial secret the fog merchants have backwards: in a market full of magic, the plain description is the differentiator. Your customers are being fogged by everyone. Be the one whose words still mean something at the end of the month. No magic in your copy is not a constraint on your marketing. It is, I have come to believe, the best marketing available to a small company in this field, because it is the only copy in the county that a careful reader cannot translate into nothing.

One more application, inward this time, because the vocabulary governs how you talk to yourself and your people, not only how you read strangers. When a tool errs in your business, the fog vocabulary offers you two useless sentences: the system is hopeless, which is menace turned inward, and the system is brilliant but moody, which is magic with an excuse attached. The honest vocabulary offers a better one: the prediction failed on a case outside its examples, or inside them at the rate the scoreboard already knew, and here is what we change, the pile, the check, the examples we feed the next version. That sentence leads somewhere. It has handles. Teams that speak it get better at using the machinery month by month, and teams that speak fog stay exactly as good as the day they bought it, because fog sentences have no handles and nothing to revise. Language is the steering wheel of a small company more than of a large one, because in a small company the owner's words become everyone's instructions by Friday. Set the vocabulary at the top and the clarity compounds downward. That, more than any single purchase, is the return on this course, and it starts the first time you catch a fog word in your own mouth and translate it before it lands. I still catch them in mine. The discipline is not a destination. It is a practice, like grading, like checking the five things, like every other professional habit this course has tried to install.

Let me give the two fog words their final examination, separately, because each deserves its own closing argument. Magic, examined, is always a claim about the speaker, never about the system. It says: I will not show you the workings, either because I cannot, which is ignorance, or because I will not, which is salesmanship. Neither deserves your money at the price wonder commands. And notice what the honest vocabulary does to wonder itself: it does not kill it, it relocates it. The real wonder in this field is not that a machine seems to think. It is that adjusted numbers, set by showing a function enough examples, can draft in your voice, rank your morning, and find the parking complaint hiding in four hundred reviews. That is wonderful the way flight is wonderful: more wonderful with the engineering visible, not less. Menace, examined, is the mirror claim. It says: I will show you intentions instead of workings, because intentions frighten and workings inform, and frightened audiences stay for the next instalment while informed ones go back to work. The honest reply to menace is not reassurance; reassurance is just magic with a kind face. The honest reply is the map from lesson five: here is where it is strong, here is where it is brittle, here is the boundary, and here is the human standing on it. A risk with an address can be managed. A menace can only be dreaded, and dread, like wonder, is a product somebody is selling. Refuse both products. Buy machinery, grade it, watch its edges, and let the theatre play to emptier rooms. That is the whole politics of this course, and it fits in one breath because the vocabulary carries it.

And a last word about the word intelligence itself, to close the circle we opened in lesson one. We are not going to win the renaming of a seventy-year-old field, and I am not asking you to try. The phrase artificial intelligence is in the name of this very course, and it will stay in the language long after both of us. The discipline was never to ban the phrase. It is to hear it, every time, with the translation running underneath, the way a person who knows a second language hears their first one still. Artificial intelligence, and underneath: programs that learn patterns from examples. A model, and underneath: a function with a memory of examples. It knows, and underneath: it predicts, from patterns, and here is its scoreboard. Two layers, always, the public word and the honest machinery, and you are never again at the mercy of the top layer alone. That bilingual hearing is the actual diploma of AI Fundamentals. Everything else, the definitions, the piles, the drills, was scaffolding to build it. You can take the scaffolding down now. The hearing stays.

One farewell exercise, the smallest in the course and the one I predict you will use most: the one-breath summary. Whenever you must explain to a partner, a customer, or yourself what one of these systems is doing in your business, say it in one breath, in honest words, in this shape: it is a model, trained on examples of this, that predicts that, and we grade it by counting this. Watch it work. It is a model, trained on examples of our past enquiries, that predicts which new enquiries deserve the first call, and we grade it by counting how many of its top calls became clients. One breath, no fog, and every important fact present: the thing, its examples, its act, its scoreboard. If a claim about any system cannot be said in that shape, the claim is not ready to be believed, including, especially, your own claims at the end of a hopeful day. Practise the breath until it is rude to do otherwise in your own head. Then teach it to one other person, because clarity compounds fastest when it is given away, and a course like this one is only ever a way of lending a vocabulary from one mind to another until the second mind no longer needs the loan.

And since a last lesson should leave the door open rather than close it, here is what your vocabulary unlocks next, stated plainly so you can hear your own progress. The next classes in this department will speak of how machines learn from data in greater depth, and you will not need their first hour, because you own their first principles. They will speak of data and where it lives and what it proves, and you will already be asking whose examples, labelled by whom, checked how. They will speak of agents, systems that act in the world through tools, and you will see the predictor inside the actor from the first minute, and ask the only question that matters about it: what does it predict, what can it touch when it is wrong, and who is standing on the boundary. That is the sound of a fundamentals course paying interest. Foundations are invisible when they work. You will simply find, class after class, that the new material lands faster for you than for the person who skipped the fog-clearing, because none of your attention is being spent on costumes. Spend it all on the machinery. There is plenty of machinery, it is genuinely interesting, and some of it, I will confess at the door of my own course, is beautiful, in the plain, unmagical way that well-made things are beautiful when you finally see how they work.

Keep the vocabulary the way you keep a good tool: oiled, in reach, and lent out carefully. It will dull if you stop using it, because the fog is patient and it publishes every day. But it sharpens just as reliably, one translation at a time, and every headline you translate without thinking is proof the sharpening is holding. That is all any teacher can honestly promise at the end of a course: not that you will never be fogged again, but that you now own the means of clearing it yourself, in your own head, at the speed of reading.

Recap, in order, and this recap carries the whole course with it, so take it slowly. Lesson one: artificial intelligence names programs that learn patterns from examples, machinery, mathematics running at enormous scale on data, and prediction is not understanding. Lesson two: three ingredients, data the examples, parameters the settings, training the showing, and garbage in means garbage out. Lesson three: a model is a function with a memory of examples, embodied rather than stored, able to generalise and unable to show its evidence. Lesson four: prediction is the core act, training in the workshop and prediction in the wild, and predictions can be graded while understandings cannot. Lesson five: strong where cases resemble the examples, brittle where they depart, one design with two faces, and you are the boundary detector. Lesson six, today: the honest vocabulary holds all of it, knows becomes predicts, understands becomes was shaped by, and no magic and no menace are permitted to stand where machinery can be named.

Come back and visit it whenever a headline tries to fog you; the vocabulary will be here, and so will the sentence we started with, the whole secret in one line: a model is a function that was shown so many examples that its guesses became good, and training is the showing. You know it now the way you know your own trade: from the inside. Class dismissed, and well done.
