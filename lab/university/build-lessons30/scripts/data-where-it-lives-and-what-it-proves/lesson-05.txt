# Charts that tell the truth and charts that lean

Good to see you. Today AI and Computing earns its keep. I am Professor Juhani Korhonen, and this is lesson five of six in Data — Where It Lives and What It Proves: charts that tell the truth and charts that lean. Every lesson so far has dealt with numbers in rows. Today the numbers put on pictures, because most people meet data as pictures — in reports, in presentations, in news, in the pitch deck of a person who would like some of your money. A chart is an argument made with geometry. It can be an honest argument or a leaning one, and the leaning is usually accomplished without changing a single number. I write the units on the board before opinions, every time. Bring your curiosity, and one chart from your own life that you are prepared to discover has been leaning at you for years; the rest, as ever, is provided, and the record of this class — all six lessons of it — stays open beside you.

First, what a chart is for. A chart translates numbers into shapes and lengths and positions, because the human eye compares lengths and positions faster and more reliably than it compares digits in a table. A good chart makes a true pattern visible at a glance. That is a genuine service, and I am not an enemy of charts; I make them weekly. But the translation has a property you must never forget: the eye believes the geometry before the mind reads the numbers. If the geometry exaggerates, the reader feels the exaggeration as fact and may never consult the digits at all. Every leaning technique in this lesson works through that one property. The numbers stay honest. The geometry lies on their behalf.

The first and most common lean is the cut axis. An axis is the numbered line along the bottom or the side of a chart that gives the scale. In a bar chart, each bar's length stands for its value, and the eye compares lengths. That comparison is only honest if the axis starts at zero, because a bar's length is measured from where the axis begins. Suppose our business's monthly enquiries run like this: ninety-six, ninety-eight, one hundred, and one hundred and two across four months — a gentle rise of six, across the whole period, on a base near one hundred. Drawn from zero, the four bars stand nearly equal, which is the truth: stability with a slight improvement. Now cut the axis: start it at ninety. The visible bars are now proportional to six, eight, ten, and twelve, and the last bar stands twice as tall as the first. The eye reports a doubling. The numbers report a rise of about six percent. Both charts use identical data. One of them is a lie told in lengths. When you meet a bar chart, your first glance belongs not to the bars but to the axis: where does it start? If it does not start at zero, the bars cannot be trusted as lengths, and you must read the numbers and ignore your eyes, which will object. Let them object. You are in charge.

A qualification, because exactness is our religion here: for line charts, which show change over time as a path rather than as bars, a non-zero start is sometimes defensible — a temperature chart that began at absolute zero would show nothing at all. The sin is not the cut itself. The sin is the cut without notice, in a chart whose visual force depends on the reader assuming a full scale. The honest remedies are simple: start at zero, or mark the break in the axis visibly, or choose a form that does not trade on length. A chart-maker who cuts an axis and hides the cut has told you something about themselves that no subsequent chart from the same hand should make you forget.

The second lean is the cherry-picked range. A range, here, is the stretch of time or the set of cases the chart includes. Every chart must include something and exclude something else; choosing is unavoidable. Leaning begins when the choice is made by the conclusion — when the maker tries start dates and end dates until the picture agrees with the sentence they intend to say, and then publishes that picture as if it were the whole story. Our business's revenue, by month, rises and falls with the seasons, as lesson four established. A chart from November to January shows a glorious climb. A chart from January to March shows a depressing slide. A chart of the full two years shows a wavy line, roughly level, with winters on top — the truth, which is less exciting than either excerpt and more useful than both. When a chart shows you a range, ask what lies immediately outside it, on both sides. The data just outside the frame is the data the maker decided you did not need. Decide for yourself whether they were right. Usually the question answers itself the moment it is asked out loud.

The third lean you already own from lesson one: the missing denominator. A chart of counts — complaints doubled, incidents soared — that never tells you out of how many. Complaints rising from five to ten is a doubling. If customers rose in the same period from five hundred to two thousand, then the complaint rate — complaints per customer — actually fell by half: five over five hundred is one percent; ten over two thousand is zero point five percent. The count doubled. The rate halved. The chart of counts, alone, announces a crisis in a business that is, by the rate, improving. Rates need denominators, and charts that show counts where rates are the honest measure are leaning, whether or not the maker intended to lean. Intent is not the standard in this class. Effect is. A chart that reliably misleads the reasonable reader is a leaning chart, full stop, and the maker's pure heart does not straighten it.

The fourth lean is the stretched picture: the same chart drawn tall and narrow to make a rise look steep, or short and wide to make the same rise look flat. The numbers and even the axis can be impeccable while the proportions do the persuading. There is no single correct proportion for a chart, which is exactly why the choice is a rhetorical one. The defence is the habit you now have: read the numbers at the ends of the line before admiring the slope. A rise from one hundred to one hundred and two is a rise of two percent, at any steepness, in any font, in any colour. Slope is theatre. Endpoints are data.

Fifth, the three-dimensional decoration and its cousins: bars drawn as cylinders, pies tilted toward the viewer, icons scaled by height when their area does the visual work. If an icon doubles in height and also doubles in width, its area quadruples, and the eye reads the area. A chart that doubles the picture's area for a doubling of the number has exaggerated by a factor of two without touching a digit. Pie charts deserve a special note. A pie chart shows parts of a whole as slices. Used for a few parts that genuinely sum to a whole — the sixty predictions dividing into thirty-nine hits and twenty-one misses — a pie is honest and clear. Used for many slices, or for numbers that do not sum to anything, it becomes a coloured puzzle in which the eye cannot rank the slices it most needs to compare. Bars rank. Pies decorate. Choose accordingly.

Now this class's set problem, which I will work in full, as is my custom. The problem: find one chart — anywhere — that leans: a cut axis, a cherry-picked range, a missing denominator. Describe in three sentences what it wants you to believe and what it hides. The worked answer is a method, not a chart: name the claim, then ask three questions — what is the denominator, what range was chosen and what was left outside it, and does the axis start where honesty starts? A chart that survives all three has earned your belief; one that fails any has told you what it is. Practise the method on this example with me. A software company advertises: customer complaints cut in half since our tool launched. Its chart shows monthly complaints falling from forty in March to twenty in June, bars drawn from an axis that starts at zero — good — across exactly those four months. Run the questions. Denominator: absent. The company's customer count is nowhere on the chart; if customers also halved in those months, the rate never moved. Range: March to June. What lies outside? July, in which complaints, let us suppose, returned to thirty-eight — outside the frame, where inconvenient months live. Axis: honest. Verdict: one pass, two failures. The chart wants you to believe the tool halved dissatisfaction. What it hides is the base the complaints came from, and the month that undid the story. Three sentences. That is the worked answer, and it is now yours to reuse on every chart anyone shows you, including — especially — charts made by people you like.

Here is your guided moment. Think of the last chart that persuaded you of something — in a meeting, in the news, in an advertisement you half-remember. Rebuild it in your mind and run the three questions slowly. Denominator: can you state it? If you cannot, the chart never gave it to you, and you were persuaded without it. Range: what would the months or cases just outside do to the picture? Axis: where did it start, and did you check at the time, or did the bars simply look the way they looked? Most people, doing this honestly, discover that the chart that persuaded them would fail at least one question. That discovery is not an embarrassment. It is the lesson working. The next chart will meet a different reader.

What does an honest chart look like, positively? It states its denominator in the title or directly under it. It shows a range long enough to include the boring parts. Its axis starts at zero, or says loudly that it does not. Its proportions are calm. Its title states the finding the numbers support — not the finding the maker wishes they supported. And it prints hits and misses together when the subject is a record: our business's predictions chart, done honestly, is sixty columns or one line with all sixty points, thirty-nine hits and twenty-one misses in the same colours of dignity, the misses not greyed, not shrunk, not exiled to a footnote. A chart of only the hits would be the visual form of the lie from lesson one: every element accurate, the collection misleading. Geometry cannot rescue a dishonest selection, and an honest selection needs no rescue. The two virtues — honest selection, honest geometry — are the whole craft. Everything else is software.

One more species deserves naming before the recap: the chart with two vertical axes, one on each side, each with its own scale, letting two lines be made to cross, kiss, or diverge at the maker's pleasure by stretching either scale. Two-axis charts are not always dishonest, but they are always adjustable, and an adjustable picture is an argument wearing a lab coat. When you meet one, cover either line with your hand and ask what the other line alone, on its own scale, actually shows. Usually: less than the pair appeared to promise. Correlation by draughtsmanship is still not causation, as lesson four would remind the chart, if charts could blush.

Let me work a second chart completely, this one drawn from our own business, so you can watch an honest chart being built and a leaning one being repaired. Take the predictions record: sixty predictions across six months, ten per month, with hits per month of six, seven, five, eight, six, and seven. Add them: six plus seven is thirteen, plus five is eighteen, plus eight is twenty-six, plus six is thirty-two, plus seven is thirty-nine. Thirty-nine hits, twenty-one misses, as always. The leaning version of this chart shows only the best month — eight hits out of ten, eighty percent — under the title Our Record-Breaking Month. Every number is true. The chart is a cherry-pick in a frame: the range is one month, chosen by the conclusion, and the other fifty predictions are outside it, unmentioned. The honest version shows all six months as bars from zero, each bar labelled with its count out of ten, under a title that states the whole: Thirty-Nine Hits and Twenty-One Misses in Sixty Predictions. The honest chart is less thrilling. It is also the one you could defend in a room full of enemies, which is my working definition of a finished chart. Thrill is available elsewhere, cheaply. Defendability is the scarce good.

Notice, in that repair, what changed and what did not. No number changed. The range widened to the whole record, the denominator — sixty, and ten per month — moved onto the chart's face, and the title began stating the whole finding instead of its best fragment. Three repairs, all of them matters of selection and labelling rather than arithmetic. This is typical. Leaning charts are rarely arithmetic failures. They are framing decisions, made one at a time, each small enough to excuse, accumulating into a picture that argues far beyond its data. Which is why the audit must also proceed one decision at a time: axis, range, denominator, proportion, title. Five checks, in that order, take under a minute. I have timed it, in classrooms, with a stopwatch I do not entirely trust, which is why I say under a minute rather than a precise figure.

A word on colour, since charts arrive coloured and colour is never neutral. Colour directs the eye: the red bar is read as the problem, the green bar as the success, before any number is consulted. A maker can paint the competitor's bar red and their own green while the values differ by a hair, and the verdict will be absorbed as decoration. Colour can also carry honest information — hits in one consistent colour, misses in another, across every chart a business publishes, so a regular reader learns the code once and reads faster forever. The rule is the same as everywhere in this lesson: decoration that argues is leaning; decoration that labels is craft. Ask of every colour what work it is doing. If the answer is that it makes the smaller number look alarming, you have found the lean.

Truncation has a quieter sibling I should name: the dual trick of the broken time axis, where equal spaces on the page represent unequal stretches of time. Months march evenly — January, February, March — and then the chart quietly skips to September, keeping the spacing, so a slow six-month change reads as a sudden jump. The line looks continuous. The time is not. Check the labels along the bottom with the same suspicion you give the numbers along the side. A chart's honesty lives as much in its spacing as in its scale, and gaps in time that are not marked as gaps are ranges cherry-picked at the level of the ruler itself.

Now, fairness to chart-makers, because this lesson could leave you merely suspicious, and suspicion alone is a poor instrument. Charts compress. Compression always discards. A chart of ten years of daily data must summarise or become wallpaper, and every summary chooses. The honest maker is not the one who discards nothing — that maker is a table — but the one whose discards are visible, stated, and not chosen by the conclusion. Show the full range in small print beside the excerpt. State the denominator in the title. Mark the axis break. These courtesies cost the maker nothing but the lean itself, which is precisely why their absence is informative. A maker who will not state the denominator is telling you the denominator would not help the argument. Believe them.

Apply that standard, finally, to the charts our business itself publishes, because this class's standard applies at home first, as lesson four insisted. The predictions chart we repaired a moment ago is the template: whole record, denominators on its face, misses at full size. The advertising chart gets the same treatment: clicks and customers side by side, so the rate cannot hide behind the count, and the range long enough to include the season's turn. A business that publishes charts it could defend in a room full of enemies will occasionally publish a chart that disappoints it. That disappointment is the receipt proving the system works. The alternative — charts that never disappoint their maker — is not a communications strategy. It is a symptom, diagnosable from the charts alone, by the methods of this lesson, in under a minute per chart. I have timed that too. The stopwatch and I have agreed to disagree about the seconds; about the diagnosis, never.

One more lean, small and ubiquitous, deserves its thirty seconds: the percentage-point shuffle. When a rate moves from two percent to three percent, that is a rise of one percentage point, and also a rise of fifty percent relative to where it started. Both statements are true. They produce entirely different feelings, and chart titles choose between them by the feeling required. A maker wanting alarm says complaints up fifty percent. A maker wanting calm says complaints up one point. The defence is mechanical and by now familiar: reconstruct the two underlying rates, with their denominators, and the shuffle loses its power. One point, fifty percent — hold both, state both, and let no title choose your emotions for you. Feelings are yours. Titles are theirs. Keep the accounts separate.

And a closing observation about why leaning works at all, which returns us to the first paragraph. The eye is fast and the mind is lazy — not as an insult, but as engineering: visual comparison costs nothing, while reading axes and hunting denominators costs effort, and effort is rationed. Leaning charts are priced for the rationing. They are built for the reader who will not spend the minute. This lesson's entire ambition is to make you a reader who spends the minute — on charts that matter, where money or belief will move because of them. You cannot audit every chart that crosses your screen, and I do not ask it. Audit the ones that ask something of you. A chart that wants nothing from you may lean in peace. A chart that wants your money, your vote, or your signature gets the three questions, the five checks, and the full minute, every time, without apology to its maker.

A last distinction, because students reliably ask it: is a chart that leans the same as a chart that lies? Not quite, and the difference matters for how you respond. A lying chart states numbers that are false. It is rare, because false numbers are checkable and the checking ruins careers. A leaning chart states numbers that are true and arranges everything else — axis, range, base, proportion, colour, title — so the true numbers argue a conclusion they do not support. It is common, because arrangement is deniable in a way digits are not. Respond accordingly. To the lying chart: rejection. To the leaning chart: repair. Ask for the full range, the denominator, the axis from zero, and watch whether the conclusion survives its own data presented honestly. Sometimes it does, and then you have gained a finding as well as a chart. More often the conclusion quietly shrinks to fit the honest picture, and the shrinking is the information you were meant not to receive. Either way, you now hold the tools. The chart's maker had them first. That advantage is hereby cancelled.

One practical drill closes the teaching, because this lesson, more than any other in the class, is a skill of the eyes and skills are built by repetition. For one week, every chart you encounter that asks something of you gets one written line in a notebook: the claim, the denominator or its absence, the range or its suspicious edges, the axis and where it starts. One line, three facts, no essays. At the week's end, read your lines back. You will see the leans cluster — the same missing denominator, the same flattering quarter — because leaning is a craft with fashions, and once you have logged a fashion twice you will spot it the third time before the chart finishes loading. That is expertise, in this domain: not a degree, not a title, but a notebook of caught attempts and the reflex they built. The businesses and publications that lean are counting on your reflex being admiration. Give them a different one.

Recap, in order. One: a chart translates numbers into geometry, and the eye believes the geometry before the mind reads the numbers. Two: a cut axis on a bar chart makes small differences look enormous; check where the axis starts before trusting any bar's length. Three: a cherry-picked range shows the stretch that agrees with the conclusion; ask what lies just outside the frame. Four: counts without denominators lean; the rate may move opposite to the count. Five: proportion, decoration, and doubled icons exaggerate without touching a digit; read endpoints, not slopes. Six: the method for any chart is three questions — denominator, range, axis — and a chart that fails any one has told you what it is. Seven: the honest chart states its base, shows the boring parts, keeps its geometry calm, and prints hits and misses together.

Before the questions, do the homework-shaped thing once, here, with me, on a chart I invent for the purpose — because the set problem asks you to find a leaning chart anywhere, and I want your first attempt supervised. A gym advertises: membership doubled in a year. Its chart shows two bars, last January and this January, the second twice the first, axis from zero, clean proportions. Run the questions. Denominator: members out of what — the town, last year's members, the people who saw the advertisement? The chart does not say, and doubled membership from a base of nine is a different event from doubled membership from a base of nine hundred. Range: January to January. What lies outside? February, when the resolution crowd traditionally departs — the chart's chosen endpoints are the two most flattering dates in the fitness calendar, which is either a coincidence or a range chosen by its conclusion, and you may form your own view. Axis: passes. Verdict: the bars are honest and the argument leans, through base and range, without a single false number. Write that verdict in three sentences and you have done the set problem once, correctly. Do it again, out there, on a chart that was not built to be caught, and the method is yours for life.

Next, the questions on this lesson, with the full text open beside them. Going back to read is learning, not cheating. Take the three questions with you in their shortest form — denominator, range, axis — the way you took lesson one's three and lesson three's four. You will notice the sets rhyme: all of them ask what was counted, out of how many, over what stretch, and what was left outside the frame. That rhyme is not an accident of my teaching. It is the shape of honesty itself, which turns out to have very few moving parts, endlessly recombined. Learn the parts once and every new disguise the lean invents will still be wearing them, visible to you, nameable by you, in the minute the chart hoped you would not spend.

You have now audited charts as a reader. The final step of this lesson is to hold the standard as a maker, because you will make charts — in a report, in a pitch, in a message to a partner — and the minute you make one, every temptation catalogued today will present itself as a formatting choice. Resist at the level of the choice, not the intention: set the axis before you look at the picture it makes, fix the range before you know which range flatters, write the denominator into the title before you see whether the title still sings. Order is the whole defence. A maker who decides the frame before seeing the picture cannot cherry-pick, because the picking would have to be done blind. That is the predictions record's discipline, translated into geometry: commit first, look second, publish the whole. Do that, and your charts will occasionally argue against you in public, which is how your readers will learn — correctly — that when your charts argue for you, they can be believed.

In lesson six, the final lesson, the subject is your own data: who holds it, what it can prove, and what it cannot. Bring your curiosity, and bring the whole course with you — the questions, the tables, the margins, the confounders, and the three chart checks — because the last lesson assembles them around the data closest to you; the rest is provided.
