# Samples and populations: asking some to learn about all

Good to see you. Today AI and Computing earns its keep. I am Professor Juhani Korhonen, and this is lesson three of six in Data — Where It Lives and What It Proves: samples and populations — asking some to learn about all. Lessons one and two gave you records and tables: observations, kept whole, in rows and columns. Today we ask what happens when you cannot observe everyone, which is most of the time, and must learn about a whole group by examining a part of it. Done properly, this is one of the great intellectual tools of the modern world. Done carelessly, it produces confident nonsense with a spreadsheet attached. We will do it properly. I write the units on the board before opinions, every time. Bring your curiosity, and bring one poll or survey you have seen this month — we will need examples of togetherness mistaken for cause, and the supply, regrettably, is not limited; the rest is provided.

Definitions first, as always. A population is the whole group you want to learn about — all of them, every one. The word does not require people. A population can be all the customers of a business, all the orders shipped last year, all the voters in a town, or all the predictions in a record. A sample is the part of the population you actually examine. A census is the special case where the sample is the population: you examined everyone. A census is exact and usually impractical. Our marketing business has eight hundred customers. It could, in principle, ask all eight hundred whether they are satisfied. That would be a census. In practice it will ask some of them, and the asking itself takes time, and some will not answer, and so it will work from a sample, as will you, in nearly every real situation you ever face.

The central question of this lesson is one sentence: how can some stand for all? The answer is also one sentence, and then we will spend the lesson earning it. A sample can stand for a population when every member of the population had a fair chance of being chosen, and the choosing was not rigged by convenience, enthusiasm, or hope. A sample chosen that way is called a random sample. Random does not mean haphazard. Random means chosen by a method that gives every member a known, equal chance and gives the chooser no say in who lands in the sample. Drawing names from a well-mixed hat is random. Asking the first fifty customers who walk in on a Saturday is not random, however innocent it feels. Saturday morning customers are not all customers. They are the customers who shop on Saturday mornings, and they differ from the others in ways you cannot see from inside the sample.

Let me make the stakes concrete with our business. The owner wants to know what share of all eight hundred customers would recommend the business to a friend. He emails a short survey to a random sample of two hundred customers — chosen by the spreadsheet, every customer equally likely, no names picked by hand. One hundred and sixty reply. Of those, one hundred and twelve say yes, they would recommend it. Divide: one hundred and twelve divided by one hundred and sixty is zero point seven. Seventy percent of the respondents said yes. Now the exact sentence the owner may say: about seventy percent of our customers would probably recommend us. Note the words about and probably, which are not weakness. They are accuracy. The sample said seventy percent. The population — all eight hundred — will not be exactly seventy percent. It might be sixty-seven. It might be seventy-three. The sample gives you an estimate, which is a number offered as close to the truth, with the honest understanding that it is not the truth itself. An estimate is not a failed measurement. It is a successful one, correctly labelled.

How close is close? That depends mainly on the size of the sample, and here is the fact that surprises everyone the first time: it depends on the size of the sample, not on the size of the population. A random sample of one hundred and sixty from eight hundred customers tells you roughly as much as a random sample of one hundred and sixty from eight hundred thousand customers. The population can grow a thousandfold and the sample's precision barely moves, provided the sample was truly random. What moves precision is the sample's own size. Small samples wobble. Large samples settle. You met the small version in lesson one: six clicks versus three clicks, out of one hundred, was noise. Here is the settled version: with a random sample of one thousand people, a result of seventy percent will usually sit within about three percentage points of the population's true value — somewhere between sixty-seven and seventy-three. That wobble has a name: the margin of error. The margin of error is the amount by which a sample result may reasonably differ from the population value, purely because you asked some instead of all. When a poll reports seventy percent with a margin of error of three points, it is saying: our best estimate is seventy, and the honest range around it runs from sixty-seven to seventy-three. A poll that reports the percentage without the margin is withholding half the finding. Ask for both, every time, the way you ask for the denominator.

Do not memorise the numbers three and one thousand as magic. Understand the shape. Quadruple the sample and you roughly halve the margin of error. A sample of one hundred carries a margin near ten points — too wide for fine decisions, fine for coarse ones. A sample of four hundred carries a margin near five points. A sample of one thousand carries a margin near three. Precision is bought with sample size, and the price rises steeply: each halving of the margin costs four times the asking. Our business, with its survey of one hundred and sixty replies, lives with a margin near eight points. Seventy percent, honestly reported, means the truth is probably somewhere between about sixty-two and seventy-eight. That is still useful. It rules out disaster and it rules out perfection, which is more knowledge than the owner had on Monday. Data rarely gives certainty. It gives narrower ignorance, and narrower ignorance, compounded over many decisions, is what competence is made of.

Now the failures, because samples fail in patterned ways and the patterns have names. The first failure is a biased sample. A sample is biased when the method of choosing systematically favours some members of the population over others, so the sample differs from the population in a direction, not merely by chance. Surveying customers through the business's social media page samples the customers who follow the page — younger, more enthusiastic, more online — and their answers will flatter the business in a consistent direction. Bias is worse than noise. Noise wobbles around the truth and shrinks as the sample grows. Bias points away from the truth and does not shrink at all: a biased sample of ten thousand is precisely, confidently wrong. Size cannot cure bias. Only the choosing method can. This is why I am unimpressed by very large samples gathered carelessly. A large biased sample is a large error with good posture.

The second failure is non-response. Non-response means the people chosen for the sample who did not answer. In our survey, two hundred were asked and one hundred and sixty replied, so forty did not. The response rate is the share who did answer: one hundred and sixty divided by two hundred is zero point eight — eighty percent. That is a good rate. Trouble begins at low rates, because non-respondents are rarely a random slice of the chosen. People with strong feelings answer surveys. People who are furious answer. People who are delighted answer. The vast, mildly content middle often does not, and so a survey with a twenty percent response rate may describe the extremes of your customers while claiming to describe your customers. When someone shows you survey results, the second question — after how was the sample chosen — is: what share of those chosen actually answered? A finding built on a small, self-selected fraction of a sample is an anecdote collection wearing a percentage, and lesson one already taught you what to call that.

The third failure is the leading question, which corrupts the sample's answers rather than its membership. A question that pushes — would you agree our friendly service deserves five stars — does not measure opinion. It manufactures it. The respondents are real, the sample may be random, and the data are still worthless, because the instrument bent every reading in one direction, like the uncalibrated scale from lesson one. Honest questions are flat: how would you rate the service, from one to five. Flat questions feel less exciting. They have the compensating virtue of measuring something.

Here is the guided moment for this lesson. Think of a claim you have heard that begins with words like most people, customers prefer, or voters want. Someone, somewhere, asked some and announced all. Reconstruct the asking. Who exactly was asked — can you picture them? How were they chosen — by a method that gave everyone a fair chance, or by whoever happened to be nearby, online, or willing? How many answered, out of how many asked? And what range would the margin of error honestly allow around the headline number? Run those four checks silently on the next such claim you meet in the wild. You will find that most claims of this kind cannot survive the second check. That is not cynicism. That is sampling theory, applied at walking pace.

Our predictions record offers a different, quieter application. Sixty predictions, thirty-nine hits, twenty-one misses — a sixty-five percent hit rate, computed in lesson two. Now ask the sampling question: what population does that record describe? It describes those sixty predictions exactly — for them, it is a census, not a sample, and sixty-five percent is not an estimate but a count. The moment someone says the record proves the business will hit sixty-five percent of future predictions, the sixty rows have been silently promoted into a sample of the future, and the future is a population with opinions of its own: different conditions, different difficulty, different luck. The honest claim is narrower and stronger: of the sixty predictions made and kept whole, thirty-nine were hits. What the future holds, the record informs but does not settle. Confusing a census of the past with a guarantee about the future is the most respectable error in this entire class, and it is still an error.

One more distinction, because it governs real money. Sometimes you do not need a sample at all. If the population is small and the records already exist — all orders last month, all complaints this year — use the records. That is a census of stored data, and it beats any survey. Sampling is for when observing everyone is impossible, expensive, or destructive. You cannot taste every bottle to check the batch. You can count every complaint in the file. Choose the census when the file exists. Choose the sample when it does not. And when you choose the sample, spend your effort on the choosing — random, fair, documented — because no arithmetic performed afterwards can repair a choosing that was rigged at the start. Arithmetic is powerful. It is not resurrection.

Let me also give you the phrase that protects you in meetings: representative. A representative sample is one that resembles the population in the respects that matter for the question — similar mix of new and old customers, of big and small orders, of every group the conclusion will be applied to. Random choosing is how you earn representativeness without hand-arranging it. The moment someone hand-arranges a sample to look representative — picking, by eye, a nice mix — they have reintroduced their own judgement into the choosing, and their judgement, however experienced, is a bias with a biography. Trust the hat. Trust the spreadsheet's random draw. Do not trust the hand, including your own. Especially your own, because yours is the hand whose biases you cannot see.

A worked example from election polling will fix the margin of error in your mind better than any definition. A town has fifty thousand voters. A polling firm draws a random sample of one thousand of them and finds that five hundred and twenty intend to vote for one candidate. Five hundred and twenty divided by one thousand is zero point five two — fifty-two percent. The firm reports fifty-two percent, margin of error three points. The honest reading: the candidate's true support in the town is probably between forty-nine and fifty-five percent. Note what follows and what does not. It does not follow that the candidate leads, because the opponent's forty-eight percent carries the same margin, and the two ranges overlap. It does not follow that the candidate will win, because intentions are not votes and three weeks remain. What follows is narrower ignorance, exactly as promised: support is somewhere near half, the race is close, and anyone announcing a certain outcome from this poll is selling certainty the sample never contained. When the sample grows to four thousand, the margin shrinks to about one and a half points, and the same fifty-two percent becomes a genuinely informative lead. Same town. Same percentage. Different sample size, different knowledge. The percentage is the headline. The sample size is the news.

Now, a technique our business can actually afford: stratification. Stratified sampling means dividing the population into groups — strata — and sampling from each group deliberately, so no group is missed by chance. The business's customers divide naturally into new customers, under a year, and established customers, a year or more. A pure random sample of two hundred from eight hundred might, by luck, draw only thirty new customers, too few to say anything about them separately. A stratified sample draws, say, one hundred new and one hundred established, on purpose, and can then speak about each group as well as about the whole, combining the groups in their true proportions when it reports the total. Stratification is not the hand-picking I warned against. The groups are defined in advance, every member still has a fair chance within their group, and the proportions are corrected in the arithmetic, openly. The difference between stratification and rigging is the same as the difference between a recipe and a guess: written down in advance, applied the same way every time, and available for inspection.

Let me also name the sample you should never trust, however large it grows: the convenience sample. A convenience sample is whoever was easiest to reach — the customers who happened to visit this week, the followers who happened to see the post, the friends who happened to answer the message. Convenience samples are not worthless for every purpose. They are excellent for discovering that a problem exists: if twelve of this week's visitors independently mention the same broken page, the page is broken, and you need no random sample to justify fixing it. They are worthless for measuring how common anything is, because ease of reaching is itself a filter, and the filter's shape is unknown. Existence, yes. Prevalence, no. Prevalence means how widespread a thing is in the population. Keep those two jobs separate and convenience samples will serve you honestly in the first and never betray you in the second.

There is one more quiet assumption to drag into the light: the population must be defined before the sample means anything. All our customers sounds precise until you ask whether it includes the customer who bought once, nine years ago, and moved abroad. Or the forty ghosts from lesson two — the duplicates still sitting in the table. A sample drawn from a dirty table inherits the dirt. Seven hundred and sixty real people and forty duplicate rows will be sampled as if they were eight hundred people, and the duplicated customers will be asked twice as often as they should be, in proportion to their ghostliness. This is why lesson two came before lesson three. Clean tables first. Fair samples second. Conclusions third, in that order, and any consultant who proposes reversing the order should be charged by the hour for the entertainment.

A word about very small populations, because small businesses live there. If the population is forty — forty major clients, say — a sample of twenty is not a shortcut. It is half a census performed to avoid the other half, and the margin of error on twenty is wide enough to embarrass the saving. With small populations, either examine everyone or state plainly that you examined a part and the part may mislead. There is no third option, and sampling theory, which is generous to large populations, offers small ones no discount. The honest small-business move is usually the census: forty clients can be telephoned. Eight hundred customers cannot, not by Friday. Let the size of the population choose the method, and let the method be written down before the first call is made.

Finally, the sentence I most want installed. Whenever a number about all is built from some, the finding is not the number. The finding is the number, plus the size of the sample, plus how the sample was chosen, plus the share who answered. Four parts. A report missing any of the four is a story with pages torn out, and you now know enough to notice the tearing. Demand the missing pages politely, in writing, before money moves. If they cannot be produced, you have learned something about the report that the report was designed to prevent you learning. Price it accordingly.

One more worked check, on the advertising numbers, because sampling hides there too. The business shows its advertisement to a sample of the town — say the platform delivers it to two thousand people chosen by the platform's own machinery — and one hundred and forty click. One hundred and forty divided by two thousand is zero point zero seven: a seven percent click rate. The owner wants to say seven percent of the town would click. Stop him, kindly. The two thousand were not a random sample of the town. The platform chose them by its own rules, which favour people likely to click, because platforms are paid when people click. The seven percent is a true fact about those two thousand deliveries. As an estimate of the town, it is biased upward by an amount nobody can compute, because the choosing method is a trade secret. This is the modern shape of sampling failure: the sample is enormous, the arithmetic is perfect, and the choosing belongs to someone whose interests differ from yours. Size cannot cure bias. I said it earlier and I repeat it here, because this is the form in which you will meet it monthly for the rest of your working life.

And a brief honourable mention for the pilot. A pilot study is a small sample used to test the method itself — does the survey question confuse people, does the form work, how long does an interview take — before the real sample is drawn. Pilots are not findings. A pilot of twenty customers that returns fourteen yes answers has taught you that the question can be asked, not that seventy percent of customers agree. Confusing a pilot with a finding is a category error I have watched grown companies commit in print. Run pilots. Learn from them. Publish nothing from them except what the method taught you.

A short historical note, because it shows the price of ignoring all of this at national scale. In nineteen thirty-six, a famous magazine polled over two million people and predicted a landslide winner in an American presidential election. The sample was gigantic. It was also drawn from car registrations and telephone directories, in a depression, when cars and telephones belonged disproportionately to the wealthier half of the country. The poorer half voted the other way, and the magazine's prediction failed completely, while a rival using a far smaller, properly drawn sample called the result correctly. Two million biased answers lost to a few thousand fair ones. I tell this story in every sampling class I teach, because students remember stories longer than rules, and this story is the rule: the choosing is the science. The counting is arithmetic. Anyone can count. Choosing fairly is the discipline, and it is decided before a single answer arrives, by a person, in advance, in writing — or it is decided afterwards, by the bias, without asking anyone's permission.

One distinction remains, small in wording and large in consequence: sampling error versus every other kind of error. Sampling error is the wobble that remains even when everything is done right — the gap between a fair sample and its population, caused by chance alone. The margin of error measures that wobble and nothing else. It does not cover a leading question, a biased draw, a dirty table, or a low response rate. Those are separate failures, unmeasured by the margin, and usually larger. This is why a reported margin of error can coexist with a badly wrong result: the margin honestly describes the one error that was controlled, while the uncontrolled errors stand offstage, unpriced. When you read a poll, read the margin as a floor under the uncertainty, never as the whole of it. The whole of it is the margin plus everything the method got wrong, and the method section — how they chose, how many answered — is where those errors are confessed, for readers patient enough to look. Be that reader. It takes four minutes, and it is the difference between consuming a finding and auditing one.

Recap, in order. One: the population is the whole group you want to learn about; the sample is the part you actually examine; a census examines everyone. Two: a sample stands for a population when the choosing is random — every member with a fair chance, and no hand on the scale. Three: a sample gives an estimate, not the exact population value, and the margin of error states honestly how far off it may be. Four: precision grows with sample size, not population size, and each halving of the margin costs roughly four times the sample. Five: bias is a choosing method that points away from the truth, and no sample size cures it. Six: watch the response rate — those who did not answer are data too, by their absence. Seven: a record of the past is a census of that past; treating it as a guarantee about the future is an error, however respectable.

Before the questions, fix the four checks in order, because order is a memory aid and this set deserves one. First: who was asked, exactly — can you picture the actual people, or only a fog with a percentage in it? Second: how were they chosen — by a method that gave every member of the population a fair chance, or by convenience, enthusiasm, or an algorithm with its own interests? Third: how many answered, out of how many asked — and is the gap between those numbers small enough to ignore, which it rarely is? Fourth: what range does the margin of error honestly allow — and does the headline conclusion still stand at the far edge of that range, or does it quietly fall over? A claim that survives all four checks has earned provisional belief, which is the strongest currency this class deals in. A claim that fails the second check has failed entirely, whatever its sample size, because bias does not shrink. Hold that hierarchy — choosing first, size second — and you will read every survey, poll, and platform report for the rest of your life with better eyes than the people who published them expected you to have. That is the lesson. The questions merely check whether it landed.

Next, the questions on this lesson. The full text stays open beside them, and going back to read is learning, not cheating. Carry the four parts in your pocket the way you carry the three questions from lesson one, because the two sets work together: the three questions test whether data exists at all, and the four parts test whether a sample's number deserves to travel from some to all. Most published findings fail one set or the other within a minute of honest inspection, and the minute is never wasted, because what survives it can be built on.

One sentence to take with you, and then we are done. Ask some, by a fair method, in sufficient number, and report the range as faithfully as the result — and some will teach you about all, within stated limits, which is all that honest knowledge ever claims.
