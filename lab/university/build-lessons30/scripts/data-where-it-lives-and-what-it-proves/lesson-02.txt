# Tables, records, and the humble spreadsheet

Good to see you. Today AI and Computing earns its keep. I am Professor Juhani Korhonen, and this is lesson two of six in Data — Where It Lives and What It Proves: tables, records, and the humble spreadsheet. In lesson one we established what data is: recorded observations, kept whole, that someone else can check. Today we look at where those records actually live, and at the shape that makes them useful instead of merely stored. I write the units on the board before opinions, every time. The unit on the board today is structure. Bring your curiosity, and if you have a messy list of your own somewhere, bring that too — by the end of the hour it will have rows, columns, and no excuses; the rest is provided.

A table is data arranged in rows and columns. That is the whole definition, and it is enough. A row is one horizontal line: one case, one person, one order, one day — one thing, with its facts beside it. A column is one vertical line: one kind of fact, recorded the same way for every row. In our small marketing business, the customer list is a table. Each row is one customer. The columns are name, email address, the date the person first bought, and what they bought. Four columns. Perhaps eight hundred rows. Nothing about this is glamorous, and everything about this class depends on it, because a table with honest rows and consistent columns can answer questions. A pile of notes cannot, or not without a weekend of archaeology first.

The single most important rule of tables is this: one row, one thing. If a row is a customer, every row is a customer — not a customer, then an order, then a note about a phone call, all mixed in one list. Mixing kinds of things in one table is the original sin of small-business data. It feels harmless while you type. It becomes expensive the first time you ask the table a question, because the table can no longer tell you what its rows mean. The second rule follows: one column, one kind of fact, in one format. If the date column holds dates, it holds dates — the same style of date, every row. The day a column starts holding dates in one row, amounts of money in another, and the word soon in a third, it stops being a column and becomes a drawer. Drawers are for socks. Data belongs in columns.

A record is one complete row: all the facts you keep about one case. A field is one cell: the single fact where a row and a column meet — this customer's email address, that order's date. These three words — record, field, table — are the entire vocabulary of this lesson's first half, and they are worth having exactly. When a colleague says the data is a mess, they usually mean one of three precise things: records are missing, fields are inconsistent, or two tables that should agree do not. Naming which one it is turns a complaint into a task. Tasks can be finished. Complaints cannot.

Now, the humble spreadsheet. A spreadsheet is a program that holds a table and does arithmetic on it: adding columns, sorting rows, filtering to the rows that match a condition, and computing totals, averages, and percentages. It is humble because it sits on nearly every computer, costs little or nothing, and excites nobody. It is also, used honestly, the most powerful data tool a small business owns. Databases and dashboards exist, and we will speak of them shortly, but the spreadsheet is where understanding starts, because in a spreadsheet you can see the rows. Seeing the rows is a virtue. Systems that hide the rows from you also hide the errors from you, and errors prefer privacy.

Let us do real work. Our business wants to know what share of its customers come back for a second purchase. In the spreadsheet, the customer table has eight hundred rows — eight hundred customers. A column marks whether each customer bought a second time: yes or no. Count the yes rows: two hundred and forty. Now the arithmetic, done slowly, because this is the calculation half the business world gets quietly wrong. A proportion is the part divided by the whole. Two hundred and forty divided by eight hundred is zero point three. A percentage is a proportion multiplied by one hundred. Zero point three times one hundred is thirty. So thirty percent of customers bought a second time. The denominator — the whole — is eight hundred, stated out loud, as lesson one taught you. If anyone reports that figure as repeat business is thirty percent without the eight hundred standing next to it, send them back to lesson one. I will wait.

Sorting and filtering are the two moves you will use daily. Sorting means rearranging rows by one column: customers by date, orders by size, complaints by day of the week. Sorting is how patterns become visible — the ten largest orders at the top of the list tell a story that the same orders scattered through eight hundred rows do not. Filtering means showing only the rows that meet a condition: only customers from one town, only orders from March, only the predictions record rows marked miss. Filtering is how you ask a table a narrower question. Notice that filtering does not delete. The hidden rows still exist. The moment a filter starts being treated as the whole table, you have built yourself a survivorship trap of the kind lesson one described — you are studying the cases that passed your filter and forgetting the rest exist. Filters are lenses, not editors.

Totals and averages come next, and averages need care. An average, in this class, means the mean unless I say otherwise: add all the values, divide by how many values there are. Our business sold five orders on Monday: ten dollars, twenty dollars, thirty dollars, forty dollars, and one hundred dollars. The total is two hundred dollars. The count is five. Two hundred divided by five is forty. The average order is forty dollars. True, and quietly misleading, because four of the five orders were below it. One large order pulled the average upward like a magnet. For such data there is a second summary: the median, which is the middle value when the values are lined up in order. Line them up: ten, twenty, thirty, forty, one hundred. The middle one is thirty. The median order is thirty dollars. Neither number lies. They answer different questions. The mean tells you where the money is. The median tells you what a typical order looks like. A report that gives you only the mean, for data with a few very large values in it, is leaning — politely, arithmetically, but leaning. Lesson five will deal with leaning in full. For now, when values vary wildly, ask for both numbers. Two numbers, honestly given, are harder to lean with than one.

Now the unglamorous half of the lesson, which is the half that determines whether any of this works: keeping the table clean. Four diseases afflict every real table, and I will name them exactly. Duplicates: the same customer entered twice, perhaps once as Robert Smith and once as Bob Smith, so your eight hundred customers are really seven hundred and sixty people and forty ghosts. Ghosts receive mail, inflate counts, and make percentages wrong in a direction nobody notices. Missing values: fields left empty — no date, no address. An empty field is not a zero. Zero is a fact. Empty is an absence, and treating an absence as a zero corrupts every average it touches. Inconsistent entries: the same town written three ways, so a filter for one spelling silently misses the other two. And stale records: addresses of people who moved two years ago, customers marked active who asked to leave. Stale data is data that was true once. It is the hardest disease to see, because every field looks plausible.

The cure is not brilliance. The cure is a routine: decide the columns, decide the format for each column, enter data the same way every time, and spend a short regular session removing duplicates and filling or flagging gaps. I will not prescribe the length of the session, because your table is not mine. I will prescribe the regularity. Tables decay the way gardens do — gradually, then visibly. A small weeding, often, beats a heroic rescue, rarely. Also, keep a copy. Before any large cleanup or change, save a copy of the table as it was, with the date in its name. A copy is cheap. Regret is expensive, and undo buttons have limits that regret does not respect.

Here is the guided moment for this lesson. Picture a table you actually use — a contacts list, a budget, a list of anything. Name its rows out loud: one row is one what? Then name its columns: what kind of fact lives in each? Now find, in your mind, one violation: a row that is not the same kind of thing as the others, a column holding two kinds of facts, a duplicate you already suspect. One violation is enough. You have just audited a table, which is a professional act performed with no equipment. If you found no violation, either your table is unusually well kept or you should look again after this lesson ends. Both outcomes are informative.

Two tables deserve a word before we close, because our business lives in them. The first is the predictions record from lesson one, and now you can see its anatomy: each row is one prediction, made on one date. The columns are the date, what was predicted, what actually happened, and the verdict — hit or miss. Four columns, no decoration. Because every prediction is a row and the miss rows are never deleted, the hit rate is a simple count: hits divided by all rows, misses included. That is why the table's shape is its honesty. Delete the miss rows and the same arithmetic produces a perfect record and a worthless one. The second table is the advertising log: each row is one advertisement on one day, with columns for how many people saw it, how many clicked, and what it cost. Three honest columns there let the business compute, any evening, the cost per click: total cost divided by total clicks. If an advertisement cost fifty dollars and produced one hundred clicks, fifty divided by one hundred is zero point five — fifty cents per click. Whether fifty cents is good depends on what a click is worth, which is a business question, not an arithmetic one. Arithmetic answers how much. It does not answer whether you should. Remember which questions belong to which department.

A word on databases, briefly, so the term does not intimidate you later. A database is a system for holding many tables and enforcing their rules — formats, required fields, no duplicates of a chosen key — automatically, at a scale a spreadsheet cannot comfortably reach. When our business has eight hundred customers, a spreadsheet is sufficient and superior, because the owner can see everything. When it has eight hundred thousand, a database earns its keep. The concepts do not change: rows, columns, records, fields. Only the machinery grows. Anyone who tells you their database has transcended rows and columns is selling something. Rows and columns are not a limitation of small tools. They are the shape of organised fact.

Finally, a warning about the most dangerous spreadsheet feature: the silent edit. Anyone can change a number in a cell, and the table keeps no memory of the change. This is convenient and hazardous in equal measure. For any table that matters, keep the raw version untouched — the table as the data arrived — and do your sorting, filtering, and correcting in a working copy. Raw means unedited, exactly as collected. Working copy means the version you may improve. If your improvements turn out to be mistakes, the raw version lets you start again. Professionals separate these two without thinking. Amateurs merge them and then argue from memory about what the original said, which brings us back to lesson one: memory is a poor storage device. The raw table is the record. Guard it.

Let me slow down on one calculation, because it is the one our business will repeat weekly: the hit rate on the predictions record, done with the table open in front of us. Suppose the record holds sixty rows — sixty predictions, each with its verdict. Count the hit rows: thirty-nine. Count the miss rows: twenty-one. Check the addition before anything else: thirty-nine plus twenty-one is sixty, which matches the number of rows, so no verdict is missing and none is counted twice. That check takes five seconds and catches a category of error that embarrasses professionals. Now divide: thirty-nine divided by sixty is zero point six five. Times one hundred: sixty-five percent. The honest sentence is: sixty-five percent of sixty predictions were hits, twenty-one were misses, and here is the whole list. Every number in that sentence can be traced to rows. That traceability — from a headline figure down to the individual records under it — is what separates a table's answer from a person's impression. If a figure cannot be traced to rows, it did not come from the table. It came from someone, and someone is not a source.

The same discipline applies to money. The advertising log for one month holds thirty rows, one per day. Add the cost column: the month cost four hundred and fifty dollars. Add the clicks column: nine hundred clicks. Cost per click for the month is four hundred and fifty divided by nine hundred, which is zero point five — fifty cents, the same figure as before, now built from a whole month instead of a single day. Notice what the table gave us: not just the answer, but the audit trail. If the owner disputes fifty cents, you do not argue. You open the rows and add again, together. Tables end arguments that opinions prolong. This is not a small benefit in a business, or in a family, or anywhere else people share money.

Now consider what happens when two tables must agree. The customer table says eight hundred customers. The order table — one row per order — holds one thousand one hundred orders, each naming the customer who placed it. Ask the order table how many distinct customers appear in it, and suppose the answer is seven hundred and ninety. Ten customers in the customer table have never ordered, or their orders were recorded under a duplicate name, or the customer table carries ten ghosts. The disagreement is not a failure. The disagreement is information: it tells you exactly where to look. Two honest tables that disagree teach you more than one table that has never been checked against anything. Cross-checking — comparing what two records say about the same world — is the cheapest audit in existence, and the spreadsheet makes it a ten-minute job. Do it before you trust either table with a decision.

A practical word on naming and dates, because more tables die of sloppiness here than of any disease I listed. Name files so a stranger can find the right one: the table's subject, and the date it was saved, in the name itself. Inside the table, use one date style. I do not mind which style you choose. I mind deeply that you choose one. A column where January the fifth appears as five one, one five, and the fifth of January will sort wrongly, filter wrongly, and eventually be read wrongly by a tired person at a consequential moment. Consistency looks like pedantry from the outside. From the inside, it is load-bearing. The same applies to names of people and places: pick the spelling the person uses, use it every time, and let the spreadsheet's find function confirm there are no strays before you report any count that depends on it.

One more summary measure belongs in your kit, because tables invite it and novices misuse it: the range. The range is the largest value minus the smallest. Our Monday orders ran from ten dollars to one hundred dollars, so the range is ninety dollars. The range tells you the spread — how far apart the extremes sit. It is honest and fragile in equal measure: one unusual order moves it completely. Report it, by all means, but report it beside the median, which the unusual order cannot bully. A typical honest summary of a column is three numbers together: the median for the typical case, the mean for where the total sits, and the range for how wide the spread runs. Three numbers, each doing one job, none pretending to do the others. That is what a summary is supposed to be: smaller than the table, faithful to the table.

And a final caution, about empty rows and totals rows living inside the table itself. If your table contains a totals row — a row that is the sum of the other rows — then any count of rows counts the total as if it were a case, and any sum of the column adds the total to itself. I have seen a business report nine hundred customers from a table of eight hundred customers, one totals row, and ninety-nine duplicates it had not yet removed. The arithmetic was flawless. The table was lying about what its rows were. Keep totals outside the data, or clearly below a blank line, and count your rows before you compute anything. One row, one thing. The totals row is not a thing. It is arithmetic wearing a row's clothing.

You now hold the whole of lesson two, and it is deliberately unglamorous: rows, columns, records, fields, four diseases, a dated copy, a raw version kept safe. Unglamorous is not a criticism. Foundations are unglamorous. The glamorous failures I am called to examine, in business after business, stand on tables nobody weeded.

One more idea earns its place before the recap, because it decides whether a table grows gracefully or collapses: the key. A key is a field that identifies each row uniquely — a customer number, an order number, something that belongs to that row and no other. Names are poor keys. Two customers can share a name, and one customer can change theirs. A number assigned once, never reused, never edited, is a good key. With keys, two tables can be joined: the order table's customer number finds the customer's row in the customer table, and suddenly you can ask questions neither table answers alone — which town produces the largest orders, which month brought the customers who stayed longest. Joining is powerful and dangerous in the usual proportion: a join on a sloppy key manufactures confident nonsense at industrial speed. Check the key first. Then join. In that order, every time.

Also, briefly: a table should record when it was last updated, and by what rule new rows arrive. A customer table fed by the website behaves differently from one typed up after trade shows. The provenance of a table — where its rows came from — belongs written at the top of the file, in plain words, where a future reader cannot miss it. Provenance means the origin and history of a thing. Future readers include future you, who will have forgotten, as we established in lesson one. Write it for them. They are relying on you.

Let me work one filtering example completely, because filtering is where beginners first feel a table answer back. The owner asks: of the customers who joined this year, how many bought a second time? Start with the full table: eight hundred rows. Filter one: keep only rows whose first-purchase date falls in this year. Suppose three hundred rows remain. Those three hundred are now your whole — your denominator — for this question only. Filter two is not needed; instead, count within those rows the ones marked yes for a second purchase. Suppose the count is one hundred and five. Divide: one hundred and five divided by three hundred is zero point three five — thirty-five percent. Compare honestly with the whole-table figure from earlier: thirty percent of all eight hundred customers bought twice, but thirty-five percent of this year's three hundred did. Both are correct. They answer different questions because their denominators differ. Most arguments about percentages in business are, underneath, arguments about which denominator was quietly chosen. Bring the denominator into the open and half of those arguments end on the spot. I regard that as a public service.

And one sorting story, short, because it shows what sorting is for. Sort the predictions record by date and read down the verdict column. Early rows: miss, hit, miss, hit, hit. Later rows: hit, hit, miss, hit, hit, hit. Nothing in that glance proves the business improved — sixty rows is a modest count, and lesson three will teach you how modest — but the sorted table lets you see the sequence, and sequence is where improvement, if it exists, would live. Unsorted, the same facts are a soup. Sorted, they are a story you can interrogate. That is the entire case for the humble spreadsheet: it turns soup into sequence, piles into tables, and impressions into counts that can be traced back to rows. No magic. Just structure, kept.

A closing practical note on sharing tables, since our business will send its record to others and receive theirs. When you send a table, send the whole table, not the filtered view you happened to be admiring. A filtered view sent as if it were the whole is how honest people accidentally mislead: the recipient computes percentages against a denominator they cannot see is partial. If you must send a part, label it a part, in the file name and at the top of the sheet, with the size of the whole stated beside it. Thirty-nine hits out of sixty rows, twenty-one of them misses, travels honestly. Thirty-nine hits, alone, is a number on tour without its chaperone. We met that failure in lesson one as the missing denominator. In tables it has an address: it lives in the filtered view, exported carelessly. You know its address now. Do not forward its mail.

Recap, in order. One: a table is rows and columns — one row per case, one column per kind of fact, in one format. Two: a record is one complete row, and a field is one cell where a row and a column meet. Three: a spreadsheet holds the table and does the arithmetic — sorting to reveal, filtering to narrow, never to delete from your thinking. Four: a proportion is the part divided by the whole, and a percentage is that, times one hundred — thirty percent of our eight hundred customers bought twice, and the denominator travels with the figure. Five: the mean is the total divided by the count; the median is the middle value; when a few values are huge, ask for both. Six: tables suffer duplicates, missing values, inconsistent entries, and staleness; the cure is a routine, plus a dated copy before any large change. Seven: keep the raw table untouched and work in a copy.

One habit to take away, small enough to survive a busy week. Every time you open a table that matters, spend the first minute counting its rows and reading its column headings aloud, even silently. That minute tells you what the table claims to be. Every error in this lesson — the totals row counted as a customer, the filtered view mistaken for the whole, the duplicate inflating the count — announces itself in that first minute to a person who counts rows before computing anything. Professionals are not faster than you. They simply check the load-bearing things first, in a fixed order, every time. This lesson has given you the order. Use it in that order and the humble spreadsheet will repay you for years.

Next, the questions on this lesson, about what was taught here and nothing else. The full text stays open beside them. Going back to read is learning, not cheating. Keep the raw file, keep the dated copy, and keep the miss rows exactly where they belong: in the table, counted. A table kept this way will still contain errors — all real tables do — but they will be the findable kind, sitting in named columns, in consistent formats, where a careful person with a spreadsheet and a free hour can hunt them down one by one. That is all structure ever promises, and it is plenty. It will not make a dishonest person honest, and it will not rescue a question that was never worth asking, but it will make honesty easy and dishonesty visible, which in practice is most of what a working business, or a working science, ever gets from its tools.
