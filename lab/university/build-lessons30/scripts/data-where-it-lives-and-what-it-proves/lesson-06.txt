# Your own data: who holds it, and what it can prove

Good to see you. Today AI and Computing earns its keep. I am Professor Juhani Korhonen, and this is lesson six of six in Data — Where It Lives and What It Proves: your own data — who holds it, and what it can prove. For five lessons the data belonged to a small marketing business: its customer list, its advertising numbers, its predictions record. Today the subject is closer. You generate data every day — when you buy, when you search, when you move through the world with a phone in your pocket — and almost none of it lives with you. This lesson maps where it lives, who holds it, what it can honestly prove about you, and what it cannot prove, however confidently someone reads it. I write the units on the board before opinions, every time. Bring your curiosity; the rest is provided.

Begin with a definition you already own. Data is recorded observation. Your data is the recorded observations of you: what you bought, where you were, what you clicked, what you searched for, whom you called, and when. Each of those is a row in somebody's table — lesson two's rows and columns, with you as the case. A record of this kind has a holder: the person or organisation that keeps the table. The holder of your data is rarely you. The shop holds the record of the sale. The phone company holds the record of the call. The platform holds the record of the click. You were present for every one of these events. Presence is not possession. That sentence explains most of the modern world, and we will spend the lesson on its consequences.

Let us inventory one ordinary day, slowly, because the length of the list is itself the lesson. You wake and check the weather on your phone: a record exists of the request, the time, and roughly where the phone was. You buy coffee with a card: the card company and the coffee shop both hold a record — the amount, the time, the place. You drive to work past a camera that reads number plates: a record. You search for a symptom, a gift, a train time: the search company holds each query, in order, with the hour attached. You message a friend: the messaging service holds the fact of the message, its time, and its size, and depending on the service, its contents. You stream music in the evening: a record of every track, every skip, every pause. By midnight, the day has produced, conservatively, hundreds of recorded observations of you, held by perhaps a dozen organisations, in tables you will never see, under rules you did not write. I am not describing a conspiracy. I am describing bookkeeping. Bookkeeping at scale, about everyone, retained for years — but bookkeeping, with all of bookkeeping's ordinary virtues and ordinary risks.

Who holds it, then, in categories. First, the organisations you deal with directly: shops, banks, employers, doctors, schools, and the government offices that tax you and license you. Their records exist because a transaction or a duty required them, and they are usually the most accurate data about you in existence, because errors in them cause immediate trouble that somebody must fix. Second, the platforms: the search company, the social networks, the video and music services, the map on your phone. You pay these with attention and information rather than money, and their tables are the widest — not the deepest facts about any single event, but observations across your whole day and year. Third, the brokers. A data broker is a company whose business is buying, combining, and selling records about people it has never dealt with directly. You have no account with a broker. You cannot picture its office. It may nevertheless hold a file on you assembled from purchases, subscriptions, public records, and platform scraps — a table built entirely from other people's rows about you. Fourth, your own devices, which hold more than most people realise: the phone keeps its own logs, the browser keeps its history, the photos carry the time and often the place they were taken, written into the file itself. That last category is the only one where the holder is you, and it is larger than you think. We will return to it, because it is the foundation of the only practical advice this lesson gives.

Now combine the categories, because combination is where the power and the trouble both live. Separately, each holder knows a slice: the shop knows the purchase, the platform knows the search, the phone company knows the tower your phone spoke to. Combined — sold, shared, breached, or lawfully requested — the slices assemble into a pattern of life: where you live and work, when you sleep, what you buy, what worries you at three in the morning, whom you know. No single record says any of that. The pattern emerges from the joining of tables, exactly the operation lesson two taught you with customer numbers and order numbers, performed here with you as the key. A key, you will recall, is a field that identifies each row uniquely. Your name, your phone number, your email address, and the identifying numbers your devices broadcast serve as keys across other people's tables. That is why a breach at a company you barely remember joining can matter: it is rarely the slice that harms. It is the key, escaping, that lets your slices be joined by strangers.

So much for who holds it. Now the second half of the title: what can it prove? Apply the standards of this class, without favour, because the subject is you. Your data can prove transactions: that this card bought this item at this time in this place. Transaction records are strong data — recorded at the moment, by a rule, with money attached to their accuracy. Your data can prove presence, approximately: that this phone was near this tower, that this card was used in this town. Approximately is the operative word, and honest readers keep it attached. Your data can prove sequences: searches made in an order, purchases across a season, journeys between places. Sequences are where records become stories, and stories, as lesson four taught you, are where causation gets pasted onto correlation by interested speakers. Hold that thought; we will need it in two minutes.

Here is what your data cannot prove, and this list matters more. It cannot prove intent. A search records the query, not the reason for it: the same search is made by the worried, the curious, the novelist researching a character, and the student writing an essay. The record is identical. The intents are opposites. Anyone who reads a motive off a log is adding the motive themselves and billing the log for it. It cannot prove attention: a video playing is a record of a screen, not of eyes, still less of agreement. It cannot prove character. A person's records show what they did, in the places that keep records, under the circumstances that produced the records. The unrecorded remainder of a life — the kindnesses done in cash and in person, the thoughts never searched for, the visits never logged — does not appear, and its absence from the table is not evidence about its size. Survivorship's mirror image, from lesson one: the table shows the recorded, and the recorded is a biased sample of a life, biased toward the digital, the commercial, and the recent. Conclusions about a whole person drawn from their data commit the exact error lesson three named: treating a sample — a slanted, convenience sample — as the population.

The predictions record returns here, for the last time, as the contrast that clarifies everything. Our business's record could prove what it proved because it was made in advance, kept whole, and kept by the business itself, for its own questions. Your data, as held by others, fails each of those tests in instructive ways. It was not made for your questions; it was made for the holder's questions — billing, advertising, security — and it answers yours only incidentally, when it answers them at all. It is not kept whole for you: holders keep what serves them, delete what does not, and show you, at most, an edited view — a dashboard, a history page — which is a filtered view of the kind lesson two warned you never to mistake for the whole table. And it was not made in advance for the claims later built on it: a log assembled for billing will be read, years later, as if it were a diary, which it is not. A diary records what its writer meant to say. A log records what a system happened to notice. Reading a log as a diary is the characteristic data error of our time, committed daily by employers, insurers, journalists, and governments, in ascending order of consequence.

Let me give you the guided moment for this lesson, and it is a practical one. Take one service you use daily — the search company, the music service, the map. Find, after this lesson, the page where it shows you your history: nearly every platform has one, usually under words like activity, history, or your data. Read it as you have been trained to read. Ask lesson one's three questions of it. What exactly was observed — the query, or the reason for the query? How much is here, and over what period? And is this the whole record, or the part the holder chooses to display? Then ask lesson five's question: what would this page look like as a chart, and what would the chart hide? You will learn two things in ten minutes: how much of your life is written down in that one table, and how carefully the display has been arranged to feel smaller than the table. Both lessons are worth the ten minutes. Do not skip the second because the first is interesting.

What, then, can actually be done? This class deals in what is true, not in comfort, so the honest answer is: less than you would like, more than nothing, in a specific order. First, know that in many places the law gives you rights over your data — to see a copy of what a holder keeps about you, to correct what is wrong, and in some places to ask for deletion. The details differ by country and by state, they change, and I am a physicist, not a lawyer; treat that sentence as a direction to look, not as legal advice. The looking is worthwhile: a request for your own file is the one audit of other people's tables about you that the system itself provides for. Second, correct what is wrong where you find it. Your file at a broker or a bank is a table, and tables carry duplicates, stale addresses, and other people's rows merged into yours — lesson two's four diseases, with your name on the folder. A wrong row in a table used for decisions about credit, work, or insurance is not an abstraction. Third, reduce what you generate where the cost to you is low: the permission a new application does not need, the account created for a single purchase, the history that can be set to expire. Do this without theatre. You are adjusting a flow, not escaping a system; the system is also the ambulance dispatch and the bank fraud alert, and a lesson that taught you to fear all recording would be a worse lesson than this one.

Fourth — and this is the point I most want kept — keep your own records. The business in our examples survived its confident moments because it held its own table: the predictions record, written in advance, hits and misses together. The personal equivalent is modest and entirely within your power. Keep your own copies of the documents that matter: contracts, receipts for large purchases, correspondence about money and work, the records of your own qualifications and your own creative work, with dates. Keep them in a place you control, in formats you can open, with a copy somewhere else against fire and failure. When a dispute ever comes — about what was agreed, what was paid, what you made and when — the party with records does not argue from memory. Memory is a poor storage device; that was lesson one, and it was always, also, about you. Your own table, kept whole, is the only dataset about your life whose choosing, keeping, and completeness you control. It will never be the largest table about you. It can be the most honest one, and in the rooms where it matters, honest and whole beats large.

One more application of the class's tools, because your data will be summarised back to you, constantly, and the summaries deserve auditing. Platforms will show you your year in review: your top songs, your most visited places, your streaks and totals. These are charts from lesson five, drawn from samples from lesson three, wearing correlations from lesson four. The totals will be counts without denominators — hours listened, without the hours awake. The categories will be the holder's categories, chosen for the holder's purposes, flattering in the direction of continued use. Enjoy them as entertainment; they are often charming. Do not mistake them for a portrait. A portrait of you drawn from your data would include the gaps, the unrecorded rooms, the denominator of your whole lived time — and no holder has those rows, because you lived them off the record, which is the normal human condition and, on the evidence of this lesson, a healthy one.

Let me also state the limit in the other direction, symmetrically, as this class insists. Your data being incomplete does not make it useless, and its holders are not villains in a story I am selling you. The same records that can be misread as a diary also catch the fraudster using your card in another country, find the phone you lost, and prove — when you need it proved — that you were somewhere, that you paid, that you sent the message before the deadline. Data about you is evidence, and evidence serves whoever reads it carefully, including you. The skill this whole class has been building — ask what was observed, count the denominator, hunt the confounder, check the axis, demand the whole record — is the skill of reading evidence carefully. Turn it, finally, on the evidence about yourself, and you will be neither paranoid nor naive about it, which is the only defensible position I know.

A concrete case will hold all of this together. Suppose a person applies for a job, and the employer buys a report assembled by a broker. The report says the applicant was associated with an address in another state for two years, and the employer's reader infers instability — a story, pasted onto rows. The rows themselves say less: a name resembling the applicant's appeared on a subscription at that address, merged into this file by a key match on the name alone. Two people, one name, one merged table — lesson two's duplicate disease, at national scale, with a person's livelihood as the stakes. The applicant who has kept their own records — leases, tax letters, dated correspondence — can do in one afternoon what no amount of confident arguing can do: put a whole, dated, first-party table next to the broker's assembled one and let the two be compared. That comparison, cross-checking, was the cheapest audit in existence when the subject was customers. It remains so when the subject is you. The moral is not that brokers always err. It is that their tables err at the ordinary rate of tables, and the correction mechanism is a better-kept table, held by the one person whose interest in its accuracy never sleeps.

Consider, too, the data you generate inside our own running example, because the marketing business holds data about its customers exactly as platforms hold data about you, and the symmetry is instructive. Its customer list is eight hundred rows of other people's data: names, addresses, first purchases. Its predictions record is about itself. The customers on that list have, most of them, never seen it, never corrected it, and would find in it the same diseases you would find in a broker's file on you — the forty ghosts, the stale addresses, the merged names. I do not tell you this to indict the business; its table is better kept than most, because its owner took lesson two. I tell you it so that the lesson's direction reverses cleanly in your mind: every table you keep about others deserves the care you wish holders would take with the tables they keep about you. The ethics of data, in this class, is not a separate subject. It is the same standard, applied in both directions — keep it whole, keep it accurate, show the person their rows when they ask, and never read a log as a diary, about a customer any more than about yourself.

One more distinction belongs in this final lesson, between data about you and data from you. Data from you is what you deliberately publish: the photograph you post, the comment you write, the review you sign. You chose its content, if not always its audience or its lifespan — a distinction worth a pause, because a post made for fifty friends and kept for fifteen years, searchable by employers and strangers, has changed its meaning without changing a word. Data about you is everything else in this lesson: the logs, the transactions, the inferences. Inferences deserve their own sentence, because they are the newest layer and the least visible. An inference is a conclusion a holder's system draws about you from your records: a category you are sorted into, a likelihood attached to your name — likely to move, likely to buy, likely to leave. Inferences are not observations. They are the holder's correlations, from lesson four, computed over millions of other people's rows and applied to yours, confounders unhunted, mechanisms unstated. Your file increasingly consists of them. When a decision about you is made from your file, the part that decided it may be neither a fact nor a record, but a resemblance — you pattern-match people who behaved a certain way. Knowing that resemblance is not evidence, and that you are entitled in many places to ask which of the two decided your case, is the grown-up form of everything this class has taught.

And a last word on deletion, because it is the promise most often made about your data and the one most loosely kept. Deleting a record from the table you can see — the history page, the account, the application — removes that view. Whether it removes the holder's underlying rows, the copies already sold or shared, the backups, and the inferences already drawn from it, are four further questions, and the answers are usually four different answers. This is not an accusation; backups and fraud records have honest reasons to persist. It is a calibration. The delete button is a real tool with a smaller radius than its label suggests, and a person who understands tables will use it where it helps, without believing it reaches the brokers, the backups, and the already-drawn conclusions. Precision about what a tool does is the beginning of using it well. That sentence, you may notice, could stand as the motto of all six lessons, and I will not pretend the resemblance is accidental.

One worked miniature, with numbers, before we close — because this class trusts arithmetic more than adjectives, including on the subject of you. A fitness application on your phone records your steps. On Tuesday it reports eight thousand four hundred steps; on Wednesday, nine thousand one hundred. A summary, later, announces your most active day and builds a small chart of your week, bars rising proudly. Audit it with the tools you now own. What was observed? Not steps, exactly: the phone observed accelerations, and an algorithm counted some of them as steps — a metric, lesson one, standing for the thing it names. The denominator: the chart shows the days the phone was carried, charged, and awake; the day you left it on the dresser is absent from the table, not recorded as zero — an empty field, lesson two, silently dropped from the average. The sample: your carried days stand for your life the way Saturday shoppers stood for all customers in lesson three, which is to say, imperfectly and in a known direction. The correlation the summary implies — that you were more virtuous on Wednesday — has confounders: Wednesday was the day you paced during a long call and twice walked to the shop you drove to on Tuesday. None of this makes the record useless. Eight thousand four hundred counted accelerations is a real observation, useful for comparing like with like. It makes the record a record — of a phone, an algorithm, and a carried week — rather than a verdict on a person. That demotion, from verdict to record, is the single most valuable move in this entire lesson, and it generalises to every file, report, and summary about you that you will ever be shown. Demote first. Then decide what the demoted thing is actually worth, which is sometimes a great deal, and never everything.

Recap, in order. One: your data is the recorded observations of you, held mostly by others — presence is not possession. Two: the holders fall into kinds — the organisations you deal with, the platforms, the brokers who assemble files without ever meeting you, and your own devices, the one category you hold yourself. Three: slices join into a pattern of life through keys — your name, number, and addresses are the fields that let tables about you be combined. Four: your data can prove transactions, approximate presence, and sequences; it cannot prove intent, attention, or character. Five: a log is not a diary — records made for billing and advertising answer your questions only incidentally, and the view a holder shows you is a filtered view, not the whole table. Six: your practical defences are to use your rights to see and correct your files, to reduce what you generate where the cost is low, and above all to keep your own records, whole, in a place you control.

If you take one practical hour from this lesson, spend it like this. Twenty minutes: open the activity pages of the two or three services you use most, and read them with the three questions, noting — actually noting, in writing — what was observed, what period the record covers, and whether what you are shown claims to be the whole table or a view of it. Twenty minutes: gather your own documents that matter into one place you control, with a copy elsewhere — the contracts, the receipts, the certificates, the dated record of work that is yours. Twenty minutes: correct one wrong thing somewhere — the stale address, the merged name, the permission an application never needed. An hour, three tasks, no purchases, no new accounts, no applications to install. At the end of it you will not have escaped the system, which was never the offer. You will have done something rarer and more useful: you will have looked at your own data with the same exactness you now bring to everyone else's claims, and acted on what you saw at the scale where action is actually possible. That is what this class means by literacy. Not suspicion. Not innocence. Inspection, followed by proportionate action, recorded — of course — in a table of your own.

That is the end of the teaching, and the end of the class. Six lessons: what data is, where it lives, how some can stand for all, why together is not because, how pictures lean, and whose hands your own rows rest in. You came in, most likely, believing that data was something other people had and other people argued about. You leave knowing it is a practice: observe honestly, record in advance, keep it whole, state the denominator, price every claim at the strength its design has earned. Do that, in a business or in a life, and you will be lied to measurably less often, including — especially — by yourself. Next, the questions on this lesson, with the full text open beside them.
