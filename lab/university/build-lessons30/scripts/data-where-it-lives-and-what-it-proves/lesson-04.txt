# Correlation, causation, and the gap between them

Good to see you. Today AI and Computing earns its keep. I am Professor Juhani Korhonen, and this is lesson four of six in Data — Where It Lives and What It Proves: correlation, causation, and the gap between them. This is the lesson where data most often gets promoted beyond its rank. Two things move together, and within a sentence one of them is causing the other, and within a paragraph money is being spent on the strength of it. We will slow that promotion down until it has to show its papers. I write the units on the board before opinions, every time. Bring your curiosity, and bring your suspicion of any sentence in which one thing is said to drive, boost, or deliver another on the evidence of a single chart; the rest is provided.

Definitions, exactly, because this lesson lives or dies on them. Two things are correlated when they tend to move together: when one is high, the other tends to be high — or, in the other direction, when one is high, the other tends to be low. Correlation is a pattern in recorded observations. Nothing more. Causation is a different claim entirely: causation means one thing makes the other happen — change the first and, because of that change, the second changes. Correlation is observed. Causation is inferred, argued, and, if you are fortunate and careful, tested. The gap between them is the subject of this lesson, and the gap is wide. The sentence to carry out of this room is the oldest in statistics, and it has not expired: correlation is not causation. Two things moving together is a clue. A clue is the beginning of an investigation. It is not the end of one.

Our marketing business supplies the first example. Over one year, the months with the highest advertising spend are also the months with the highest revenue. Spend and revenue rise together and fall together. The correlation is real — it sits in the advertising log and the accounts, in rows, checkable. The owner concludes: advertising causes revenue, so double the advertising. Perhaps. But look at the calendar. The highest-revenue months are November and December, when customers buy for the holidays regardless of what any business does. The business also advertises most in November and December, because that is when the customers are buying. A third thing — the season — is pushing both. This pattern has a name: a confounder. A confounder is a third factor that influences both things you are comparing, making them move together without either one causing the other. The season does not merely muddy this example. It may explain all of it. Advertising might add a great deal, a little, or nothing, and the correlation, by itself, cannot tell you which. Anyone who reads a correlation as a cause without hunting for confounders is not analysing. They are applauding.

Let me give you the classic absurd case, because absurdity is a teaching instrument. In many towns, ice cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly correlated across the year: both peak in the same months. Ice cream does not cause drowning, and banning ice cream would save no swimmers. The confounder is summer heat: heat sells ice cream, and heat sends people swimming, and swimming, occasionally and tragically, involves drowning. Two effects of one cause, marching together, impersonating a cause and its effect. Whenever you meet a correlation, your first professional act is to ask: what third thing could be pushing both? Do not ask whether there is a confounder, as if it were a remote possibility. Ask which confounder it is, as if it were certainly present and merely unnamed. That posture — assume a confounder until the hunt comes back empty — will protect you more reliably than any formula in this class.

The second impostor is reversed direction. Correlation is symmetric: if A moves with B, then B moves with A, and the pattern alone cannot tell you which, if either, is the cause. Our business finds that its most loyal customers are the ones who receive its email newsletter. Does the newsletter create loyalty? Or do loyal customers — people already fond of the business — sign up for newsletters at higher rates? Both stories fit the same correlation perfectly. The arrow of causation points one way in the first story and the opposite way in the second, and the data, as collected, are silent about arrows. Businesses spend heavily on the wrong arrow every year: they observe what their best customers do and then push all customers to do it, as if the behaviour manufactured the loyalty rather than merely advertising it. Sometimes it does. Often it is the loyalty wearing a uniform. The correlation cannot tell you which uniform it is.

The third impostor is coincidence, and it deserves respect because with enough data lying around, coincidence is guaranteed to produce beautiful correlations. If you track a hundred pairs of unrelated things, some pairs will march together for a while by pure chance — lesson one's noise, wearing a correlation's clothing. The more pairs you examine, the more striking coincidences you will find, and the finding will feel like discovery precisely because you examined so many. This is why a correlation discovered by trawling — by testing everything against everything until something lines up — deserves deep suspicion, while the same correlation, predicted in advance for a stated reason, deserves a hearing. A prediction made in advance, recorded, and then checked: you have met that discipline before. It is the predictions record again. The record's whole design — write it before, keep it whole — is a machine for separating real patterns from trawled coincidences, in business exactly as in science.

Now, the honest question: if correlation cannot prove causation, what can? The strongest everyday tool is the experiment. An experiment means you deliberately change one thing, hold everything else as steady as you can, and compare the outcome against a group where you changed nothing. That unchanged group is the control group. The group you changed is the treatment group. Our business can run one this month. It wants to know whether its new advertisement actually causes more enquiries, or whether enquiries would have risen anyway. It shows the new advertisement to a randomly chosen half of its audience and the old advertisement to the other half, in the same week, at the same spend per person. Randomly chosen — lesson three's machinery, doing its job — so the two groups are alike in every way except the advertisement. Suppose the new advertisement's group produces one hundred and twenty enquiries from ten thousand viewers, and the old advertisement's group produces one hundred enquiries from ten thousand viewers. One hundred and twenty against one hundred, on equal denominators, in the same week. Now the causal claim has earned a hearing: the only systematic difference between the groups is the advertisement, so the difference in enquiries is credited to it — provisionally, with the counts stated, and with lesson three's warning attached that even twenty extra enquiries on these numbers could partly be luck. Repeat the experiment and the credit firms up. That is how causation is actually established in practice: not by one dramatic proof, but by controlled comparisons, repeated, with confounders designed out in advance rather than argued away afterwards.

Notice what the experiment did that observation could not. In observation, the season, the audience, and the advertisement all change together, tangled. In the experiment, the design untangles them before the data arrive: same week kills the season as a confounder, random assignment kills the audience difference, equal spend kills the budget difference. Design beats argument. An hour of design before the fact is worth a month of arguing after it, and considerably cheaper in temper.

Where experiments are impossible — you cannot randomly assign people a childhood, a town, or a decade — causation must be argued from converging evidence: the correlation is strong, it appears in many settings, the supposed cause comes before the effect in time, and a plausible mechanism connects them. A mechanism is an account of how the cause would produce the effect, step by step. Timing matters absolutely: a cause must precede its effect. If revenue rose in March and the campaign began in April, the campaign did not cause the March rise, whatever the annual correlation says, and you would be surprised how often that elementary check is skipped in reports written by people with salaries. Hold the checklist: strength, consistency across settings, timing, mechanism — and even with all four, the honest word is supports, not proves. In this class, proves is a word we spend rarely, and only where the design has earned it.

Here is the guided moment. Recall a causal claim you have heard recently — an advertisement causes sales, a policy causes crime to fall, a food causes energy. Do not attack it. Interrogate it, in order. First: what is the correlation actually observed, stripped of causal words? Say it in moving-together language only. Second: name one confounder — one third thing that could push both. If you cannot name one, you have not looked; look again, the season and wealth and age are confounders in a remarkable share of all claims. Third: could the arrow run backwards? Fourth: was there an experiment, or a comparison that resembles one? You have now done, in two minutes, the analysis that most published causal claims never receive. The claim may survive. Many do. It will at least have been examined by someone, which distinguishes your reading of it from nearly everyone's.

Apply the whole apparatus to the predictions record, because it is this class's standing example of honesty and I want its limits stated by me, not discovered by you. The record shows sixty predictions, thirty-nine hits, twenty-one misses, kept whole. Does that prove the business's method causes good predictions? Carefully. It proves the predictions were made in advance and that thirty-nine of sixty matched what happened. That is a fact about the record. The causal claim — the method, rather than luck, produced the hits — needs more: it needs the difficulty of the predictions stated, because predicting sunrise sixty times would also yield a fine record, and it needs a comparison — what would chance alone, or a simpler method, have scored on the same sixty questions? Without that comparison, the record is honest description, not causal proof. Honest description is valuable. It is the floor everything else stands on. But this class does not promote description into proof, for our own record any more than for a consultant's brochure. The standard is the standard, and it applies at home first.

One more term, because you will meet it in print: a spurious correlation is a correlation that is real in the numbers but meaningless in the world — produced by a confounder, by coincidence, or by both marching together. Spurious does not mean the arithmetic was wrong. The arithmetic is usually impeccable. It means the story pasted onto the arithmetic is false. Data do not lie, people say. Data do not speak, either. People speak for data, and the speaking is where the lying, and the erring, and occasionally the science, happens. Your job, trained by this class, is to audit the speaking.

Let me work the season example with actual numbers, so the confounder stops being an abstraction. Suppose the business spends four hundred dollars on advertising in June and earns four thousand dollars in revenue. In December it spends one thousand two hundred dollars and earns twelve thousand dollars. Spend tripled; revenue tripled; the correlation across the year looks immaculate. Now the confounder's arithmetic. December brings, let us say, three times the customer traffic of June, holidays being what they are. Revenue per advertising dollar is ten dollars in both months: four thousand over four hundred, twelve thousand over one thousand two hundred. The advertising did not become more effective in December. There were simply more buyers in the room, and both the spending and the earning were responses to their presence. The correlation between spend and revenue is real, and the causal story — spending drove the revenue — may still contain some truth, but the clean tripling belongs to the season. A business that doubles its June advertising on the strength of December's correlation will discover that June has no holidays hidden in it. I have watched this exact expenditure happen. The report afterwards blamed the advertisement. The advertisement was innocent. The calendar did it.

A second worked example, from the customer table this time. The business notices that customers who buy on Mondays spend more per order than customers who buy on Fridays. The correlation is in the table; sort it and see. A causal story arrives instantly: Monday shoppers are serious shoppers, so aim the best offers at Monday. But hunt the confounder first. Suppose businesses — offices, workshops, other firms — order on Mondays, at the week's start, in bulk, while individuals order on Fridays, in small quantities, for the weekend. Then the confounder is customer type: firms spend more and shop on Mondays; individuals spend less and shop on Fridays. The day is not causing the spending. The customer is causing both the day and the spending. Aim the best offers at firms, on whatever day they shop, and the business profits. Aim them at Monday, indiscriminately, and it discounts large Friday individuals and small Monday firms alike, having mistaken a calendar for a cause. Same table, same correlation, two strategies, only one of them aimed at the actual mechanism.

Now let me teach you the phrase that does the most work in this whole lesson, because you can use it in any meeting without giving offence: what else changed? When a result is credited to one cause — the new advertisement, the new price, the new manager — ask what else changed in the same period. Something always else changed. Seasons turn, competitors move, prices of supplies rise, a road closes, a holiday lands on a different weekday. The question does not deny the credited cause. It audits the credit. If the answer is a thoughtful list with reasons each item cannot explain the result, the causal claim grows stronger in front of you. If the answer is a blank look, you have learned that the claim was never examined, only asserted. Assertions are free. Examinations are the product. Buy examinations.

There is a special case worth isolating: the before-and-after comparison, which is the experiment's poor relation and the most common causal argument in business. Sales were X before the change and Y after it; therefore the change worked. The flaw is structural: before and after differ in time, and time brings everything else with it — season, trend, weather, news. A rising business makes many changes, and each change claims the rise that the trend was delivering anyway. The repair, when a true experiment is impossible, is a comparison group that lived through the same period without the change: another town, another product line, the customers who were not sent the offer. If the changed group rose ten percent and the unchanged group rose nine percent in the same weeks, the change's honest credit is about one point, not ten. That subtraction — the comparison group's change removed from the treatment group's change — is the single most valuable arithmetic habit in causal reasoning. It converts applause into estimate. Use it wherever two groups and two periods exist, which is more often than businesses realise.

A word, too, on dose. If a cause is real, more of it usually produces more of the effect, up to some limit: a dose-response pattern. Advertising that genuinely causes enquiries should, within reason, produce more enquiries at higher spend — not perfectly, not forever, but visibly. When doubling the spend produces nothing at all, repeatedly, the causal story weakens, however fine the original correlation looked. Dose is not proof either — a confounder can also rise with the dose, as December's traffic rises with December's spend — but a complete absence of dose-response is evidence against a cause, and evidence against deserves the same attention as evidence for. Symmetric standards, again: we grade our own favourite claims with the same instruments we aim at other people's. That symmetry is not politeness. It is what makes the instruments trustworthy when they finally say yes.

Let me close the teaching with the hierarchy, stated plainly, so you can rank any claim you meet. At the bottom: a single anecdote with a causal story — lesson one's weakest material, promoted without authority. Above it: a correlation, honestly measured, cause unknown. Above that: a correlation with confounders hunted, timing right, mechanism plausible, found in several settings. Above that: a before-and-after with a comparison group, differenced honestly. At the top, for everyday purposes: a controlled experiment, randomly assigned, repeated. Claims should be priced by their floor in this building. The tragedy of business data is not that low-floor claims exist — they are where inquiry starts — but that they are so often priced as penthouses. Your training is simply this: read the floor before admiring the view.

One more caution, on language, because language is where causal claims smuggle themselves past guards. Watch for verbs. Linked to, associated with, and rises with are correlation words — honest ones. Drives, boosts, delivers, and fuels are causation words. Reports frequently begin with the honest words in the headline's small print and graduate to the causal verbs by the second paragraph, without any new evidence arriving in between. The evidence did not change. Only the verbs did. Train your ear for the graduation. When you hear a causal verb, ask which floor of the hierarchy the speaker is standing on, and whether the rent for the higher floor was ever paid. You will find, often, that the entire causal claim is a verb choice. Verbs are cheap. Experiments are not. Prefer the people who pay.

And the mirror-image error, briefly, so you do not overcorrect: refusing all causal claims because none is perfect. That posture feels rigorous and is merely lazy. The experiment with random assignment, equal denominators, and repeated results is strong knowledge — strong enough to spend money on, carefully, in stages, watching the next results as they arrive. Science is not the refusal to conclude. It is concluding at exactly the strength the design has earned, and no stronger. A claim priced correctly can be acted on. A claim refused entirely cannot, and the business that refuses all claims will be out-competed by the business that prices them. Exactness, not timidity, is the virtue this class is building.

A brief note on a phrase you will now start hearing everywhere, precisely because you have a name for the thing it hides: studies show. Studies show a correlation, usually, in a particular population, under particular conditions, with confounders hunted to a particular depth. The phrase studies show compresses all of that into two words of borrowed authority. Decompress it, every time, with the instruments of this lesson: what moved with what, in whom, what else changed, which way might the arrow run, and was anything compared against a control? A study that survives decompression is worth your attention. A claim that cannot survive being stated precisely was never a finding. It was a fog with a citation. You are no longer required to breathe fog, in this class or out of it.

One more pattern completes the set, and it is the subtlest: the correlation that is real, causal, and still misread, because the cause works through a chain. Advertising may cause enquiries, enquiries may cause calls, and calls may cause sales. If the business measures only advertising against sales, the correlation will look weak and erratic, because two leaky links sit between them — many enquiries never call, many calls never buy. The weak correlation does not acquit the advertisement, and a strong one would not convict it either; the chain must be measured link by link, which the tables from lesson two make possible: advertisement rows against enquiry counts, enquiry rows against calls, calls against orders. Causal chains are where small businesses quietly misjudge their marketing for years, crediting the last link — the salesperson, the web page — for the whole chain's work, or blaming the first link for a break that happened at the third. Measure each link. Credit each link. Repair the broken link, which is usually not the one the argument is about.

Recap, in order. One: correlation means two things tend to move together; causation means one makes the other happen; the first is observed, the second must be earned. Two: a confounder is a third factor pushing both, and your first act on meeting any correlation is to hunt for it. Three: correlation cannot tell you the direction of the arrow — the cause may run backwards from the story being told. Four: trawling many pairs manufactures coincidences, while predictions recorded in advance earn a hearing. Five: an experiment — a treatment group, a control group, random assignment, everything else held steady — is the strongest everyday road to a causal claim. Six: where experiments are impossible, argue from strength, consistency, timing, and mechanism, and let the word be supports, not proves. Seven: even an honest record describes before it proves; the standard applies at home first.

One last exercise in imagination before the questions, because the next claim you meet will not wait for a convenient moment. Picture the owner, in January, holding a chart of last year's spend and revenue marching upward together, proposing to triple the advertising budget. You are the person in the room who has taken this lesson. You do not say correlation is not causation and sit down, although the sentence is true; slogans persuade nobody who holds a chart. You ask, in order: what else changed across those months — and let the season be named by the room, not by you. You ask what revenue per advertising dollar did in June versus December, and let the arithmetic — ten dollars in both — do the arguing. You ask whether a comparison exists: a product line that was not advertised, a town where the campaign did not run. And you propose the experiment: one month, two halves of the audience, randomly assigned, equal spend, enquiries counted. Notice the shape of what you have done. You have not blocked the owner. You have converted an act of faith into a testable question with a price tag of one month, which is what this entire class means by thinking with data. If the advertisement truly causes enquiries, the experiment will say so, and the budget can triple with evidence under it. If it does not, the business has purchased the truth at a discount. Either way the room wins, and either way you were the reason. That is the professional this course is building, one lesson at a time.

Answer them at the strength the lesson earned and no stronger: that instruction is a joke about causation, and also, not entirely, a joke. The serious version is the whole course in one line: conclude at the strength your evidence has earned, state the strength when you conclude, and let the record — kept whole, counted honestly, compared fairly — carry exactly the weight it can bear and not one gram more. That discipline, practised daily, is indistinguishable from wisdom at a distance.

Next, the questions on this lesson, with the full text open beside them — going back to read is learning, not cheating. Keep the question what else changed within reach at all times. It costs nothing, offends no one who is honest, and has ended more bad expenditures than any software I own. Questions of that quality are rare. You now own two sets of them — the three from lesson one, the four checks from lesson three, and this single sentence from lesson four — and together they will examine nearly any claim that data is asked to carry.

You came in, most likely, using the words correlation and causation almost interchangeably, as the world does. You leave with them separated by a gap you can measure: on one side, patterns observed in records; on the other, claims that must be earned by design, comparison, timing, and mechanism. That separation, maintained under pressure — under the pressure of a confident owner, a persuasive consultant, a beautiful chart, and your own hope that the expensive thing worked — is most of what separates a person who uses data from a person who is used by it. Hold the separation. It is the lesson. And when you catch yourself graduating a correlation into a cause in your own speech — you will; everyone does; the verbs are lying there polished and ready — correct yourself out loud, in the room, by name. Nothing builds a reputation for exactness faster than a person visibly refusing their own exaggeration, and nothing in this class is worth more than that reputation, because it is the asset every later claim you make will be priced against.
