# What counts as data, and what is just noise with confidence

Good to see you. Today AI and Computing earns its keep. I am Professor Juhani Korhonen, and this is Data — Where It Lives and What It Proves. This is lesson one of six: what counts as data, and what is just noise with confidence. I will write the units on the board before opinions, every time. That is my habit, and it will be yours by the end of this class. Bring your curiosity, a pencil, and a healthy suspicion of round confident numbers; the rest is provided.

Here is what this lesson teaches, plainly. First, what data is: recorded observations, written down in a form you can check later. Second, what data is not: a feeling, a rumour, a confident sentence, or a number somebody invented because a number was needed. Third, how to tell the difference, using three questions you can ask about anything anyone shows you. By the end you will be able to look at a claim and say, calmly and correctly, whether there is data under it or only confidence on top of it. That is worth knowing, because most bad decisions begin with someone mistaking confidence for evidence.

Let us start with a definition. A definition is a sentence that tells you what a word means and what it does not mean. Data means recorded observations. Each word matters. An observation is something noticed through the senses or through an instrument: you saw it, you heard it, you counted it, a sensor measured it. Recorded means it was written down, saved, or stored somewhere outside a person's memory, so it can be checked later by someone else. Data is the plural of datum, which is Latin for a thing given. One recorded observation is a datum. Several of them, gathered together, are data. In ordinary speech people say data is, even though the word is plural. I will not correct you for that. I will correct you for calling an opinion data. That error costs money.

Consider a small marketing business, because we will use one throughout this class. Imagine a company with a customer list, a website, and a modest budget for advertisements. The owner says, our customers love the new service. That sentence may be true. It is not data. It is a claim. A claim is a statement offered as true, whether or not anyone has checked it. Now suppose the owner shows you a list: forty-two customers were emailed last Tuesday, thirty-one opened the message, and nine booked a call. Those are recorded observations. Someone counted. The counts were written down. You can check them against the email system. That is data. Notice the difference. The first sentence feels warm. The second one can be verified. Warmth is not verification.

Here is the first test, and I want you to hold onto it. Data can be checked by someone who was not there. If the only way to know a thing is to trust the person saying it, you do not have data. You have testimony. Testimony means a person's report of what they believe happened. Testimony is not worthless. Witnesses matter in courts and in life. But testimony depends on memory, and memory is a poor storage device. Memory edits. Memory forgets the inconvenient parts and sharpens the flattering parts, usually without asking permission. A record does not do that. A record written on Tuesday still says on Friday what it said on Tuesday. That is the entire advantage, and it is a large one.

Now the opposite of data in this lesson is not falsehood. The opposite is noise with confidence. Noise, in this class, means random variation: the ups and downs that happen for no meaningful reason. If you flip a fair coin ten times, you might get seven heads. Nothing caused the seven. The coin has no preference. The seven is noise. Confidence means sounding certain. Noise with confidence is a random result described as if it were a discovery. It is the most common substance in business meetings, and I say that without exaggeration, which you will learn is a high standard for me.

Let me give you a small honest example with numbers, because numbers are where this lesson lives. Our marketing business runs an advertisement for five days. On Monday, one hundred people see the advertisement and four of them click it. On Tuesday, one hundred people see it and six click. On Wednesday, one hundred people see it and three click. The owner announces on Wednesday evening that interest is collapsing and the advertisement must be replaced. Is that data speaking? The counts are data, yes. They were recorded. But the conclusion is noise with confidence. With numbers this small, a difference between six clicks and three clicks is well inside ordinary random variation. If nothing at all had changed, you would still expect days like Tuesday and days like Wednesday, simply because small counts jump around. A count of three and a count of six, out of one hundred, do not prove a trend. They prove that counting happened. That distinction — between the count, which is real, and the story pasted onto the count, which may not be — is the heart of this lesson.

So if X is a small count, then Y, a large conclusion, does not follow. Hold onto that. Small numbers swing widely. Large numbers settle down. We will do the arithmetic properly in lesson three, when we study samples. For now, remember the shape of the error: a true count, an untrue certainty.

Next, a word you will meet everywhere: a metric. A metric is a number chosen to stand for something you care about. Clicks are a metric for interest. Revenue is a metric for sales. Neither one is the thing itself. Interest is a state of mind. A click is a finger pressing a button, which usually means interest and sometimes means an accident. When someone shows you a metric, ask what it stands for and what it leaves out. A business can double its clicks while halving its sales, if the new advertisement attracts curious people who never buy. The metric rose. The business fell. Both statements are true, and only one of them matters. Choosing a metric is a decision, not a discovery, and decisions can be wrong.

Now let me teach you the three questions. When anyone — a colleague, a salesperson, a headline, or your own hopeful brain — presents you with a finding, ask these. Question one: what exactly was observed? Not what was concluded. What was observed. Question two: how many observations are there? Question three: where is the record, and can I see all of it, not only the part being shown to me? Three questions. They take twenty seconds. They will save you, over a lifetime, a sum I will not estimate, because I do not invent numbers. I only note that the sum is not small.

Apply them to our owner. A consultant tells him: businesses like yours typically see a forty percent lift from this tool. Question one: what was observed? The consultant does not say. Question two: how many businesses? The consultant says, many. Many is not a number. Question three: where is the record? There is a brochure. A brochure is not a record. It is an advertisement for the claim. Verdict: there may be data somewhere behind that sentence, but none of it has been shown to you, so for you, today, it is confidence without data. You may still buy the tool. People buy things for many reasons. But do not tell yourself the evidence persuaded you. It did not. It was not present.

Contrast that with an honest record from the same business. The business keeps a predictions record. Each week it writes down, in advance, what it expects to happen — for example, how many enquiries a campaign will produce — and later it writes down what actually happened, hits and misses printed together, at the same size, in the same list. That is data honesty, and it deserves the name. The misses are not hidden in a drawer. The hits are not enlarged. Because the predictions were recorded before the outcomes were known, nobody could quietly rewrite them afterwards. A record made in advance, kept whole, is the strongest kind of everyday data a small business can own. We will return to this example in lesson six, because it shows what data can prove and what it cannot.

Notice what made that record strong. It was not cleverness. It was timing and completeness. Timing: the observation was written before the result. Completeness: all of it was kept, the flattering and the unflattering together. A record that keeps only successes is not a record of performance. It is a record of successes. Those are different objects, and confusing them is how businesses lie to themselves without ever saying a false word. Every individual entry can be accurate while the collection misleads. Remember that sentence. It will matter again when we reach charts in lesson five.

Let us define two more terms while we are here, because sloppy words make sloppy data. A fact is a statement that is true and can be shown to be true. An anecdote is a single story about a single case. Anecdotes are observations, in a weak sense — something did happen to someone — but one case cannot tell you what usually happens. If one customer writes to say the service changed his life, that is one datum about one customer. It tells you nothing reliable about the other nine hundred and ninety-nine. The plural of anecdote is not data. Actually, a pile of anecdotes, collected carelessly, is worse than one anecdote, because the pile looks like evidence while remaining a pile of stories. Data begins when cases are collected by a rule, not by enthusiasm. Collected by a rule means: you decided in advance which cases count, and you took all of them, not only the ones that pleased you.

Here is a quieter example. The business keeps a customer list with names, email addresses, and the date each person first bought something. Is that data? Yes. Each entry is a recorded observation: this person, this address, this date. Is the list good data? That is a different question, and a better one. If addresses were typed in haste, some will be wrong. If customers who asked to be removed are still on it, the list is out of date. Data can be real and still be poor. Poor data is recorded observation with errors, gaps, or staleness in it. The opposite error of this lesson also exists: dismissing real data because it is imperfect. Imperfect data, used with its flaws stated, beats confident guessing. Perfect data does not exist outside of textbooks, and I include my own textbooks in that remark.

Let me pause and give you the guided moment for this lesson. This is the doing part. Think of the most recent claim someone made to you with a number in it. It might be at work. It might be in an advertisement. It might be your own claim, which is the hardest kind to examine and therefore the most valuable. Now run the three questions on it, slowly. What exactly was observed? If you cannot answer, say so out loud: I do not know what was observed. How many observations were there? If the answer is a word like many, or typical, or everyone, notice that no number was given. Where is the record, and can you see all of it? Picture the actual record — a list, a log, a file — or notice that you are picturing nothing. Done. You have just performed the basic move of this entire class. It took less than a minute. Do it often enough and it becomes reflex, which is where I want it.

One more distinction before the recap, because it causes endless confusion. Precision is not accuracy. Precision means the number is stated in fine detail. Accuracy means the number is close to the truth. A report that says sales rose by thirty-seven point four percent sounds precise. If the underlying count was taken from a broken till and half the receipts were missing, the number is precise and wrong. Detail is cheap. Anyone can add a decimal point. Accuracy is expensive, because it requires careful counting. When a number arrives wearing a decimal point, do not be impressed. Ask how it was counted. A whole number honestly counted outranks a decimal invented to impress, every time.

And the mirror image: a vague statement can be accurate. Saying about a third of our customers return within a month may be exactly right, if someone counted returning customers, counted all customers, and divided. We will learn to do that division properly in lesson two. The point for today is that the sound of a claim — crisp or vague, confident or hesitant — carries no information about whether data stands under it. Only the record carries that information. Judge the record. Ignore the tone. Tone is weather. Records are climate.

Let me also say what data cannot do, because this lesson is titled what counts as data, and honest teaching includes limits. Data cannot tell you what to want. It cannot tell the owner whether to chase growth or stability, whether to be kind or merely legal. Data describes what was observed. Decisions require values as well as observations. A person who says the data made me do it is usually hiding a choice they made themselves. Data informs choices. It does not make them, and it does not excuse them. Keep that fence standing and you will reason better than most professionals I have met.

Now a companion word to noise: signal. A signal is a real pattern — a difference or a change that is actually there, caused by something, and large enough to stand above the noise. If our business sends two different email subject lines, each to five thousand customers, and one produces four hundred replies while the other produces two hundred, that gap is probably signal. Five thousand is a large count. Large counts settle, as I said. If instead each subject line went to fifty customers, and one produced four replies and the other produced two, that gap is probably noise wearing a suit. Same ratio — double — completely different meaning, because the counts underneath differ. This is why question two, how many observations, is not pedantry. It is the difference between learning something and fooling yourself at speed. Whenever someone reports a percentage, demand the count underneath it. A percentage without a count is a hat without a head. Fifty percent of two is one. Fifty percent of two thousand is one thousand. The percentage is identical. The evidence is not.

A related trap deserves its own paragraph: the missing denominator. A denominator is the bottom number in a fraction — the total out of which the top number came. Nine customers complained this week. Is that bad? You cannot know until you know the denominator. Nine complaints out of ten customers is a catastrophe. Nine complaints out of nine thousand customers is a quiet week. People who wish to alarm you will give you the top number alone. People who wish to soothe you will do the same, in the other direction. The honest habit is mechanical: every time you hear a count of something interesting, ask, out of how many. Make it a reflex, like checking a mirror before changing lanes. Lesson five will show you charts built entirely on hiding denominators. You will be ready for them.

Another quiet failure: survivorship. Survivorship bias means you only see the cases that survived some process, and the missing cases would have changed the picture. Our business interviews its ten happiest long-term customers and concludes that customers stay because of friendly service. Perhaps. But the customers who left — the ones who did not survive as customers — were not interviewed, and they might have left precisely because of the service. The record of current customers is real data about current customers. It is silent about the departed, and conclusions about why people stay, drawn only from people who stayed, are built on that silence. Whenever a collection is shown to you, ask who is missing from it and why. A collection with a hole in it is still data, but only about what is inside the hole's border.

Let me also distinguish measurement from counting, because both produce data and they fail differently. Counting is deciding how many: how many orders, how many visitors, how many complaints. Counting fails when the rule for what counts is fuzzy. Does a visitor who opens the page twice count once or twice? Either rule can be defended. Switching rules halfway through the month cannot. Measurement is deciding how much: how long a call lasted, how heavy a parcel is, how satisfied a customer says they are on a scale from one to five. Measurement fails when the instrument is off — a clock running fast, a scale uncalibrated, a survey question worded to push the answer. When you inspect a record, ask which kind it is, counting or measuring, and then ask how the counting rule or the instrument could have gone wrong. You will not always find an error. You will always learn how much trust the record has earned.

Satisfaction scores deserve thirty seconds of their own, because businesses love them and they are the softest data in this lesson. A customer circling four out of five has recorded something — a mark on a form — but what it stands for is murky. One customer's four is another customer's five. Some customers never give the top mark on principle. Others give it to end the conversation. Treat such scores as weak data: real records, loosely connected to the thing they claim to measure. They are not useless. A fall from four point six to three point nine across thousands of replies probably signals something real. A difference between four point one and four point two across forty replies signals nothing except that forty people filled in a form. Size again. It always comes back to size, and to whether the record was kept whole.

One more example from our business, this time about money, because money concentrates the mind. The owner says advertising works because revenue rose in the month after the campaign began. The revenue figures are data — recorded, checkable, from the till and the bank. But two true facts side by side do not yet make a proof. Revenue also rises in December for reasons that have nothing to do with advertising, and falls in January for the same reason. That problem — things moving together — is lesson four, correlation and causation, and we will take it apart properly there. For today, file the shape of it: data can be genuine, relevant, and still insufficient for the conclusion being drawn from it. Insufficient is not the same as worthless. It means the honest sentence is we do not know yet, and that sentence, spoken plainly, is a sign of a well-run mind, not a weak one.

I will add one practical habit before the recap, because habits outlast lectures. Keep a small notebook, or a file, for claims. When someone gives you a number that matters — a promised lift, a forecast, a typical result — write it down with the date and the name of the person who said it. In a month, compare the record with what happened. This is the predictions-record idea from earlier, applied to other people's confidence. You will discover, within a few months, whose confidence is calibrated — whose seventy percent means roughly seventy percent — and whose confidence is decoration. That discovery is data about people, collected by a rule, kept whole. It will quietly improve every decision you make afterwards. No software required. A pencil will do, although lesson two will offer you a better tool.

Two more terms, briefly, because you will hear them used loosely and I prefer them used exactly. Primary data is data you collected yourself, for your own question, by your own rule. The customer list our business maintains is primary data. Secondary data is data someone else collected, for their question, which you are borrowing for yours. Industry averages are secondary data. Secondary data is not inferior by definition — a national statistics office counts better than you do — but it was collected under someone else's rule, for someone else's purpose, and the fit to your question is your problem to check. When the consultant says businesses like yours, the words like yours are doing heavy lifting. Like yours in size? In country? In what they sell? Each answer changes whether the borrowed data describes you at all. Ask. The asking is free.

And now the hardest case, which I save for near the end because it involves you. Your own memory of your own business is the data source you will trust most and should trust least. The owner remembers the campaign that worked. He remembers the delighted customer. The failed campaign and the angry customer fade, not from dishonesty but from ordinary human storage. This is why the record must live outside the head. Write it down at the time, all of it, by a rule. Future you is a stranger. Leave that stranger good records. I have kept laboratory notebooks for decades, and the notebook has corrected my memory more times than my memory has corrected the notebook. The count is not close.

So the standard for this class is now set, and it is not a high bar dressed as one. It is a low bar, honestly cleared: observe, record, keep it whole, state the count, name the denominator, and let the conclusion be exactly as large as the record allows — no larger. Most of what calls itself data-driven fails that bar. You, after one lesson, already know how to check. That is a good return on thirty minutes.

One last worked check, start to finish, so you see the whole method on one claim. A software seller tells our owner that his tool finds, on average, twenty percent more repeat customers. Run the questions. What was observed? After pressing, the seller says repeat purchases were counted in the shops using the tool. How many observations? He says the figure comes from twelve shops over one spring. Where is the record? He cannot show it; it belongs to his company. Now judge precisely. Twelve shops is a real count, not a fog. One spring is a real period. But you cannot see the record, you do not know how repeat was defined, and you do not know whether the twelve shops were chosen because their numbers pleased the seller. The correct verdict is not liar. The correct verdict is unverified: a claim with a shape of data behind it that has not been shown. Unverified claims may turn out true. You simply may not spend as if they already have. That sentence — unverified, not false — is the exact, dry judgement this lesson exists to teach. Most claims you meet will land there, and landing there honestly is a success, not a failure of nerve.

Recap, in order. One: data is recorded observation — noticed, counted or measured, and written down where others can check it. Two: a claim without a checkable record is confidence, not evidence, however warmly it is delivered. Three: noise is random variation, and small counts swing widely, so a small count cannot carry a large conclusion. Four: a metric is a number chosen to stand for something else; ask what it stands for and what it leaves out. Five: ask the three questions — what was observed, how many observations, and where is the whole record. Six: a record made in advance and kept whole, hits and misses together, is data at its most honest. Seven: precision is not accuracy, and tone is not evidence.

Before you go, one sentence to carry into every meeting you attend this year. When the room goes quiet after a confident number, you are allowed to be the person who asks, politely, what was counted, how many there were, and where the record is. You will occasionally be unpopular for eleven seconds. Then the room will think more clearly, which is the entire point.

Next, you will answer questions on this lesson — ten or so, about what I actually taught here and nothing else. The full text of this lesson stays open beside the questions. Going back to read is learning, not cheating. Use it. Write the three questions somewhere you will see them. Paper is fine. Paper has an excellent record of surviving, which is more than can be said for most confident claims made in warm rooms by people holding brochures instead of records.
