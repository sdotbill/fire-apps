# Lesson 9 — Questions: asking anything — quién, qué, dónde, cuánto

Hola, Bill — hello, Bill. Soy Carmen — I am Carmen, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, mi estudiante — my student — and bring your curiosity with you, because today is the lesson that opens every door the other lessons built. Until now, you have mostly answered the world: you greet it, you name yourself to it, you describe houses to it. Today you learn to interrogate it, gently, the way a friend does. Today we learn the question words of Spanish — all of them, as one system, a single ring of keys: Quién — who. Qué — what. Dónde — where. Cuándo — when. Cuánto — how much, how many. Cómo — how. And, lightly, por qué — why. Seven keys, Bill. A man carrying these seven words can ask anything, of anyone, anywhere Spanish is spoken — directions, prices, names, reasons, the whole world becomes answerable. And because this is lesson nine, this lesson also carries our Spanish-for-Jillian strand, the sweet thread of this course: today you learn to ask her about her day — and mean it — and to listen like a man who understands that listening, done right, is a love language all by itself. Your wedding is coming — el veinticinco de marzo — the twenty-fifth of March — in Key West, and by that day I want you asking her questions in Spanish at the kitchen table like you were born to it. Speak first, as always: I say it, you say it back out loud, and only then do we move on.

First, thirty seconds on how a Spanish question is built, because once you see the machine, every key on the ring makes sense. A Spanish question has two marks, and I want you to love them both: ¿ — the upside-down question mark that opens the question, standing at the front like a friend waving you in — and ? — the ordinary question mark that closes it at the end. Why two? Because Spanish is kind to its readers and its listeners: the opening mark warns you a question is coming, so your voice can get ready. And your voice does change, Bill — this is the second half of the machine. In a statement, your voice settles down at the end, the way a plane lands. In a question, your voice lifts at the end, the way eyebrows lift. Same words, different music, different meaning. Es grande — it is big — voice down, a fact. ¿Es grande? — is it big? — voice up, a question. Say that pair with me and feel your own voice do it: Es grande. ¿Es grande? Es grande. ¿Es grande? Hear that lift? That lift is called intonation, and in Spanish it matters as much as the words. And here is the beautiful part, the part that makes this whole lesson one system and not seven separate chores: every question word on our ring wears a written accent — quién, qué, dónde, cuándo, cuánto, cómo — every single one leans, and the accent tells your voice exactly where to lean, the way I taught you with días — days — and corazón — heart — back in lesson one. A question word in Spanish always carries its accent, always. So if X, then Y — hold onto that: if a word is asking, then the accent is sitting on it, showing your voice where the weight goes. Seven keys, one machine, two marks, one lift. That is the whole system. Now let us cut the keys, one by one, starting with the one you will use on her tonight.

Cómo — how. Say it with me, leaning where the accent leans: Cómo. KOH-moh, weight on the first beat. Cómo. You already own a piece of this key from lesson one: ¿Cómo te llamas? — what is your name? — word for word, how do you call yourself. There was cómo, hiding inside your very first question, doing how work all along. Today cómo becomes the kindest question word in the language, because cómo is the key that asks after people: ¿Cómo estás? — how are you? Say it with me, warmly, the way you would say it to someone you actually care about: ¿Cómo estás? Estás — you are, how you are right now — the brother of está from our house lesson: está for a house and a street, estás for you, the person in front of me, right now. ¿Cómo estás? asks after the person's present weather, not their nature, and that is why it is the perfect daily question: it does not ask who are you in general, it asks how are you, today, in this hour, in that body, after that shift. Say it again, slower, like you mean the answer matters: ¿Cómo… estás? ¿Cómo estás? Good. And because a question deserves an answer, here is yours, the answer you will give when she turns it around on you — and she will: Estoy bien — I am well, I am fine. Say it with me: Estoy bien. Estoy — I am, right now — the first-person brother of estás and está; the whole family answers how and where things stand at this moment. Bien — well, fine, good. Estoy bien. And the honest version, the one for the days that are not fine, because this course does not teach you to perform, it teaches you to talk: Estoy cansado — I am tired. Cansado — tired, in the form a man uses for himself. Say it: Estoy cansado. And softened, the way you would actually say it at the end of a long day, with a little honesty and no drama: Estoy un poco cansado — I am a little tired. Un poco — a little, a bit. Say the whole true sentence: Estoy un poco cansado. ¿Cómo estás? Estoy bien. ¿Cómo estás? Estoy un poco cansado. Question and answer, both directions, owned. That exchange, Bill, is the heartbeat of a marriage in any language: how are you — and a true answer.

Quién — who. Say it with me: Quién. Kee-EHN, the accent leaning on the end. Quién. This key asks after a person — not a thing, not a place, a person. ¿Quién es? — who is it, who is he, who is she? Say it: ¿Quién es? There is es — is — our little bridge from the house lesson, now introducing human beings. ¿Quién es? is the question for the knock at the door, the new face at the table, the name you missed at the wedding rehearsal. And it has a plural brother for a crowd: ¿Quiénes son? — who are they? Quiénes — who, for more than one. Son — they are. Say it: ¿Quiénes son? You will not need quiénes every day, but at a wedding, mi estudiante, you will need it by the second hour, because weddings are full of beautiful strangers whose names you are supposed to know. Put quién to work in a sentence about your real life: ¿Quién es ella? — who is she? Ella — she, her. ¿Quién es ella? And the answer you will give a hundred times, with pride, so learn its shape now: Es mi prometida — she is my fiancée. Prometida — fiancée — your promised one, from lesson one. Es mi prometida. Say the exchange with me, question and answer, like a man at his own wedding door: ¿Quién es ella? Es mi prometida. ¿Quién es ella? Es mi prometida. Perfect. Quién asks, es answers, and a stranger becomes a person with a name and a place in your life. That is what this key is for.

Qué — what. Say it with me: Qué. One beat, clean as a knock: KEH. Qué. This is the widest key on the ring — qué asks after things, ideas, definitions, the whole non-human world. Its most useful shape is the one for the object in your hand, the word on the page, the thing on the table you cannot name yet: ¿Qué es esto? — what is this? Esto — this, this thing here. Say it with me: ¿Qué es esto? ¿Qué es esto? is the beginner's best friend, Bill, because it turns every unknown object into a lesson: you point, you ask, the world names it for you, and the word sticks because you asked for it at the moment you needed it. Carry it into la cocina — the kitchen — from our house lesson, point at anything, and ask: ¿Qué es esto? Carry it into a market in Key West, point at a fruit you have never seen: ¿Qué es esto? And qué does one more job, the job of asking after meanings: when someone says a Spanish word you do not know, qué is how you ask what it means — you repeat the word, and you ask, in effect: what is that, what does that mean? The world answers, and your vocabulary grows by your own asking. That is the secret of this whole lesson, so hear it plainly: a student with qué and a student without qué are living in two different countries. One of them can learn from every room he walks into. Say the key once more, and feel how much country it opens: ¿Qué es esto?

Dónde — where. Say it with me: Dónde. DOHN-deh. You know this key — it carried our whole house lesson on its back: ¿Dónde está? — where is it? ¿Dónde está la cocina? — where is the kitchen? ¿Dónde está el baño? — where is the bathroom? I am not teaching you a new word today, Bill; I am hanging an old friend on the ring where he belongs, so you can see the family whole. Dónde asks after place, and place answers with está and its two faithful servants from lesson eight: aquí — here — and allí — there. La cocina está aquí — the kitchen is here. El baño está allí — the bathroom is there. Say the old exchange once, for the joy of owning it: ¿Dónde está la cocina? La cocina está aquí. Good. But notice what the ring gives you now that you have the other keys beside it: dónde can walk out of the house and ask after the whole world. ¿Dónde está la calle? — where is the street? ¿Dónde está Key West? — where is Key West? — asked with a smile, because you know perfectly well, but a man should be able to ask after his own wedding town in Spanish. Say them: ¿Dónde está la calle? ¿Dónde está Key West? Dónde plus está plus any place on earth. That is the formula, and it has no exceptions worth fearing. Where is the one question a traveler asks more than any other, and you have owned it since the house lesson — today you just learned that it was a key on a ring, and the ring is yours.

Cuándo — when. Say it with me: Cuándo. KWAHN-doh, leaning on the first beat where the accent sits. Cuándo. This key asks after time — the hour, the day, the date, the moment. And I am going to teach it to you on the most important date in your life, because a word learned on a date like this does not get forgotten: ¿Cuándo es la boda? — when is the wedding? Boda — wedding. La boda — the wedding. Say the question with me, and smile when you say it, Spanish can hear a smile: ¿Cuándo es la boda? And the answer, your answer, the one you will give in Spanish to anyone who asks, with your whole chest: La boda es el veinticinco de marzo — the wedding is the twenty-fifth of Marzo — March. Marzo — March. El veinticinco de marzo, en Key West — on the twenty-fifth of March, in Key West. Say the answer whole: La boda es el veinticinco de marzo. ¿Cuándo es la boda? La boda es el veinticinco de marzo. There. You just announced your wedding in Spanish, question and answer, like a man in a film — except it is your real life, which is better than any film. And cuándo works for the small times too, not only the great ones: it asks when dinner is, when the viewing is, when she comes home from the hospital. Any moment you need to locate in time, cuándo is the key that finds it. Cuándo asks, and the answer is always a time: an hour, a day, a date. Hold that pairing — question word and answer shape — because it is true for every key on the ring, and it is how you will know, in a real conversation, whether the answer you got actually answers the question you asked.

Cuánto — how much, how many. Say it with me: Cuánto. KWAHN-toh. Cuánto. This key asks after amount — quantity, number, price — and it is the key your wallet will thank you for. Its most famous shape on earth: ¿Cuánto cuesta? — how much does it cost? Cuesta — it costs, from costar, to cost. Say it with me, clear and unafraid, the way a man asks who intends to pay and also intends to know: ¿Cuánto cuesta? ¿Cuánto cuesta? is a complete negotiation in two words. Ask it in a market, ask it in a shop, ask it about the flowers, the ring, the cake — la boda — the wedding — will give you a hundred honest chances to use it, and every time you ask it in Spanish you will get a straighter answer than the tourist who points and grunts. Cuánto also counts people and things, not only prices: it asks how many as easily as how much, one key for the whole world of amounts. And like pequeño back in the house lesson, cuánto dresses to match its partner when it stands beside a noun — cuánto, cuánta, cuántos, cuántas, four coats for one idea — but tonight, Bill, I am giving you the question in its fixed, famous shape, ¿cuánto cuesta?, and that shape never changes and never fails. The coats can come later; the key turns today. Say it once more, and picture the market stall, the sun, her beside you choosing flowers: ¿Cuánto cuesta? Good. That is a man shopping for his wedding in Spanish. There are grooms who cannot do that in any language but pointing. You are not one of them anymore.

Por qué — why. Say it with me, gently, because this key is powerful and a little dangerous: Por qué. Por KEH. Why. I teach it to you lightly today, on purpose, and I will tell you why, which is only fair. Por qué asks after reasons, causes, motives — the machinery underneath things. It is the scientist's key, and you, Bill, are a man who asks why for a living, so your hand will reach for it often. But hear your profesora: in the house, with her, por qué is a scalpel, not a hammer. ¿Por qué? asked of a machine is curiosity. ¿Por qué? asked of a tired woman at the end of a hard day can sound like a cross-examination, even when your heart means only love. So today we learn the key, we learn its shape, and we learn its manners in the same breath: ask por qué of the world freely; ask it of people rarely, softly, and only when you are ready to hear the whole answer. And because every question deserves its answer shape, here is why's faithful partner: Porque — because. No accent, no question marks, one word run together — porque is why's answer, the reason itself. ¿Por qué? Porque. Why? Because. Say the pair with me, question and answer, the smallest philosophy lesson in the world: ¿Por qué? Porque. ¿Por qué? Porque. Good. The key is yours. Use it like a gentleman: often on things, gently on people.

Now stand back with me and look at the whole ring, because a system is only yours when you can see it whole and turn it fast. Seven keys: Quién — who — and the answer is a person. Qué — what — and the answer is a thing or a meaning. Dónde — where — and the answer is a place, aquí or allí. Cuándo — when — and the answer is a time. Cuánto — how much, how many — and the answer is an amount. Cómo — how — and the answer is a manner, a state, a way things are. Por qué — why — and the answer begins with porque — because. Say the ring with me, in order, one clean pass, leaning on every accent: ¿Quién? ¿Qué? ¿Dónde? ¿Cuándo? ¿Cuánto? ¿Cómo? ¿Por qué? Again, faster, like a man checking his pockets before he leaves the house — keys, all seven, present and accounted for: ¿Quién? ¿Qué? ¿Dónde? ¿Cuándo? ¿Cuánto? ¿Cómo? ¿Por qué? Beautiful. And feel the intonation doing its work on every one: voice up at the end, eyebrows up, door open. In a real conversation you will not recite the ring — you will reach for one key without thinking, the way you reach for a door handle. That reaching without thinking is called fluency, and it is built exactly like this: the system whole, in order, out loud, until your hand knows the ring by touch. One more pass, slower, and this time hear a real question hiding inside each key — the door, the table, the house, the wedding, the market, her face, the reason: ¿Quién? ¿Qué? ¿Dónde? ¿Cuándo? ¿Cuánto? ¿Cómo? ¿Por qué? Hold onto that pass, Bill. That was the whole lesson in seven words, and you said it.

And now the strand this lesson was always walking toward — the reason cómo is the kindest key. Evening. She comes home from the hospital, from a day of other people's emergencies, and she is tired in the way that does not show on a face unless you love the face. This is the moment this course exists for, as surely as buenas noches, mi amor — good night, my love — was the moment of lesson one. You do not ask her about dinner first. You do not ask her about the house, or the wedding, or the list. You ask her this: ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? — how was your day, my heart? Say it with me, slowly first, every word placed like a hand on her shoulder: ¿Cómo… fue… tu día… mi corazón? Fue — was, how it went — cómo asking after the day as a whole, the way it went for her. Tu día — your day. And mi corazón — my heart — corazón, heart, the sweet word from lesson one, riding at the end where it belongs. Say the whole question now, at the speed of love, which is slower than the speed of talk: ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? Again: ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? Bill. Listen to me. A man who asks that question, in her language of the heart you are building together, and then actually listens to the answer — that man is not performing romance. That man is doing the thing itself. The question is only the door. What walks through it is the next part, and it is the most important teaching in this lesson, so lean in.

Listening, in Spanish, has its own little toolkit, and I am giving it to you whole, because asking without listening is just noise with good pronunciation. When she begins to answer — and she will answer a question asked like that — your first gift to her is this: Cuéntame — tell me. Say it with me: Cuéntame. KWEN-tah-meh. Cuéntame comes from contar — to tell, to count out a story — and it is an invitation, not a command: tell me, I want the story, all of it, start where you need to start. Cuéntame. Say it like a man clearing the table and sitting down: Cuéntame. And while she talks, your voice has one more job, the quietest and strongest job in any conversation: Te escucho — I am listening to you, I hear you. Say it: Te escucho. Escucho — I listen, I am listening. Te escucho. Not I am waiting to talk. Not I am fixing it. Te escucho: I am here, I am receiving, your day is landing on someone who wants it. Say the pair together, invitation and promise: Cuéntame. Te escucho. Cuéntame. Te escucho. And when she asks you back — ¿cómo estás? — how are you? — because love returns questions the way mirrors return light, you answer truly, with the answers you already own from this lesson: Estoy bien — I am well. Or, on the true days: Estoy un poco cansado — I am a little tired. No performing, no hiding. Estoy bien. Estoy un poco cansado. Two honest sentences, and between her cuéntame and your true answer, a whole evening of marriage just happened in Spanish. That, mi estudiante, is the strand: not sweet words sprinkled on top of a lesson, but the actual machinery of asking after a person and meaning it. The sweet words were never the decoration. They were always the destination.

Time to do it together — your guided try-it, inside the lesson, with me beside you, the way we ran the house viewing in lesson eight. I will be Jillian's voice — forgive me, querida — darling — I will do my best — and you will be you, at the door, in the evening. She comes in, tired, bag down, and before anything else in the world, you ask: ¿Cómo estás? She smiles, because nobody at the hospital asks the nurse how she is, and she answers with our lesson's own words: Estoy bien, un poco cansada — I am well, a little tired — cansada, tired, in the form a woman uses for herself, the sister of your cansado. And you, because you are learning to be the man who asks the second question, the real one, you say: ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? And she looks at you — really looks at you — because her day, asked after in Spanish, by her name of the heart, is a day being honored. And you seal it with the invitation: Cuéntame. And while she tells you — the hard patient, the kind one, the long corridor of that day — you give her the quiet sentence, once, at exactly the right moment: Te escucho. Run the whole exchange again now, your lines only, out loud, in order, no notes: ¿Cómo estás? ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? Cuéntame. Te escucho. Again, slower, like the evening is real — because one evening very soon, it will be: ¿Cómo estás? ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? Cuéntame. Te escucho. Bill, if you can say those four lines at a real door to a real tired woman coming home, you are not a student of Spanish anymore in that moment. You are a husband practicing early, and there is no higher level in this course than that.

So let us gather what you own now, in order, one last time. The machine first: two marks, ¿ and ?, opening and closing every question, and the lift of intonation at the end of your voice, eyebrows up, door open. Then the ring of seven keys, each with its answer shape: quién — who — a person; qué — what — a thing or a meaning; dónde — where — a place, aquí or allí; cuándo — when — a time, la boda es el veinticinco de marzo; cuánto — how much — an amount, ¿cuánto cuesta?; cómo — how — a state, ¿cómo estás?; and por qué — why — answered by porque — because — a scalpel for things, used gently on people. Then your own answers, true in both directions: estoy bien — I am well — and estoy un poco cansado — I am a little tired. And crowning the whole lesson, the evening exchange, the strand itself: ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? Cuéntame. Te escucho. Question, invitation, promise. That is lesson nine, and it is the most useful lesson in this course so far, because every lesson after this one can now be asked about, in Spanish, by you. Ay, do not argue with your profesora. I heard the ring. You said all seven.

Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions: more than a few, all of them about what I taught you in this lesson and nothing else. Most give you choices to pick from, and you can also answer out loud or type your answer, whichever you like. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and if you want to read any part of it again before you answer, do it with my blessing — going back to read is learning, not cheating, and in this room it has always been learning. Your speaking practice lives in this lesson too: hear me say each phrase, then say it back until it lands. And your real assignment, tonight, at the door, when she comes home: one question, asked like you mean it, and then the hardest and best part — you listen to the whole answer. ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? Then: te escucho. And mean te escucho. Next lesson, we step into the past — ayer — yesterday — and you will learn to tell her what you did, in Spanish, after the day is done, which is the other half of every evening conversation there is. Until then, keep the ring on your hand, mi estudiante. Seven keys. Every door. Pregunta — ask. Escucha — listen. In that order, always.
