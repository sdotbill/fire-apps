# Lesson 3 — Numbers, money, and prices: counting what things cost

Hola, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, mi estudiante — my student — and roll up your sleeves, because today we do something every grown man needs in a second language: we count. Lesson one gave you your name and your first sweet words. Lesson two gave you your people — la familia, your family, named and presented with pride. Today, lesson three, we give you numbers: how to count from zero to one hundred, how to talk about money, and how to ask what something costs and understand the answer when it comes back at you. And because this is my room, and you are my student, there is a second thread woven through today, the Spanish-for-Jillian strand: compliments. Sweet words with numbers around them. A man should be able to count his money and compliment his woman in the same language, on the same day, without breaking stride. By the end of this lesson, you will do both.

Why numbers, and why now? Because numbers are where a tourist becomes a person. You can smile and say hola in any shop on earth, but the moment money is on the counter — a coffee, a ring, a bouquet of flowers for a certain woman — you need to know what you are being told and what you are agreeing to. Numbers are also hiding inside your real life everywhere: prices, dates, times, ages, addresses, the cost of the wedding you are planning for March the twenty-fifth in Key West. A wedding, Bill, is a beautiful event built almost entirely out of numbers. So today we tame them, all of them, from cero to cien — zero to one hundred — and I promise you, Spanish numbers are kinder than English ones. They follow their pattern honestly. Learn the pattern and the numbers are yours forever.

The way we work has not changed: I say it, you say it back out loud, every time I say say it with me. Numbers especially must live in your mouth, not on the page — you will never have time to read a price tag twice in a real shop, so we drill until your ear hears a number and your mind sees it. When the lesson is finished there will be questions below, only about what I teach today, and the lesson text stays open beside them. Going back to read is learning, not cheating. Ready? Vamos — let us go. And we start, as all counting starts, at the bottom.

Cero — zero. Cero is zero, the empty hand, the starting line. Say it: Cero. And now the first five, and I want them slow and clean, because everything else is built on these. Uno — one. Dos — two. Tres — three. Cuatro — four. Cinco — five. Say the run with me, counting on your fingers like a child, because that is not childish, that is how every human being who ever lived learned to count: Cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco — zero, one, two, three, four, five. Again: Cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco. Uno is a word with a secret second job you will meet later — for now it is simply one, the loneliest and proudest number. Dos, two — short and round. Tres, three — hear the little tap of the r, the sound we practiced in lesson one. Cuatro, four — KWAH-troh, two strong beats. Cinco, five — SEEN-koh. Hold those five. They are the foundation stones.

Now the second five, and the first ten stand complete. Seis — six. Siete — seven. Ocho — eight. Nueve — nine. Diez — ten. Say them with me: Seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez — six, seven, eight, nine, ten. Again, slower: Seis… siete… ocho… nueve… diez. Siete and ocho are the long ones, two beats each — SEE-eh-teh, OH-choh. Nueve, nine, is a word you will love later, because it is a cousin of new — nueve, nuevo, the new thing and the number nine grew up together. And diez, ten, ends the first family with a little flourish, DEE-ehs. Now the whole staircase, zero to ten, in one breath, and do not rush it: Cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez. Again: Cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez. Bill, you can count to ten in Spanish. That is not nothing. That is the first floor of the whole building, and most prices, most counts, most real life starts right there.

Now the teenagers, eleven through fifteen, and I will be honest with you the way I always am: these five are individuals. They each have their own name and they do not follow a pattern yet — you simply learn them, the way you learned the names of your own family. Once — eleven. Doce — twelve. Trece — thirteen. Catorce — fourteen. Quince — fifteen. Say them with me: Once, doce, trece, catorce, quince — eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen. Again: Once, doce, trece, catorce, quince. Once sounds like OHN-seh — yes, it looks like the English word once, but in Spanish it is eleven, and now you will never forget it, because that little surprise is a hook all by itself. Doce, twelve, is a cousin of dozen — hear it? Doce, dozen, twelve. Quince, fifteen, is the famous one — a quinceañera is a girl's fifteenth birthday celebration, one of the great parties of the Spanish-speaking world, and the word quince sits right at the front of it. Learn these five as five people: once, doce, trece, catorce, quince.

And now, sixteen through nineteen, Spanish starts building instead of naming, and this is where the language shows you its honest machinery. Dieciséis — sixteen. Look at it: dieci is diez, ten, folded in, and séis is seis, six — ten and six, sixteen, the parts sitting right on the surface of the word. Diecisiete — seventeen, ten and seven. Dieciocho — eighteen, ten and eight. Diecinueve — nineteen, ten and nine. Say the family with me: Dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve — sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen. You are not memorizing four new words here, Bill. You are learning one trick — diez folded together with the small number — and the trick makes the words for you. That is how this language likes to work, and it is why I say Spanish is honest: it shows you its parts.

And then, the round one that closes the first twenty: Veinte — twenty. Veinte is twenty, a word of its own, VEIN-teh. Say it: Veinte. So here is your whole first country, zero to twenty, and let us walk it once at a steady pace, the way you would count bills out of a wallet: Cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez, once, doce, trece, catorce, quince, dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve, veinte. One more time, and this time feel how the ground changes under you — the first ten are names, eleven to fifteen are names, sixteen to nineteen are built, and twenty is the gate to the rest: Cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez, once, doce, trece, catorce, quince, dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve, veinte. Beautiful. Hold onto veinte, because everything above it is built from the tens, and the tens are next — but first, a breath, and a check. So if diez is ten and I add seis, I get dieciséis, sixteen — hold onto that building trick. It is about to make you rich in numbers.

Now the tens, the big round numbers, the ones prices actually live on. Treinta — thirty. Cuarenta — forty. Cincuenta — fifty. Sesenta — sixty. Setenta — seventy. Ochenta — eighty. Noventa — ninety. Cien — one hundred. Say the whole row with me, slow and proud: Treinta, cuarenta, cincuenta, sesenta, setenta, ochenta, noventa, cien — thirty, forty, fifty, sixty, seventy, eighty, ninety, one hundred. Again: Treinta, cuarenta, cincuenta, sesenta, setenta, ochenta, noventa, cien. Now look at them with your profesora's eyes and find the hooks, because they are full of them. Treinta carries tres, three, inside it — thirty is three tens, and Spanish lets you hear the three. Cuarenta carries cuatro, four. Cincuenta carries cinco, five. Sesenta carries seis, setenta carries siete, ochenta carries ocho, noventa carries nueve. Do you see it? The tens are the small numbers wearing a working coat. You already own the roots; the tens are just the roots grown up. And cien, one hundred, stands alone at the top like a king — cien, short, clean, complete.

Between the tens, Spanish does the most sensible thing in the world: it joins the ten and the small number with our little friend y — and — from lesson two. Veintiuno — twenty-one: veinte folded with uno. Treinta y uno — thirty-one: thirty and one, said as two words with y between them. Treinta y dos — thirty-two. Cuarenta y cinco — forty-five. Cincuenta y ocho — fifty-eight. Say a few with me, and build them yourself as we go: Treinta y uno. Treinta y dos. Cuarenta y cinco. Cincuenta y ocho. Noventa y nueve — ninety-nine, the last stop before cien. Do you feel it, Bill? You do not know ninety-nine as a memorized word. You build it: noventa, y, nueve. Ninety and nine. Any number I point at between twenty-one and ninety-nine, you can now build out loud from parts you own. That is not vocabulary. That is a machine, and it is in your hands.

Let us put the machine to work right now, guided, the way we do things in this room — I give you a number in English, you build it in Spanish, out loud, and then I say it so you can check your work against my voice. Twenty-five. Build it: veinte family… no — twenty-five is past veinte, so we say veinticinco — twenty-five, veinte folded with cinco in the twenties, the one place Spanish folds instead of joining. Say it: Veinticinco. Thirty-seven: treinta y siete — thirty and seven. Say it: Treinta y siete. Forty-two: cuarenta y dos. Say it: Cuarenta y dos. Sixty-three: sesenta y tres. Say it: Sesenta y tres. Eighty-one: ochenta y uno. Say it: Ochenta y uno. And the big one, one hundred: cien. Say it: Cien. Ay, Bill — you just built numbers you were never taught, out of parts, correctly, in your third lesson. That is the difference between studying a language and learning how it thinks.

Now we give the numbers their natural home: money. El dinero — money. Dinero is the word for money itself. El dinero. Say it: El dinero. Every man in this world has a relationship with dinero — earning it, counting it, spending it wisely, spending it on flowers when wisdom takes the afternoon off. In Spanish the word sits comfortably in sentences you already almost own. Tengo dinero — I have money. Tengo is a giant of a word: tengo means I have. You met its family in lesson two's future — no, you meet it right now, clean: Tengo — I have. Tengo dinero — I have money. Say it with me, like a man who earned it: Tengo dinero. And its honest opposite, which every man has also said in his life: No tengo dinero — I do not have money, I have no money. No in Spanish is the same word as English no, doing the same job — put it in front and the sentence turns around. No tengo dinero. Say it: No tengo dinero. Hopefully you need that one less and less, mi estudiante, but a complete man owns both sentences.

And the verb that walks beside money everywhere: Quiero comprar — I want to buy. Quiero means I want — from querer, the same verb that gave you te quiero in lesson one, I love you, because in Spanish wanting and loving were never separate ideas, remember? Quiero comprar: comprar means to buy. Quiero comprar — I want to buy. Say it: Quiero comprar. And now the sentence becomes a tool the moment you point it at something. Quiero comprar flores — I want to buy flowers. Flores means flowers. Quiero comprar flores. Say it with me, and think of exactly who they are for: Quiero comprar flores. And a man planning a wedding in Key West might also say: Quiero comprar un anillo — I want to buy a ring. Un anillo means a ring. Quiero comprar un anillo. Say it: Quiero comprar un anillo. Tengo, quiero, comprar — I have, I want, to buy. Three verbs and a man can walk into any shop in the Spanish-speaking world and start a real transaction.

Which brings us to the question this whole lesson bends toward, the question you will ask more than any other in shops, markets, and cafés for the rest of your life. ¿Cuánto cuesta? — how much does it cost? Cuánto means how much, cuesta comes from costar, to cost. ¿Cuánto cuesta? Say it with me, with the polite tilt of a man who is interested but not desperate: ¿Cuánto cuesta? Again: ¿Cuánto cuesta? That question, Bill, is a key — the money key, to stand beside por favor and gracias, the politeness keys from lesson one. Walk into a market in Key West during wedding week, pick up something beautiful, and ask ¿Cuánto cuesta? — and you are no longer a tourist pointing and grunting. You are a customer. A Spanish-speaking customer. The whole conversation changes temperature when a man asks the price in the language of the shop.

And the answer will come back in numbers — your numbers, the ones you built today — usually riding on a small word: dólares — dollars. Dólares means dollars. Cuesta veinte dólares — it costs twenty dollars. Cuesta veinte dólares. Hear the shape: cuesta, then the number, then dólares. Say it: Cuesta veinte dólares. Another: Cuesta cincuenta dólares — it costs fifty dollars. Say it: Cuesta cincuenta dólares. And a small one: Cuesta cinco dólares — it costs five dollars. Say it: Cuesta cinco dólares. So the exchange, whole, the way it will really happen: you hold up the thing, you ask ¿Cuánto cuesta?, and the answer comes: Cuesta treinta dólares — it costs thirty dollars. Run that exchange with me now, both parts, you asking and you answering: ¿Cuánto cuesta? Cuesta treinta dólares. ¿Cuánto cuesta? Cuesta treinta dólares. Your ear is the skill here, Bill — when a number flies at you in a real shop, you need it to land. That is why we drill.

Now, once you know the price, a man needs an opinion about it, and Spanish gives you two words that cover almost every price ever named. Es caro — it is expensive. Caro is expensive, the price that makes you whistle. Es caro. Say it: Es caro. And its opposite, the price that makes you smile: Es barato — it is cheap, it is a good price, the price that makes you smile. Barato is cheap, a bargain. Es barato. Say it: Es barato. Hear them side by side: Caro, barato. Expensive, cheap. Es caro. Es barato. Say the pair with me: Es caro. Es barato. And notice — es, the same little word from lesson two, ella es, él es — es, is. It costs thirty dollars and es caro, or es barato — price and opinion, two sentences, and you sound like a man who has bought things before and cannot be fooled. One warning from your profesora, with love: barato is a happy word in Spanish, a bargain, a good deal — it does not insult the goods the way cheap sometimes can in English. Use it freely. Es barato is a compliment to your own shopping.

Let us change the scenery now, because a phrase is only yours when it survives a new room. Picture the wedding week in Key West. You slip away in the morning — alone, on a mission — to a flower shop near the water, because a man does not arrive at his own wedding week empty-handed. You greet the room, the way I taught you in lesson one: Buenos días — good morning. The woman in the shop smiles. You look at the flowers, the beautiful ones, and you say your sentence whole: Quiero comprar flores — I want to buy flowers. She shows you an arrangement that would make Jillian's whole face change. You ask the key question: ¿Cuánto cuesta? She answers: Cuesta cuarenta dólares — it costs forty dollars. Forty. Cuarenta. You built that number today; you hear it land cleanly. And you, Bill, a man who knows a bargain when the flowers are for her, you smile and you say: Es barato — it is a good price. Because for her, at that price, it is. She wraps them. Gracias — thank you — por favor riding along where it belongs, and you walk out into the Key West morning with flowers in your hand and every word of that transaction spoken in Spanish. Same words as my classroom. New room. Still yours.

And now — ah, now we arrive at the part of the lesson that is not really about flowers, and you knew it was coming, because this is lesson three and in my room the odd-numbered lessons carry the sweet strand. You have spent forty minutes learning to count what things cost. Now learn to say what no money counts. The compliments. And we begin with the word itself. Bonita — pretty. Bonita is pretty, for a woman, and it is one of the kindest words in Spanish. Bonita. Say it: Bonita. It sounds like what it means — soft at the front, bright at the end. A dress can be bonita, a day can be bonita, but the word was born, I am convinced, for a woman's face. Say it once more, slower: Bonita.

Now build the compliment whole, because a word alone is a note and a sentence is music. Eres muy bonita — you are very pretty. Eres means you are — soy is I am, from lesson one; eres is its partner, you are. Muy means very. Eres muy bonita — you are very pretty. Say it with me, and aim it where it belongs, at the picture of her in your head: Eres muy bonita. Again, slower, like you mean every syllable: Eres… muy… bonita. Eres muy bonita. Bill, that sentence, said at the right moment — not as a performance, just at the kitchen door, in the morning, when she is not expecting it — that sentence will do more than the flowers. Flowers are cuarenta dólares. That sentence is free, and it is worth more. Say it one more time: Eres muy bonita.

Second compliment, and this one is for a specific day — today, any today, the day she has gotten ready and she looks like the woman in the photographs men keep forever. Te ves hermosa hoy — you look beautiful today. Take it apart gently. Te ves means you look — how you appear, what the eyes see. Hermosa means beautiful — a deeper word than bonita, the beauty that stops a conversation. Hoy means today. Te ves hermosa hoy — you look beautiful today. Say it with me: Te ves hermosa hoy. Again: Te ves hermosa hoy. Hoy is the word that makes it live in the present tense of a real marriage, Bill — not you were beautiful at some party in some photograph. Hoy. Today. This morning. In that dress, in this light. Te ves hermosa hoy. A compliment with hoy in it can be said every single day and never wear out, because every day it is true in a new way.

And the third compliment, my favorite, because it is not about beauty at all — it is about joy. Me gusta tu sonrisa — I like your smile. Sonrisa is smile. Me gusta means I like, it pleases me — literally, it pleases me, your smile pleases me. Tu is your, from lesson two — tu sonrisa, your smile. Me gusta tu sonrisa — I like your smile. Say it with me: Me gusta tu sonrisa. Again, warm: Me gusta tu sonrisa. Of all the compliments in this lesson, Bill, this is the one that tells her why. Bonita tells her what you see. Hermosa tells her what you feel. But me gusta tu sonrisa tells her that her happiness itself is the thing you love looking at — and a woman who hears that, in any language, hears that she is loved for her joy and not only her face. Say it once more, for her: Me gusta tu sonrisa.

Let us do the guided moment now, the real examination — not the questions below, those are friendly — the real one: a whole morning, with money and sweetness in it, at conversation speed, with you speaking into my silences. Ready? Morning, Key West, the day before the wedding. You are in the little shop. Greet the room. … Buenos días. The flowers, tell her what you want. … Quiero comprar flores. She names the price of the big arrangement — cuarenta y cinco dólares, forty-five dollars — and you, checking you heard it, you ask the price yourself first. Ask it. … ¿Cuánto cuesta? Good. And your opinion — for her, at forty-five, it is a bargain. Say it. … Es barato. Now you are home, and she comes into the room ready for the day, and the whole shop is forgotten because she is standing in the light. Tell her she is very pretty. … Eres muy bonita. Tell her how she looks today. … Te ves hermosa hoy. And the one about her joy, the one that is really about why. … Me gusta tu sonrisa. Bill. Stop. Look at what just happened. You greeted a shop, bought flowers, asked a price, judged it, and came home and complimented your prometida three ways — in Spanish, in one unbroken morning, in your third lesson. A man who can do that is not studying Spanish anymore. He is living a small, real piece of his life in it.

A few hooks before we gather it all, so today's numbers stay yours. Uno, dos, tres — hang them on the count you have heard in a hundred songs and movies; your ear owned them before your mind did. Doce hangs on dozen — twelve, the same root. Once hangs on its own surprise — it looks like once and means eleven, and surprises are unforgettable. Cien hangs on century and cent — a hundred years, a hundred cents, cien, the same old root for one hundred running under both languages. Cuesta hangs on cost — ¿cuánto cuesta? is how much does it cost with the family resemblance right on its face. Caro hangs on the English word dear, which once meant expensive too — a dear price, es caro, the costly thing. And sonrisa — hang it on her. That is the best hook in the lesson and you already own it: every time you see her smile between now and March the twenty-fifth, the word will be there. Me gusta tu sonrisa.

So let us gather what you own now, in order, one last time. The count: cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez — zero through ten. Once, doce, trece, catorce, quince — eleven through fifteen, the five individuals. Dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve — sixteen through nineteen, built from diez and the small number. Veinte — twenty. The tens: treinta, cuarenta, cincuenta, sesenta, setenta, ochenta, noventa, cien — thirty through one hundred, and everything between the tens built with y — treinta y uno, cuarenta y cinco, noventa y nueve. The money words: el dinero, money; tengo, I have — tengo dinero, no tengo dinero; quiero comprar, I want to buy — quiero comprar flores, quiero comprar un anillo. The key question: ¿Cuánto cuesta? — how much does it cost — answered cuesta veinte dólares, it costs twenty dollars, and judged with es caro, it is expensive, or es barato, it is a good price. And the sweet strand, the heart riding inside the counting lesson: bonita, pretty; eres muy bonita, you are very pretty; te ves hermosa hoy, you look beautiful today; me gusta tu sonrisa, I like your smile. Count your money, Bill, and compliment your woman. Today you can do both in Spanish.

That is lesson three, and you did beautifully — do not argue with your profesora, I counted along with you and your numbers are clean. Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions: all of them about what I taught you in this lesson and nothing else — nobody will ask you one single thing I did not teach. Most give you choices to pick from, and you can also answer out loud or type your answer, whichever door you like. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and if you want to read a part again before you answer, do it with my blessing — that is what it is there for. And your speaking practice is here in the lesson too: hear me say each phrase, then say it back until it lands and you see the green check. Your assignment tonight has two halves, and both are pleasant. First: count things in your house in Spanish — the stairs, the windows, the dollars in your wallet — out loud, cero to cien where you can. Numbers become yours by being used on real things. Second, and you already know it: tonight, or tomorrow morning at the kitchen door, one compliment, out loud, to her. Eres muy bonita is my suggestion, but any of the three will do, and she will not mind which. Lesson four will take you to work — your company, your clients, and what you sell, in Spanish. Until then: gracias — thank you — for my third class, Bill. Count well, spend wisely, and compliment her like a man who means it. Buenas noches, mi amor — you know that one is for her too.
