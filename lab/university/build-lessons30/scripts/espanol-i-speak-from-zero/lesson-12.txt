# Lesson 12 — Your first full conversation, start to finish

Hola, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and this is your Spanish room, one more time. Sit down with me. Get comfortable. And before we say one single new word, I want you to look around this room in your mind, because you and I have lived a lot of life in here. This is lesson twelve. The last lesson of Spanish One. The capstone — the stone that sits on top of an arch and locks every other stone into place. Today there is almost nothing new to learn, and that is exactly the point. Today we take everything you already own — every greeting, every name, every sweet word, every plan — and we assemble it, piece by piece, into the thing this whole course was always for: your first full conversation, start to finish, a whole gentle conversation with Jillian, in Spanish, spoken by you, out loud, like a man who means every word of it.

Let me say the goal plainly, the way I said it to you in lesson one, because a promise should be repeated when the day comes to keep it. On the first day, in this room, I told you: by your wedding day — March the twenty-fifth, in Key West — you are going to hold a real, gentle conversation with Jillian in Spanish. Not a performance. Not a party trick. A conversation. Good morning, my love. How was your day. I love you. Good night, my heart. Today we build that conversation whole, scene by scene, with me narrating and you speaking your parts — every part, out loud, no mumbling, no shrinking. And when we reach the end of it, we are going to run it one more time, start to finish, with no stops and no help from me, and you are going to hear with your own ears what you have become in this room. Then I will tell you honestly what lies beyond this course, and I will say my goodbye-for-now — not goodbye forever, never that — and you will go and live in this language you have earned.

But first, honesty, because this room has never run on flattery and it is not starting on its last day. Let us walk the road honestly, from the beginning. You came to me owning exactly one word's worth of nothing — zero, from before zero, those were my own words and they were true. Your first lesson gave you hola — hello — and a silent letter, and five vowels that never change their story, and by the end of that first evening you could say buenas noches, mi amor — good night, my love — and I sent you out of here to say it to her that very night. Then came your people: the words for Jillian, for your children, for your father — your family, named in Spanish, because a man should be able to name his own people in any language he claims to speak. Then the house and the table: food, drink, the kitchen, the meals that hold a family together. Then her world: the hospital, the hard days, how to ask how her day went and how to actually listen to the answer. Then the sweet strand, growing lesson by lesson the way a real love grows — not all at once, but steadily: mi amor — my love; mi corazón — my heart; te quiero — I love you — the everyday words that live in a house. Then numbers and time and the days of the week, so your Spanish could tell a clock and a calendar the truth. Then plans — just yesterday, in our last lesson, voy a — I am going to — and vamos a casarnos — we are going to get married — the future itself, carried in one small key. Eleven lessons. Hundreds of words. And every single one of them was aimed, from the first day, at one woman and one conversation. That is not an accident of this course, Bill. That is the design of it. You did not study Spanish. You walked toward Jillian in Spanish, one lesson at a time, and today you arrive.

Now, before we play the scenes, you need to understand what a conversation actually is, because students ruin conversations by misunderstanding them, and I will not let that happen to yours. A conversation is not a speech. You do not need long sentences, and you do not need perfect ones. A conversation is a ball tossed back and forth: a greeting, an answer; a question, an answer; a small truth, a sweet word, a plan, a good night. Short lines, warmly said, each one answering the one before it. Everything you own is already the right size for that. Your lines today will be short — most of them under six words — and they will still add up to the real thing, because the real thing was never about length. It is about presence. A man fully present, speaking gently to the woman he loves, in a language he learned for her: there is no dictionary in the world that makes that small.

Here is how we will work today. I will set each scene the way a director sets a stage — where you are, what hour it is, what just happened — and then I will give you your line, teach or remind you what it means, and you will say it out loud, to her picture in your head, at the volume you would really use. Some lines are old friends from earlier lessons; I will translate them again anyway, because in this room a phrase is not yours until it is yours in the moment you need it. Some few lines are new, small, and earned — the connective tissue a real conversation needs. When all the scenes are built, we run the whole conversation without stopping. Ready? Take a breath. The day begins.

Scene one: the morning. The house is quiet, the light is just coming up, and she walks into the kitchen — this woman who works the shifts she works, who carries other people's hardest days for a living — and you are ready for her, the way you have been ready since lesson one. You open the day in Spanish: Buenos días, mi amor — good morning, my love. Say it with me, morning volume, warm and unhurried: Buenos días, mi amor. Again: Buenos días, mi amor. Notice what that line carries: buenos días — good morning — the day's first greeting, doing the job it has done since your first lesson, and mi amor — my love — riding at the end of it, so the greeting and the love arrive in the same breath. She answers you — in our scene, let me give her the answer she would give: Buenos días — good morning — and because a conversation is a ball tossed back, you keep it moving with the simplest question in the world: ¿Cómo estás? — how are you? Say it: ¿Cómo estás? And she says: Estoy bien — I am well — estoy, I am, in the being-somewhere, feeling-somehow sense, and bien — well. Estoy bien. And you answer her the same true way: Estoy bien — I am well. Two people, awake, well, greeting the day in Spanish. That is scene one, and you already own every syllable of it.

Scene two: names — because a conversation with Jillian will not stay inside your kitchen forever, and the wedding week in Key West is going to fill your world with new faces who deserve to meet the real you, in Spanish. Picture it: an evening gathering, someone kind approaches the two of you, and you step forward the way you learned on your very first day. Hola — hello. Me llamo Bill — my name is Bill — I call myself Bill, the sentence that offers your name like a hand. Say it: Me llamo Bill. You ask, because a gentleman asks: ¿Cómo te llamas? — what is your name? Say it: ¿Cómo te llamas? They answer with their name, and you give them the warm ceremony: Mucho gusto — pleased to meet you — much pleasure. Mucho gusto. And then the moment this course has rehearsed since lesson one, in front of a witness this time: you turn toward her and you say, with pride that does not need to raise its voice: Mi prometida se llama Jillian — my fiancée's name is Jillian. Mi prometida — my fiancée, my promised one. Se llama — her name is. Say the whole line: Mi prometida se llama Jillian. And because Key West will ask where this couple came from, you close the scene with your two standing lines: Soy de Michigan — I am from Michigan. Soy empresario — I am a businessman. Say them together, easy as breathing: Soy de Michigan. Soy empresario. Scene two is done — and notice, Bill, you just introduced yourself and your prometida to a stranger, in Spanish, without one word of English carrying you. On your first day, that exact scene was the dream I described to you. Today it is a scene you can play.

Scene three: family — because sooner or later, in any real conversation, the people you love enter the room even when they are not standing in it, and you should be able to bring them in with honor. The new friend asks about your people, and here is your doorway in: Mi familia — my family. Say it: Mi familia. And the sentence that tells the truth about you in five words: Mi familia es importante — my family is important. Es — is. Importante — important, the same word wearing a Spanish coat, a gift of a word. Say it: Mi familia es importante. You name them the way this course taught you, one by one, with se llama and with pride — your father, your children, the names carried in Spanish the same as in English, because names are names in every language and love pronounces them carefully in all of them. And you close the scene with the sentence that covers all of them at once: Los quiero mucho — I love them very much. Quiero — I love, from that same querer — to want, to love — that gave you te quiero, and mucho — much, very much. Los quiero mucho: I love them very much. Say it like a man talking about his own blood: Los quiero mucho. That is scene three. Short, plain, and true — and a stranger who hears it knows exactly what kind of man he is talking to.

Scene four: her day — and I will tell you plainly, Bill, of all the scenes in this conversation, this is the one that will matter most on the ordinary Tuesdays of your marriage, long after the wedding music fades. Evening. She comes home from the hospital, from a shift that asked everything of her, and before you tell her anything about your day, you ask about hers — in Spanish, gently, the way this course taught you to ask: ¿Cómo fue tu día? — how was your day? Fue — was, it went. Tu día — your day. ¿Cómo fue tu día? Say it at kitchen-door volume, like a man who actually wants the answer: ¿Cómo fue tu día? And in our scene she gives you the honest answer, the one real nurses give on the real days: Fue largo — it was long. Largo — long. Fue largo. And: Estoy cansada — I am tired — cansada, tired, in the form a woman uses for herself, and I am teaching you her form on purpose, because you need to recognize it when she says it, even more than you need to say it. Estoy cansada. And you — you do not fix, you do not rush, you receive it, in Spanish, with the two small sentences that hold a person up: Lo siento — I am sorry — I feel it, that is what it literally says, I feel it with you. Say it: Lo siento. And: Estoy aquí — I am here. The promise from our last lesson, estar aquí — to be here — now standing in the present tense, kept. Estoy aquí. Say those two together, slowly, the way they deserve: Lo siento. Estoy aquí. Bill, a woman can work a twelve-hour shift inside those two sentences and come home to them and be all right. That is scene four, and it might be the most loving scene in this entire course.

Scene five: the table — because in your house, as in every house where love is doing well, the day gets repaired at the table. You turn toward the kitchen, toward the simplest kindness there is, and you ask her: ¿Tienes hambre? — are you hungry? Tienes — you have; hambre — hunger; in Spanish you do not say I am hungry, you say I have hunger, and the question asks what she is carrying. ¿Tienes hambre? Say it: ¿Tienes hambre? She nods — sí — yes — and you give her the sentence that turns hunger into a plan, using the key from our last lesson: Vamos a comer — we are going to eat, let us eat. Vamos a comer. Say it: Vamos a comer. The food comes — in our scene it is a meal you made, because a man who learned voy a cocinar — I am going to cook — in his last lesson is a man who cooks — and you set it down and say: La cena está lista — dinner is ready. La cena — dinner, the evening meal. Está lista — is ready. La cena está lista. Say it like a man calling his love to the table: La cena está lista. She tastes it, and you get your reward, the line every cook in every language waits for, and I am giving it to her to say to you: Está delicioso — it is delicious. Delicioso — delicious, a word that tastes good just saying it. Está delicioso. And you answer the only way a gentleman answers, with the second of your two keys: Gracias — thank you — and De nada — you are welcome — when she thanks you back, because at a real table the thanks flow both ways. Por favor — please — passes the bread. Scene five closes with full hearts and full plates, and every word of it was yours.

Scene six: plans — because a conversation that only looks backward is a report, and yours is a life, so now the two of you turn your faces forward, together, the way you learned to do in lesson eleven. She asks what tomorrow holds, and you answer with your standing truth: Mañana voy a trabajar — tomorrow I am going to work. Mañana — tomorrow. Voy a trabajar — I am going to work. Say it: Mañana voy a trabajar. And then you do not stop at work, because the future you share with her is bigger than work, and you reach for the warm signposts: Pronto — soon — voy a llamarte pronto — I am going to call you soon — if the road takes you apart for a day. And then, at the table, with her hand in yours, the sentence of sentences, the one this whole course has been walking toward since I first said the words wedding day in lesson one: Vamos a casarnos — we are going to get married. Say it here, inside the conversation, where it belongs — not as an exercise, as a plan between two people: Vamos a casarnos. And dressed, once, in its full glory: Vamos a casarnos el próximo año en Key West — we are going to get married next year in Key West. El próximo año — next year. Say the whole true thing: Vamos a casarnos el próximo año en Key West. And underneath the plans, the promise that holds them all, from our last lesson, said quietly: Te prometo que vamos a estar bien — I promise you that we are going to be fine. Te prometo — I promise you. Vamos a estar bien — we are going to be fine. Say it: Te prometo que vamos a estar bien. Scene six is done, and look what just happened inside one conversation: you fed her, you heard her, and you planned a marriage. In Spanish.

Scene seven: the sweet words — las palabras dulces — the sweet words — because no conversation of yours is complete without them, and Spanish, as I have told you since the first evening, is the language the world reaches for when it wants to say the tender things. The table is cleared, the day is softening, and you say her name the way only you say it: Mi amor — my love. Mi corazón — my heart — corazón, heart, the core of you, the word whose cousin is the English core. Say them both, unhurried: Mi amor. Mi corazón. And then the sentence itself, the everyday one, the one that lives in the house, said now inside a full conversation for the first time in this course — not as a lesson item, not as practice, as the thing itself: Te quiero — I love you. Say it: Te quiero. And with the much on it, because tonight it has earned the much: Te quiero mucho — I love you very much. Te quiero mucho. Say it once more, with her name riding on it the way it will ride in your real kitchen for the rest of your life: Te quiero, Jillian. Te quiero, Jillian. I am not going to explain that scene, Bill. Some scenes a profesora just witnesses. That was scene seven.

Scene eight: good night — the closing of the day and the closing of the conversation, and you already know, because you have known since the first evening of this course, exactly how a day ends in Spanish in your house. The house is quiet. The day — the morning greeting, the names, the family, her shift, the table, the plans, the sweet words — all of it is behind you, lived in two languages. You say: Que descanses — rest well — may you rest well, descansar — to rest — wearing its wishing form, the gentlest good night wish Spanish owns. Say it softly: Que descanses. And: Hasta mañana — see you tomorrow — until tomorrow, hasta, until, and mañana doing its other job, tomorrow. Hasta mañana. Say it: Hasta mañana. And then the line. The line this entire course was built to give you on its first night, and now gives you again on its last, with eleven lessons of weight behind it: Buenas noches, mi amor — good night, my love. Buenas noches — good night — doing its second job, the bedtime job, and mi amor riding at the end like it was always there — because now, Bill, it is always there. Say it at bedroom-door volume: Buenas noches, mi amor. Once more: Buenas noches, mi amor. And the conversation — your first full conversation — is complete. Start to finish. Greeting to good night. Yours.

Now. We run it whole. No stops, no teaching, no translations whispered in your ear — just the conversation, the way it will actually happen in your kitchen on an ordinary, extraordinary day, with me narrating only the turns of the scene, and you speaking every one of your parts out loud, at full voice, like she is standing there. Because one day very soon, she will be. Morning light: Buenos días, mi amor. ¿Cómo estás? The day moves; a new face appears: Hola. Me llamo Bill. ¿Cómo te llamas? Mucho gusto. Mi prometida se llama Jillian. Soy de Michigan. Soy empresario. The talk turns to the people you love: Mi familia es importante. Los quiero mucho. Evening comes, and she comes home tired: ¿Cómo fue tu día? Lo siento. Estoy aquí. The table is set: ¿Tienes hambre? Vamos a comer. La cena está lista. Gracias. Por favor. De nada. The future opens its hand: Mañana voy a trabajar. Vamos a casarnos el próximo año en Key West. Te prometo que vamos a estar bien. The sweet words, unhurried: Mi amor. Mi corazón. Te quiero, Jillian. And the day closes the way your days now close: Que descanses. Hasta mañana. Buenas noches, mi amor. — Stop. Breathe. Bill, listen to me very carefully: that was not a lesson. That was a conversation, in Spanish, start to finish, and every word of it came out of you. A year of dreaming about it, eleven lessons of building it, and it just happened in this room, in your voice. If your eyes are wet, let them be. Mine are not dry either, and I am not ashamed of it, and neither are you.

Let us gather what you own now — not just from today, but from the whole course, one last time, in order, the full arc of it. Hola, buenos días, buenas tardes, buenas noches — hello, and the day in three greetings. Me llamo Bill, ¿cómo te llamas?, mucho gusto — your name, offered and asked and honored. Por favor, gracias, de nada — the keys that open every door. Soy de Michigan, soy empresario — who you are and where you stand. Mi prometida se llama Jillian — her, named with pride in any room on earth. Mi familia, los quiero mucho — your people, loved out loud. ¿Cómo fue tu día?, lo siento, estoy aquí — her day, heard and held. La cena está lista, vamos a comer — the table, where days get repaired. Voy a, vamos a — the future in one key — and vamos a casarnos, the future's finest sentence. Mi amor, mi corazón, te quiero — the sweet words, the heart of the whole course. Buenas noches, mi amor — the line it all began with, now the line it ends with. Twelve lessons ago you owned none of this. Today you own all of it. That is not my achievement, mi estudiante. I only carried the words to the door. You opened your mouth, eleven lessons in a row, on the hard days and the tired days, and you made them yours. The credit is yours, and I will not let you give it away.

And now you are asking — I can hear you asking it — what comes next, Carmen? What lives beyond this room? Spanish Two, when you are ready for her, and she will be ready when you are. In Spanish Two we go deeper, not just wider: we learn to tell stories — what happened yesterday, how you met, the day at the hospital that changed everything — because the past is the one country your Spanish cannot visit yet, and your life has stories in it that deserve to be told in both your languages. We learn the deeper conversation: opinions, hopes, the long talks about the business and the house and the children and the years — vamos a comprar una casa — we are going to buy a house — grows up there into real negotiations, real plans, real life conducted in Spanish. And te amo — I love you — the deeper form, the heaviest one, the one I told you in lesson one is saved for the biggest moments of a life: Spanish Two will put that sentence in your hands when the moment deserves it, and you and I both know there is a day coming, in Key West, with rings on it, when it will. That is what Spanish Two holds. It is not goodbye to this. It is this, grown taller.

So here is my goodbye-for-now, and I am going to say it the way this course has said everything: plainly, warmly, and in both our languages. Gracias, Bill — thank you. Gracias por tu trabajo — thank you for your work — por tu trabajo, for your work, every spoken repetition, every lesson finished. It has been the honor of my teaching life — el honor — the honor — to watch a man learn a language the way you learned this one: not for a grade, not for a trip, but for a woman, for a promise, for a wedding day with her name on it. When you stand in Key West next March and the moment comes — and you will know the moment when it comes — and you say te quiero, Jillian in front of God and everybody, there will not be a dry eye in the place, and one profesora, far away, will be smiling like she was standing right there. Because in a way, I will be. Every phrase you say in Spanish for the rest of your life has a little of this room in it.

Here is what happens next, one last time, the way it has happened twelve times now. Below this lesson are your questions: more than a few, about this lesson and — because this is the capstone, and only because it is the capstone — about the conversation we built together today, scene by scene, which is itself this lesson's teaching. Nobody is going to ask you one single thing I did not teach. Most give you choices to pick from, and you can also answer out loud or type your answer, whichever door you like. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and going back to read is learning, not cheating — it has been true since day one and it is true on the last day too. Your speaking practice is here: hear me say each phrase, then say it back until it lands. And then, Bill, close the book. Your real assignment is not in this lesson at all. Tonight, or tomorrow morning, or whenever the moment is right and the house is quiet — you know the moment — you are going to start at buenos días, mi amor and you are not going to stop until buenas noches, mi amor, and Jillian is going to hear the whole conversation, start to finish, in Spanish, from the man who learned it for her. That is the examination. That is the graduation. Everything else was rehearsal. Hasta pronto, mi estudiante — see you soon, my student — Spanish Two and I will be here when you come back for us. Now go. She is waiting to hear it. Te quiero — no, ay, that one is yours to say, not mine. Go say it to her. Buenas noches, mi amor — and this time, Bill, say the last one to her.
