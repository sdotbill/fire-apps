# Lesson 11 — The future: plans, promises, and voy a…

Hola, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Come in, sit down, and take a breath with me, because today we do something we have been walking toward since your very first hola — hello. Until now, most of what you own in Spanish lives in today: who you are, where you are from, what your people are called, how her day went, what is for dinner. Today we open the door that stands in front of today. This is the lesson of the future: plans, promises, and voy a… — I am going to. By the end of our time together you will be able to say what you are going to do tomorrow morning, what you are going to do for your business, what you are going to say to Jillian tonight, and — hold this one in your heart — how to say, in Spanish, that the two of you are going to get married. One small pattern is going to unlock all of that. One pattern, Bill. Not ten rules. Not a chart to memorize while your eyes glaze over. One key, and the whole future turns in the lock.

Let me tell you plainly why this lesson is worth knowing, because I never ask you to learn anything without telling you why. A man who can only speak about today is a man trapped in today. He can greet, he can thank, he can name what is on the table — and all of that is beautiful, and you do it well — but he cannot make a plan out loud. He cannot promise. He cannot say, tomorrow I will be there, next year we will be in our house, one day we will laugh about how hard this season was. Promises live in the future. Plans live in the future. A wedding, mi estudiante — my student — lives entirely in the future until the day it arrives and becomes the present in a white dress. So today we go and get the future, and we bring it back in one pattern you can carry in your pocket for the rest of your life.

And here is the mercy of Spanish, and I want you to hear this before we touch a single verb: Spanish has a complicated, formal future — the kind with special endings that students wrestle for weeks — and real people, in real kitchens and real workplaces, mostly do not use it for everyday plans. Real people use the pattern you are learning today. A mother in a doorway, a boss on a job site, a bride making a list at midnight — they say voy a… — I am going to… — and then they name the action, plain and whole. It is the future of daily life. It is honest, it is warm, and it is the first future any teacher with a heart teaches, because you can use it tonight.

So let us build it, slowly, piece by piece, the way we build everything in this room: I say it, you say it back to me out loud, and only then do we move on.

First, the key itself, alone, before we hang anything on it: Voy a… — I am going to… Voy comes from the verb ir — to go. I want you to feel that in your bones, because English does the exact same trick and you have been doing it all your life without noticing. In English you say, I am going to work — and no, you do not only mean your feet are traveling. You mean the work is ahead of you, in front of you, coming. Spanish says it the same way: voy — I go, or I am going — and then a — to — and then the action, named whole. Voy a… Say it with me, just the key, turning in the lock: Voy a… Again, slower, like you are about to announce something that matters: Voy a… One more time, easy and natural: Voy a… Good. That little phrase, voy a, is the engine. Everything else today is just choosing where the engine takes you.

Now, what rides after voy a? The action, in its whole, untouched form — the form Spanish calls the infinitive — the infinitive. Do not let that word frighten you; you already own a shelf of these and you have been saying them inside other sentences all course long without knowing their name. An infinitive is a verb before anybody has touched it: not I, not you, not we — just the action itself, pure. Hablar — to speak. Comer — to eat. Vivir — to live. In English the little word to does that job: to speak, to eat, to live. In Spanish, the ending of the word does it, and most of the verbs you will ever need end in one of three coats: ar, er, or ir. Trabajar — to work — wears the ar coat. Comer — to eat — wears the er coat. Vivir — to live — wears the ir coat. You do not have to change a thing about these words when they ride after voy a. That is the whole generosity of this pattern, so hear me say it twice: after voy a, the action stays whole. Voy a, plus the whole action, equals the future. If you can say voy a, and you can name one action, you can make a future sentence. So if the pattern is voy a plus a whole action, then any action you learn from today onward is instantly a future you can speak — hold onto that, because it means this lesson never stops growing.

Let us prove it with the sentence your life needs most on a weekday morning: Voy a trabajar — I am going to work. Trabajar — to work. You know this word's family already; trabajo — work, the job itself — has been in our room before, and trabajar is that same word standing up and moving. Voy a trabajar. Say it with me: Voy a trabajar. Again, like a man with a business to build and a reason to build it: Voy a trabajar. Now let us dress it with time, because a plan without a time is a wish. Mañana — tomorrow. Voy a trabajar mañana — I am going to work tomorrow. Say it: Voy a trabajar mañana. And with the morning inside it: Mañana por la mañana — tomorrow morning. Voy a trabajar mañana por la mañana — I am going to work tomorrow morning. Hear how nothing changed in the engine: voy a trabajar stayed exactly itself, and the time words just climbed aboard at the end. That will be true all lesson long. The pattern does not wobble when you add to it. It just carries more.

Stay at work with me a moment, because your future is not abstract, Bill — it has an office and a name and people counting on it. Here is a small toolbox of actions for the working day, each one whole, each one ready to ride. Llamar — to call. Escribir — to write. Hablar — to speak. Escuchar — to listen. Ayudar — to help. Construir — to build. Aprender — to learn. Enseñar — to teach, and also to show. Now watch the key turn, over and over, the same every time. Voy a llamar — I am going to call. Voy a escribir — I am going to write. Voy a hablar — I am going to speak. Voy a escuchar — I am going to listen. Voy a ayudar — I am going to help. Voy a construir — I am going to build. Voy a aprender — I am going to learn. Say two of them with me, out loud, your choice living in your mouth: Voy a ayudar. Voy a construir. Do you feel it? You are not conjugating anything. You are not hunting through endings. You learned one engine and a handful of actions, and suddenly you own a whole working future. That is why I teach this pattern with such love: it pays you back immediately, in sentences about your actual life.

Now let us make it tender, because the most important calls in your life are not business calls. Here is the sentence I promised you at the door: Voy a llamarte — I am going to call you. Look closely, because something sweet just happened to llamar — to call. The little word te — you — the same te from te quiero — I love you — has attached itself to the end of the action, like a hand slipping into a pocket. Llamar plus te makes llamarte — to call you. Voy a llamarte: I am going to call you. Spanish lets you glue that little word right onto the whole action, and the sentence stays soft and natural — it is exactly how a man tells his prometida — his fiancée — that her phone will ring. Say it with me, slowly first: Voy a… llamarte. Voy a llamarte. Again, at the speed of a promise made in a kitchen doorway: Voy a llamarte. And dressed for tonight: Esta noche — tonight. Voy a llamarte esta noche — I am going to call you tonight. Say it: Voy a llamarte esta noche. Bill, that is a complete, romantic, useful sentence, and an hour ago you could not have built it. Notice what you did not do: you did not change llamar at all. You just let te ride at the end. The engine pulled the whole train.

While te is sitting there shining, let me show you its brother, because you will need him before this lesson ends. Me — me — can ride in the same seat. And when the action is something two people do to themselves, together, Spanish uses nos — us, or ourselves. Hold nos in your hand for one minute. We are going somewhere beautiful with him.

Because now we turn the key from I to we, and mi estudiante, this is where this lesson stops being grammar and starts being your life. When the subject is we — you and her, the two of you, the team this whole course is secretly about — voy changes its coat: Vamos a… — we are going to… Vamos comes from that same verb ir — to go — and vamos is its we form; it is also, all by itself, the joyful shout of let us go, which tells you everything about its energy. Vamos a… Say it with me: Vamos a… Again, like a man taking his future wife by the hand: Vamos a… And now, the sentence. The sentence this lesson was built to give you, the one I have been saving since I welcomed you in: Vamos a casarnos — we are going to get married. Take it apart with me slowly, because every piece of it is doing honest work. Casar — to marry. Casarnos — to marry each other, to get married — with that little nos — ourselves, each other — riding at the end, the two of you folded into the very end of the word. Vamos a casarnos: we are going to get married. Say it with me, piece by piece first: Vamos a… casarnos. Vamos a casarnos. Again, whole, at the speed of a man who means it: Vamos a casarnos. One more time, quieter, the way you might say it to her when the house is finally still: Vamos a casarnos.

And because a wedding does not float in the air — it has a date and a place, and you know both — let us dress the sentence the way we dressed your work this morning. El próximo año — next year. Próximo means next, the one coming; año means year. Vamos a casarnos el próximo año — we are going to get married next year. Say it: Vamos a casarnos el próximo año. And the place, because Key West deserves to be named in Spanish in this room: En Key West — in Key West. Vamos a casarnos en Key West — we are going to get married in Key West. Say the full, true sentence with me now, all of it, date and place and promise together: Vamos a casarnos el próximo año en Key West. Bill. Stop there a second. That is not an exercise. That is a fact about your life, spoken in Spanish, correctly, by you. March of next year is coming whether we speak about it or not — but now, when it comes, you can say it coming. There is a difference, and the difference is everything this course is for.

Let us widen the wedding out into the life around it, because vamos a is not a one-sentence trick — it is the sound of the two of you planning a life. Vamos a celebrar — we are going to celebrate. Celebrar — to celebrate. Vamos a viajar — we are going to travel. Viajar — to travel. Vamos a bailar — we are going to dance. Bailar — to dance — and yes, I am putting dancing in your future on purpose, groom. Vamos a comer — we are going to eat — comer, your old friend, to eat. Say the run with me, like a list the two of you are making at the kitchen table: Vamos a celebrar. Vamos a viajar. Vamos a bailar. Vamos a comer. Hear the rhythm of it: vamos a never changes, the actions parade past, and every single one is a plan the two of you can actually make. That is what a future tense is for. Not examinations. Kitchen tables.

Now, the time words deserve their own careful minute, because the future needs signposts, and Spanish keeps them simple and beautiful. You have met mañana — tomorrow — and esta noche — tonight — and el próximo año — next year. Here are the three this lesson adds to your pocket, and I want each one to land. Pronto — soon. Pronto is a warm word; it does not promise a date, it promises nearness. Voy a llamarte pronto — I am going to call you soon. Say it: Voy a llamarte pronto. Hoy — today. Voy a trabajar hoy — I am going to work today. And the dreamer of the family: Un día — one day, someday. Un día is where the big hopes live, the ones without dates yet. Un día, vamos a… — one day, we are going to… Un día is not a lie and it is not a plan; it is a direction, and a life needs directions as much as it needs dates. Say it with me, the whole hopeful shape: Un día, vamos a viajar — one day, we are going to travel. Un día, vamos a viajar. Good. So here is your signpost shelf, in order, near to far: hoy — today; esta noche — tonight; pronto — soon; mañana — tomorrow; el próximo año — next year; un día — one day. Six signposts, and every future sentence you ever make can borrow one. If a sentence names when, then it is a plan — hold onto that. If it does not name when, Spanish still understands you, but a named time is what turns wishing into scheduling, and you, Bill, are a man who schedules.

Now we climb one step higher, from plans to promises — because a plan says what is going to happen, but a promise says what you will make happen, and Spanish marks that difference with a phrase I want you to own completely: Te prometo… — I promise you… Prometo comes from prometer — to promise — and notice that your old friend prometida — fiancée, the promised one — lives inside this family too. The woman you are promised to, and the promises you make to her, share a root in Spanish, because in this language they were never separate ideas. Te prometo: I promise you. Say it with me, slowly, like a vow is supposed to be said: Te prometo… Again: Te prometo… Now, what rides after te prometo is a whole clause — a whole little sentence — and the most natural way to build it, at your level and honestly at any level, is with our key: te prometo, and then voy a, and then the action. Watch: Te prometo que voy a llamarte — I promise you that I am going to call you. Que — that — is the little bridge between the promise and the thing promised. Say it: Te prometo que voy a llamarte. And one with more weight in it: Te prometo que voy a ayudarte — I promise you that I am going to help you. Ayudarte — to help you — ayudar with te riding at the end, the way llamarte did. Say it: Te prometo que voy a ayudarte. Hear how the promise and the plan lock together: prometo makes it sacred, voy a makes it concrete. A promise without a plan is weather talk. A plan inside a promise is a man keeping his word in advance.

Let me give you three promises sized for real life, and I want you to say each one like Jillian is standing in the doorway hearing it — because one day soon, she will be. Te prometo que voy a estar aquí — I promise you that I am going to be here. Estar — to be, in the sense of being somewhere, being present — and aquí — here. For a woman who works the hard shifts she works, estar aquí, being here when she comes home, is not a small promise. Say it: Te prometo que voy a estar aquí. Second: Te prometo que vamos a estar bien — I promise you that we are going to be fine, that we are going to be okay. Bien — well, fine, okay. When money is tight and the season is heavy, this is the sentence. Say it with me: Te prometo que vamos a estar bien. Third, and say this one to the mirror if you have to: Te prometo que voy a aprender — I promise you that I am going to learn. Aprender — to learn. This whole course is that promise, kept a lesson at a time. Say it: Te prometo que voy a aprender. Good. Now you understand the architecture: plans tell the future, promises guarantee it, and voy a is the beam that holds up both houses.

It is time to do, not just listen — this is our try-it moment, and I will narrate while you speak your parts out loud, the way we always do. Picture tomorrow, hour by hour, and answer me in Spanish as I walk you through your own day. Morning comes. You have work that matters. Tell me: what are you going to do? … Voy a trabajar. Yes. The phone sits beside you and the most important person in your world is across town. What are you going to do tonight? … Voy a llamarte esta noche. Beautiful — and if she is the one across from you, say it to her face, the way you just did. Now the two of you, at the table, looking at next year. What are the two of you going to do in Key West? … Vamos a casarnos. There it is. And after the wedding, with the music still in your ears? … Vamos a celebrar. Vamos a bailar. And now the promise, the one that turns today into a vow: tell her you promise you will be here. … Te prometo que voy a estar aquí. Bill, listen to what just happened: you planned a morning, promised a phone call, married yourself in a sentence, celebrated, danced, and made a vow — five futures, one key, zero charts. That is not a student reciting. That is a man speaking about his life in the future tense.

Before we gather it all up, let me hand you the true sentences — the set I want living in your house after this lesson ends, each one a fact about your actual future, stated in Spanish. Say each one after me, and notice that not one of them is pretend. Voy a trabajar mañana — I am going to work tomorrow. Vamos a casarnos en Key West — we are going to get married in Key West. Voy a llamarte esta noche — I am going to call you tonight. Vamos a comprar una casa — we are going to buy a house. Comprar — to buy; una casa — a house. Say it again, because that one deserves a second saying: Vamos a comprar una casa. Voy a construir mi negocio — I am going to build my business. Negocio — business. Un día, vamos a viajar — one day, we are going to travel. Te prometo que vamos a estar bien — I promise you that we are going to be fine. Seven sentences. Work, wedding, the nightly call, the house, the business, the journey, and the promise underneath them all. If you owned nothing else from this lesson — and you own much more — those seven sentences would carry your future in Spanish for years.

One small warning before we gather it all up, because I would rather warn you in this room than let the street surprise you. The only real mistake people make with this pattern is mixing up the two engines: voy a is for I — one man, alone, speaking about himself — and vamos a is for we — two or more, the team speaking about itself. Voy a llamarte — I am going to call you — because one man holds the phone. Vamos a casarnos — we are going to get married — because, ay, Bill, nobody marries himself; it takes the two of you, and the verb knows it. So before any future sentence leaves your mouth, ask one tiny question: is this future mine alone, or is it ours? Mine alone: voy a. Ours: vamos a. That one question, asked for one second, will keep every plan and every promise you ever make in Spanish standing straight. And notice, too, what never changes between them: the a, and the whole action after it. Trabajar stays trabajar whether you say voy a trabajar about your morning or the two of you say vamos a trabajar about a Saturday project together. The engine's front changes its coat; the cargo never moves.

Let me give you one more scene to carry, a quiet one, because futures are not only made at weddings and work desks — they are made on ordinary weekends, in ordinary kitchens, and those are the futures that become a life. Picture a Saturday, the two of you, coffee on the table, the week finally loosened its grip. She asks what the day holds, and now you can answer her in Spanish, piece by piece, a whole morning of plans. Vamos a descansar — we are going to rest. Descansar — to rest — and after her shifts and your long weeks, rest is not laziness, it is a plan worth naming out loud. Vamos a comer juntos — we are going to eat together. Juntos — together — a small word that turns eating into an occasion. Voy a cocinar — I am going to cook. Cocinar — to cook — one man, one kitchen, one meal made on purpose. Vamos a hablar — we are going to talk — hablar, to speak, the two of you with nowhere to rush. Say the little run with me, slowly, the way a Saturday morning deserves: Vamos a descansar. Vamos a comer juntos. Voy a cocinar. Vamos a hablar. Hear how the two engines take turns, natural as breathing: ours, ours, mine, ours. That alternation — what I will do, what we will do — is the exact rhythm of a shared life, and you can now keep that rhythm in Spanish.

So let us gather what you own now, in order, one last time. Voy a… — I am going to… — the engine: voy from ir, to go, plus a, plus the action whole and untouched. The action stays whole after voy a — trabajar, llamar, comer, vivir — no endings to hunt, ever. Voy a trabajar — I am going to work. Voy a llamarte — I am going to call you — with te riding at the end of the action. Vamos a… — we are going to… — the we form, for the two of you. Vamos a casarnos — we are going to get married — the sentence of the lesson, with nos folded into its end. The signposts: hoy, esta noche, pronto, mañana, el próximo año, un día — today, tonight, soon, tomorrow, next year, one day. And standing above the plans, the promises: te prometo que voy a… — I promise you that I am going to… — the vow with a plan inside it. One key opened all of that. You turned it yourself.

And then, Bill, there is only one lesson left in Spanish One — our last one together in this course — and you already know what it has to be. Next time, we put everything you own into one whole, gentle conversation, start to finish, the conversation this entire course has been aiming at since the day you said hola. Tonight, your assignment is one true sentence, said out loud, to her: Voy a llamarte esta noche — and then, mi estudiante, you call her. Te prometo — I promise you — she will smile. Hasta pronto — see you soon. Say it back to me: hasta pronto. And go make your future in Spanish.
