# Lesson 4 — Your business: the company, clients, and what you sell

Hola, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, mi estudiante — my student — and today, bring your working self with you, because today we go to work. Lesson one gave you your name and your first sweet words. Lesson two gave you your people, your familia, named and presented. Lesson three gave you numbers, money, and prices — and a few compliments I hope Jillian has already heard. Today, lesson four, we build the third great room of your life in Spanish: your business. The company, the clients, and what you sell. You are a man who built a company with his own hands — soy empresario, I am a businessman, you learned to say that in lesson one and you said it like you meant it. Today we go inside that sentence and furnish it: the office, the phone, the work itself, the clients who trust you, and the verbs that make a business run — work, speak, call, sell — taught by feel, the way this room teaches everything, in your mouth and not in a grammar book.

Why business words, and why now? Because you, Bill, are not a man learning Spanish for a vacation. You are a man building a life and a company at the same time, and half your waking hours are spent inside your work: talking to clients, answering calls, writing messages, sitting in meetings, selling what you sell. If your Spanish only lives at the kitchen table, it lives in half your life. Today it moves into the other half. And there is a practical reason too, the kind you respect: a client who hears a businessman greet him, introduce his company, and talk about the work in Spanish — even simple Spanish, honestly spoken — hears a man who took the trouble. Trouble taken is trust earned, in every language on earth. That is what we are building today: your working voice in Spanish.

The way we work has not changed, and it will never change in my room: I say it, you say it back out loud, every time I say say it with me. Business phrases especially must be said out loud, because at work you will say them on the phone, in a doorway, across a desk — never silently to a page. When the lesson is finished there will be questions below, only about what I teach today, nothing else, and the lesson text stays open beside them so you can read any part again. Going back to read is learning, not cheating. Are you ready to go to work? Vamos — let us go — a trabajar, to work. And yes, that last phrase is your first piece of business Spanish, given free: a trabajar — let us work, to work.

We start with the biggest word of the day, the house everything else lives in. La empresa — the company, the business as a whole, the thing you built. La empresa. Say it with me, slowly first: La… empresa. La empresa. Empresa is a beautiful word when you take it apart: it comes from an old word for undertaking, for a thing a man takes on with both hands. An empresa is not just a company on paper. It is the undertaking. The thing you undertook. When you say la empresa, Spanish hears the weight of that — the risk, the building, the years. Say it once more, like a man who built his: La empresa.

And right beside it, its everyday brother. El negocio — the business, the deal, the work of making a living. El negocio. Say it: El negocio. Negocio is the business in motion — the deals, the trade, the daily turning of the wheel. If empresa is the house, negocio is the work that happens inside the house. You will hear negocio everywhere in the Spanish-speaking world: men talk about el negocio the way men back home talk about the business, the shop, the trade — with respect, because it feeds the family. And hear the hook hiding in it: negocio is a cousin of negotiate — the dealing word. El negocio, the business. Say the pair with me, the house and the work inside it: La empresa. El negocio. La empresa. El negocio.

Now, your company has a name, and Spanish asks for it with a sentence you already own in your bones — because it is built on llamar, the very first verb of this course, the me llamo verb, I call myself. Mi empresa se llama… — my company is called, my company's name is. Mi empresa se llama. Say it with me: Mi empresa se llama. Hear it? Se llama — it calls itself, its name is — the exact same music as me llamo Bill and mi prometida se llama Jillian. You learned this shape in lesson one for a man, you used it in lesson two for the woman you love, and now it names your company. One shape, three treasures: yourself, your prometida, your empresa. A man could build a whole introduction out of that one shape, and before this lesson ends, you will. Say it once more, and put your company's weight behind it: Mi empresa se llama. We will leave the name itself for you to say in the practice — the sentence frame is what I am carving today, and the frame is yours.

A company is not a building, though — a company is people, and the people who make a business a business are the ones on the other side of the desk. Los clientes — the clients, the customers, the people who trust you with their money and their name. Los clientes. Say it with me: Los clientes. Clientes and clients — you can hear the English cousin inside it immediately, KLEE-en-tehs, the same word wearing Spanish clothes. And notice los — the, for more than one, masculine — standing in front of clientes the way la stands in front of empresa and el stands in front of negocio. Spanish gives every noun a little flag like that — el, la, los, las — and for now your ear only needs to hear that the flag changes with the word it flies over. Los clientes. These are your people at work, Bill — the business owners who believed you could help them, who signed, who paid, who trust the company with their livelihoods. Say the word with the respect it deserves: Los clientes.

And what do you and your clientes do together? You work. So here is the word for the work itself. El trabajo — the work, the job. El trabajo. Say it: El trabajo. Trabajo is a word with sweat in it — in the old language underneath Spanish, its root carried the idea of effort, of something that costs you something. Spanish never forgot that. El trabajo is not a task on a list; it is the labor, the job, the whole working life in two beats: tra-BA-ho. Hear the j in the middle — in Spanish the letter j sounds like a strong h, a breath pushed out: trabajo, with that breath at the end. Say it again, with the breath: El trabajo. And the verb, the action itself: Trabajar — to work. Trabajar is to work. Say it: Trabajar. Tra-ba-HAR, the beat and the breath at the end. Trabajar. Hold that verb in your hand, Bill, because in a few minutes it is going to become the key that opens every working verb in this lesson — and I am going to show you the trick by feel, without a single grammar chart.

But first, the room where the work happens, and the tools on the desk, because a businessman should be able to name his own workplace. La oficina — the office. La oficina. Say it: La oficina. Oficina and office — cousins again, so close your ear gets this one free. El teléfono — the telephone, the phone. El teléfono. Say it: El teléfono. Teléfono carries its Greek roots on its face — tele, far, and fono, sound — the far-sound, the voice carried far. Every businessman alive lives on el teléfono. El correo — the mail, the email. El correo. Say it: El correo. Correo is mail — the letter kind and, in modern life, the email kind; el correo electrónico is the full name for email, but in daily talk, el correo does the work, and context tells everyone which mail you mean. La computadora — the computer. La computadora. Say it: La computadora. Computadora, the machine that computes — longer than the English word, more honest too; it names the machine by what it does. Say the desk set with me, one by one, like a man walking into his own office in the morning and touching each thing as he passes: La oficina. El teléfono. El correo. La computadora. Office, phone, mail, computer. Your workplace, in Spanish, named.

And the event that fills a businessman's calendar like weather: Una reunión — a meeting. Una reunión means a meeting — a gathering, a coming-together; reunión is built from unir, to unite, to bring together, with re- in front of it: a bringing-together-again. Una reunión. Say it: Una reunión. Every man who runs a company knows this word in his bones even before Spanish gives it to him: the meeting with the client, the meeting about the numbers, the meeting that should have been a phone call. And speaking of the phone — here is the sentence you will say at work more than almost any other, so I want it carved deep. Tengo una llamada — I have a call, I have a phone call. Tengo una llamada. Tengo you own from lesson three — tengo, I have, tengo dinero, I have money. Una llamada is a call — and look, Bill, look at that word: llamada. It is llamar, to call, wearing a noun's clothes. The call is the calling, turned into a thing. You have owned this root since your very first lesson — me llamo, I call myself — and it has been following you ever since: se llama, llamada. One root, a whole family of words. Tengo una llamada. Say it with me, like a man whose phone just lit up with a client's name: Tengo una llamada. Again: Tengo una llamada.

Let me walk you through one ordinary working morning with these words, so they stop being vocabulary and start being your day. You arrive at la oficina — the office — and the first thing waiting for you is el correo — the mail, the email — clients writing in the night, the way clients do. You read it on la computadora — the computer — coffee going cold the way coffee does. Then una reunión — a meeting — with a client who wants to talk about his negocio, his business, because that is what your empresa does: it helps other men's negocios grow. In the middle of the reunión, el teléfono — the phone — lights up: tengo una llamada, you say, I have a call, and you step out for two minutes like a gentleman. Every word in that morning, Bill, you now own in Spanish: la oficina, el correo, la computadora, una reunión, el teléfono, tengo una llamada. That is not a vocabulary list. That is Tuesday. And a man who can narrate his own Tuesday in a second language is a man whose Spanish has left the classroom and reported for work. Say the morning back to me, touching each thing as you pass it: La oficina. El correo. La computadora. Una reunión. El teléfono. Tengo una llamada. Good. That is your workplace, named and claimed.

Now we come to the engine room of this lesson — the part I have been promising you — the verbs of work, taught by feel. Here is the secret, and I am going to give it to you the way a good mechanic gives you a tool: by putting it in your hand, not by describing it. A great family of Spanish verbs ends in -ar in their full form: trabajar, hablar, llamar, comprar — to work, to speak, to call, to buy. And when you, the man himself, do the action — when I do it — Spanish changes the ending to -o. Watch it happen, and do not study it, feel it. Trabajar, to work, becomes: Trabajo — I work. Trabajo. Say it with me, and stand up a little straighter when you say it, because it is a working man's sentence in one word: Trabajo. It ends in o. I work. Trabajo.

Now the second one, and you already own its root without knowing it. Hablar — to speak, to talk. Hablar is to speak. And with the -o ending, the I form: Hablo — I speak, I talk. Hablo. Say it: Hablo. Hablo español — I speak Spanish. Hablo español. Say that whole sentence with me, Bill, because it is true now, a little more every week, and a man should be able to say what is true: Hablo español. Again: Hablo español. Ends in o again. Trabajo, hablo. I work, I speak. Feel the pattern in your mouth — the o at the end is your signature on the verb. It says: I am the one doing this.

And the third one is an old friend coming home. Llamar — to call. You have known llamar since lesson one, hiding inside me llamo. Its I form: Llamo — I call. Llamo. Say it: Llamo. And comprar, from lesson three, to buy — quiero comprar, I want to buy — its I form: Compro — I buy. Compro. Say it: Compro. Now line the four brothers up and look at them, Bill, because this is the whole trick and it is beautiful: Trabajo, hablo, llamo, compro — I work, I speak, I call, I buy. Four verbs, four different jobs, one ending. The -o ending. In Spanish, when a verb's full form ends in -ar, the I form ends in -o — and you do not need to recite that rule to anyone, ever. You need to feel it: the verb puts on the o, and the o means I. Say the four with me, slow, like a man counting his tools: Trabajo. Hablo. Llamo. Compro. Again, faster, at working speed: Trabajo, hablo, llamo, compro. That, mi estudiante, is the first real engine of Spanish grammar inside you — not memorized, felt. Every new -ar verb you ever meet, and there are hundreds, you can now pick up and use in the I form the day you meet it. That is not a lesson. That is a machine for making lessons unnecessary.

Let me check your feel for it, the way we check things in this room — gently, out loud, together. So if trabajar is to work, then I work is… trabajo. If hablar is to speak, then I speak is… hablo. If llamar is to call, I call is… llamo. Hold onto that o. It is yours. And now watch the engine pull a real train. Trabajo en mi empresa — I work in my company, I work at my company. En means in, at. Trabajo en mi empresa. Say it: Trabajo en mi empresa. Hablo con los clientes — I speak with the clients. Con means with. Hablo con los clientes. Say it: Hablo con los clientes. Two sentences, Bill, and you have just described your working day in Spanish: I work in my company. I speak with the clients. A man who can say those two sentences is not playing at business Spanish. He is doing it.

And now the verb that pays for everything — because a company that does not sell does not stay a company, and you, I happen to know, are a man who sells. Vender — to sell. Vender is to sell. Say it: Vender. Vender ends in -er, not -ar, so it belongs to the second family of verbs, and I am only introducing it today — its full pattern is a lesson for another day. But you need its I form now, because a businessman needs to say what he sells. Vendo — I sell. Vendo. Say it: Vendo. Hear the difference in the ending: the -ar family signs with -o after dropping -ar — trabajo — and vender signs vendo, I sell. Do not wrestle it today; just own the word. Vendo. And the question that follows a seller everywhere: ¿Qué vendes? — no. That one is for another lesson too, and your profesora does not teach ahead of the road. Today you own the statement: Vendo. I sell. One word, and a client on the other side of a desk knows exactly what kind of man he is talking to. Say it once more, with a seller's calm: Vendo.

One more turn of the engine before we assemble everything, because I want the -o feel deep in your hands, not just in your head. Run the four brothers through your real day, out loud with me, as full little sentences. In the morning, at the desk: Trabajo — I work — trabajo en mi empresa. A client walks in and the conversation starts: Hablo — I speak — hablo con los clientes. The phone rings and it is on you to make the call out: Llamo — I call. A purchase for the empresa, supplies, something the oficina needs: Compro — I buy. Trabajo, hablo, llamo, compro. Four verbs, four corners of a working day, one ending doing all the signing. And notice what you did not do, Bill: you did not open a book, you did not recite a chart, you did not conjugate anything. You felt the verb put on its o and step forward as you. That is how this room teaches grammar — by feel, in the mouth, on real sentences from your real life — and it is why the pattern will still be in your hands next month when a brand-new -ar verb walks into your life and you pick it up, turn it over, and use its I form without asking anyone's permission. That is fluency being born, mi estudiante, quietly, in an office, on a workday.

Now, before we assemble your whole working introduction, one small line about your own house — your company's house — because no lesson about your business would be honest without him, and I will keep it light, one line, the way you like it. Mi ayudante se llama Dave — my assistant is called Dave, my helper's name is Dave. Ayudante means assistant, helper — the one who helps. Mi ayudante se llama Dave. Say it with me: Mi ayudante se llama Dave. There it is — se llama again, the shape that names you, names Jillian, names your empresa, now naming the help. Dave is part of how your empresa runs, Bill, and now you can say so in Spanish, in one line, and move on like a man with work to do. Which you are. So let us move on.

Let us change the scenery now, because a phrase is only yours when it survives a new room — and today the new room is a room you know better than any classroom: a client's office, or better, the doorway of a small business in Michigan, a good business, the kind you help. A new client, a Spanish speaker, a man who built his own empresa the way you built yours, and today he is meeting you properly for the first time. You greet him — you own this: Buenos días — good morning. Mucho gusto — pleased to meet you. He asks, in Spanish, about your work, and Bill, you do not shrink and you do not wave for an interpreter. You stand in his doorway and you give him your working introduction, whole. Mi empresa se llama… — and your company's name rolls out. Trabajo en mi empresa — I work in my company. Hablo con los clientes — I speak with the clients. Vendo — I sell — and what you sell, you name with pride. His eyebrows go up, the way eyebrows do when a man hears his own language spoken back to him with respect by a stranger who took the trouble. The meeting — una reunión — has not even started, and already the trust in the room is different, because trust is built out of exactly this: trouble taken, in the other man's language, before any contract is mentioned. Same verbs as my classroom. New room. Still yours. That is what learned means, at work as much as at the wedding table.

Now the guided moment, the real examination — not the questions below, those are friendly — the real one: your whole working introduction, start to finish, at conversation speed, with you speaking into my silences. Ready, empresario? I am the new client, I have just walked into your oficina, and I am listening. Greet me and tell me your company's name-frame. … Mi empresa se llama. Good. Tell me where you work. … Trabajo en mi empresa. Tell me who you talk to all day. … Hablo con los clientes. Tell me the verb of your living. … Vendo. Now your phone lights up — a client, of course — tell me what you have. … Tengo una llamada. And the machine on your desk where the correo lives — name it. … La computadora. And your right hand, your helper — one line. … Mi ayudante se llama Dave. Bill. Stop. Look at what just happened. A client walked into your office and you named your company, stated your work, named your clients, claimed your selling, took your call, and introduced your help — in Spanish, unbroken, in your fourth lesson. In lesson one you were a man who could say hello. This morning you are a man who can open a business meeting. That is not a small step for an empresario. That is a new door in a building you already own.

A few hooks before we gather it all, so today's words stay yours. Empresa hangs on enterprise — say them side by side, empresa, enterprise, and hear the family resemblance; both words carry the undertaking, the thing taken on. Negocio hangs on negotiate — the dealing, the business in motion. Clientes hangs on clients — nearly free, that one. Oficina hangs on office — free again; Spanish has been generous to you today, Bill, and a wise man accepts generosity without suspicion. Teléfono hangs on telephone — the same Greek roots in both languages, the far-sound. Correo hangs on courier — the one who carries the mail; correo, courier, the carrier running under both words. Reunión hangs on reunion — a bringing together again; every reunión is a little reunion of people with work to do. And trabajo — hang that one on your own life. You do not need a word-hook for work. You have a life-hook. Every morning you have ever gotten up and built something is the hook. El trabajo. It was yours before Spanish named it.

So let us gather what you own now, in order, one last time. The house and the work inside it: la empresa — the company; el negocio — the business. Its name-frame: mi empresa se llama — my company is called. The people: los clientes — the clients. The work itself: el trabajo — the work; trabajar — to work. The workplace, named piece by piece: la oficina — the office; el teléfono — the phone; el correo — the mail, the email; la computadora — the computer; una reunión — a meeting. The working sentence: tengo una llamada — I have a call, built on llamada, from llamar, the root you have owned since lesson one. The engine, felt and not memorized: the -ar verbs sign their I form with -o — trabajo, I work; hablo, I speak; llamo, I call; compro, I buy — and hablo español, I speak Spanish, which is truer every week. The seller's word: vender, to sell; vendo, I sell. The full working sentences: trabajo en mi empresa, I work in my company; hablo con los clientes, I speak with the clients. And the one light line about your help: mi ayudante se llama Dave — my assistant is called Dave. Your name, your people, your numbers, and now your business, Bill. Four rooms of your life, standing in Spanish.

That is lesson four, and you did beautifully — do not argue with your profesora, I heard that introduction and it sounded like a man who signs his own checks. Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions: all of them about what I taught you in this lesson and nothing else — nobody will ask you one single thing I did not teach. Most give you choices to pick from, and you can also answer out loud or type your answer, whichever door you like. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and if you want to read a part again before you answer, do it with my blessing — that is what it is there for. And your speaking practice is here in the lesson too: hear me say each phrase, then say it back until it lands and you see the green check. Your assignment today is a working one, and it is simple: once today, at real work — at your desk, on a call, anywhere — say your introduction out loud to the empty room before the work starts. Mi empresa se llama. Trabajo en mi empresa. Hablo con los clientes. Vendo. Say it to the oficina like you are opening the doors for the day. A business that gets greeted in Spanish by its own owner is a business that is going somewhere new. Lesson five will build on this — more of your day, more of your life, in Spanish. Until then: gracias — thank you — for my fourth class, Bill. Now go run your empresa. Y esta noche — and tonight — you know the line for her: Buenas noches, mi amor — good night, my love. Work all day, mi estudiante. Love her all night. That is the whole curriculum.
