# Lesson 8 — Moving house: rooms, streets, and asking for what you need

Hola, Bill — hello, Bill. Soy Carmen — I am Carmen, your profesora, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, mi estudiante — my student — and take a breath, because today we do something very hopeful together. Today we learn how to talk about a house. Not a house in a textbook. Your house. The next house. The house you and Jillian are looking for, room by room, street by street, until one day you open a door and the two of you just know. By the end of our thirty minutes you will be able to name the rooms of a house, walk through a new place and ask where everything is, say whether a place is big or small or new, and — this is the part that changes everything — say clearly what you need and what you are looking for. As always in this room, we speak first. I say it, you say it back to me out loud, the way you mean it, and only then do we move on. When I say say it with me, I want to hear you, wherever you are. We are building a home in a new language, one word at a time, together.

Here is the map of the lesson, plainly, because I never hide the map from you. First, the house itself: la casa — the house. Then we walk through it the way your feet would go at a real viewing: la puerta — the door, la sala — the living room, la cocina — the kitchen, el baño — the bathroom, el cuarto — the bedroom, la habitación — the room, and la ventana — the window. Then we step outside to la calle — the street, because a house is also the street it stands on. Then the question that unlocks every viewing: ¿dónde está? — where is it? — and its two faithful answers: aquí — here, and allí — there. Then the describing words: grande — big, pequeño — small, and nuevo — new. And finally the two engines: necesito — I need, and busco — I am looking for, so you can stand in any house and say what you need like a man who knows his own mind. That is the whole lesson: a house, a walk, a question, and two engines. Hold onto that shape, and let us begin with the word the whole lesson stands on.

La casa — the house. Say it with me, out loud: La casa. Again, slower: la… casa. La casa. Casa is one of the warmest words in Spanish. It does not only mean a building with a roof. It means home — the place where your people are, where the food is, where the day ends. So when you learn la casa today, you are not learning a real estate word. You are learning the word for the life you and Jillian are building. Say it once more, like you mean that: La casa. Good. Now, houses come in sizes, and you need honest words for them, because house-hunting is no place for pretending. Grande — big, large. Say it with me: Grande. Una casa grande — a big house. Una — a, one, in the form it takes beside a word like casa. Una casa grande. And the opposite: Pequeño — small. Say it: Pequeño. Hear the letter ñ in the middle, the enye, with its little wave on top — it sounds like the ny in canyon, soft and quick: peh-KEH-nyoh. Pequeño. Una casa pequeña — a small house. Notice how the describing word dressed to match its partner, the way you learned in lesson one with buenos días — good morning — and buenas tardes — good afternoon. Casa takes the ending that ends in a, so pequeño becomes pequeña beside it, while grande is a generous word that wears the same coat for everybody. You do not need a grammar book for that tonight. You only need your ear. Say the pair with me, big and small: Una casa grande. Una casa pequeña. Una casa grande. Una casa pequeña. Beautiful. Your ear is doing grammar again, and we have barely opened the front door.

The third describing word is the one that matters most to a man who is moving: Nuevo — new. Say it with me: Nuevo. Una casa nueva — a new house. There is the matching coat again: nuevo beside a word like el cuarto, nueva beside casa. Una casa nueva. Now listen carefully, because there is a kindness hidden inside this word. Nuevo does not only mean newly built, fresh from the builder. It also means new to you. A house that is a hundred years old can be una casa nueva — a new house — for the family that just found it. You are not only looking for a new building, Bill. You are looking for a new beginning that happens to have walls. So if X, then Y — hold onto that: if the house is new to you and Jillian, then una casa nueva is an honest thing to call it, whatever year is carved over the door. Say the whole little family with me now: La casa. Una casa grande. Una casa pequeña. Una casa nueva. Good. You can already walk up to any house in the Spanish-speaking world and say what kind of house it is. Now let us do what you actually do at a viewing. Let us go inside.

Every viewing starts at the same place: La puerta — the door. Say it with me: La puerta. Again: La puerta. The door is the first promise a house makes you. A good door says you are welcome here, and also you are safe here, both at once. When you and Jillian stand in front of a house you might love, the first Spanish word out of your mouth can be this one: la puerta. Point at it, say it, smile at her. She will know you have been practicing, and she will love that you practiced on a door, because it means you were picturing the two of you arriving. Now we open it in our imagination and step into the first room of almost every house on earth: La sala — the living room. Say it with me: La sala. La sala is where life happens. It is where people sit and talk, where the family gathers, where you will one day fall asleep on a couch with the television still talking and Jillian will cover you with a blanket and pretend she did not. In many houses the sala is the biggest room: La sala es grande — the living room is big. Es — is. Es is the little bridge word that lets you describe anything. La sala es grande. And the other side: La sala es pequeña — the living room is small. Say both with me: La sala es grande. La sala es pequeña. Good. Hold onto es. La casa es nueva — the house is new. La sala es grande. One bridge, every description you will ever make, and we will cross it all lesson long.

From the sala, every house hunter on earth walks to the same place next, because the kitchen tells the truth about a house: La cocina — the kitchen. Say it with me, and say it with respect: La cocina. Again: La cocina. In Spanish, la cocina is not a utility room. It is the heart with appliances. It is where the coffee happens in the morning and where a man learns, over years, exactly how his wife takes her tea. When you walk into a viewing, walk into la cocina and ask yourself the only question that matters: can I see her here, in the morning light, half awake, laughing at something I said? If the answer is yes, the house is still in the running. If the answer is no, no sala in the world will save it. Say the room in full sentences now: La cocina es grande — the kitchen is big. La cocina es pequeña — the kitchen is small. Say them with me: La cocina es grande. La cocina es pequeña. And keep one question warm in your ear for a few more minutes: ¿dónde está la cocina? — where is the kitchen? When I teach it to you properly, you will already own every word in it except the asking itself. That is how this lesson is built, layer on layer, like a house.

Down the hall now — picture the hallway of this hopeful house we are inventing together — and here is the small room every guest asks about and every buyer checks twice: El baño — the bathroom. Say it with me: El baño. Hear the enye again, the same soft ny as in pequeño: BAH-nyoh. El baño. Baño comes from the word for a bath, and that is exactly what the room is for: washing, water, getting ready to face the world or to leave it behind for the night. Notice the little word in front changed its coat: la cocina, la sala, la puerta — and now el baño. El — the, in the form it takes beside a word like baño. Spanish nouns travel in two families, the la family and the el family, and you do not choose the family; the word brings its own. Baño is an el word. Cuarto, coming next, is an el word. Cocina, sala, puerta and casa are la words. Do not memorize a rule tonight. Just say each room with its own little word attached, the way I give it to you, and your ear will sort the families by itself. Say the set with me: La cocina. El baño. La cocina. El baño. Good. And in sentences: El baño es pequeño — the bathroom is small. There is pequeño in its first coat, beside an el word. El baño es grande — the bathroom is big. Say both: El baño es pequeño. El baño es grande. You are describing a house now, Bill, room by room, out loud, in Spanish. Feel how real that is.

Now the room where the day begins and ends, the room I saved for the middle of our walk because it deserves a slow moment: El cuarto — the bedroom, the room. Say it with me: El cuarto. Again: El cuarto. Cuarto has two jobs in Spanish, and I will teach you both honestly. Its first job is the one you need today: a room, and very often, in a house, the bedroom — the room where you sleep. It can also mean a fourth, a quarter — in an old house the rooms were counted off, and the sleeping room kept the counting word. For today, hold the house meaning tight. And Spanish gives you a second, longer word for the same place, and you should own them both, because agents and listings use them side by side: La habitación — the room, the bedroom. Say it with me, slowly first, it is a long, beautiful word: la ha-bi-ta-ción. La habitación. Again, whole: La habitación. Habitación comes from living, inhabiting — it is the room you inhabit, the room where your life actually sleeps. So here is your pair, and either one is correct in any house in any Spanish-speaking town: el cuarto, la habitación. Say the pair with me: El cuarto. La habitación. El cuarto. La habitación. In sentences: El cuarto es grande — the bedroom is big. La habitación es pequeña — the bedroom is small. Say both: El cuarto es grande. La habitación es pequeña. And picture it as you say it — not a showroom bed with forty pillows. Your bed. Her side and your side, already decided without a word, the way couples decide it. A word tied to a picture like that does not get forgotten. Your memory keeps what your heart files.

Speaking of her side of the bed, it has morning light coming through the last stop on our walk: La ventana — the window. Say it with me: La ventana. Again: La ventana. A ventana is where the street says good morning, where you check the weather with your own eyes, where Jillian will stand one day with coffee, looking out at a yard that is hers. Ventanas take your whole toolkit: La ventana es grande — the window is big. La ventana es pequeña — the window is small. Say them: La ventana es grande. La ventana es pequeña. Now let me give you a small, true scene to carry these rooms in, because a phrase is only yours when it survives a new room — you know that is my law. Picture the viewing. La agente — the agent, a woman showing you the house — opens la puerta and waves you in. You step into la sala, and it is bigger than the photos promised, and you say it out loud: La sala es grande. The agent smiles, because buyers who can talk are buyers who are serious. Jillian drifts, the way she will, straight to la cocina, and you follow her and say the honest sentence: La cocina es pequeña, pero bonita — the kitchen is small, but pretty. Pero — but. Bonita — pretty, beautiful, in the form beside a la word. Pero is the most honest word in house-hunting, Bill: every house has a pero, and the art is finding the house whose pero you can live with. Say it with me: La cocina es pequeña, pero bonita. Down the hall: El baño es nuevo — the bathroom is new. Nuevo in its first coat beside an el word; nueva beside la casa — you own both coats now. Then el cuarto es grande — the bedroom is big — and la ventana es grande — the window is big — and the morning light does the rest of the selling, the way morning light always does. There it is. You have walked a whole house, named every room and described every room, in Spanish, in sentences, out loud. That is not studying. That is viewing a house like a man who lives in the language.

Now we step outside, because a house is not only its rooms, and you know this in your bones from every place you have ever lived: La calle — the street. Say it with me: La calle. Again, and hear the double l doing its soft y work, the sound you tamed in lesson one inside llamo: la calle sounds like KAH-yeh. La calle. The street is where the house meets the world. When you hunt for a house with Jillian, you are hunting for a calle too: Una calle tranquila — a quiet street. Tranquila — quiet, calm, peaceful. Una calle tranquila. Say it: Una calle tranquila. And a house stands on its street with the little word en — in, on, at: La casa está en la calle — the house is on the street. Stop there with me, because a new giant just walked in and I want you to meet him properly. Está — is, is located, is right now. You already own es from our descriptions: la casa es grande — the house is big — that is what the house is like, its nature. Está is its brother, and está answers a different question: where is it, and how is it right now? Es tells you what a thing is. Está tells you where a thing is. La casa es grande, its nature. La casa está en la calle, its place. So if X, then Y — hold onto that with both hands: if you are describing what something is like, reach for es; if you are saying where something is, reach for está. That one distinction, learned today in a doorway, will carry you through years of Spanish. Say the pair with me, nature and place: La casa es grande. La casa está en la calle. La casa es grande. La casa está en la calle. Perfect. You just learned one of the great truths of this language standing on an imaginary sidewalk, in a sentence about a house you want.

And now, with está in your hand, I can finally give you the question I promised you in la cocina, the question that unlocks every viewing and every new town for the rest of your life: ¿Dónde está? — where is it? Say it with me, and notice the music: ¿Dónde está? Dónde — where. Está — is, is located. ¿Dónde está? Look at the marks around it, because Spanish dresses its questions properly: an upside-down question mark at the start, so your eye knows a question is coming before it arrives, and the ordinary one at the end. And hear the accent on dónde — that little leaning line tells your voice where to lean, the way I taught you with días — days — and corazón — heart: DOHN-deh, lean on the first beat. Say it again, leaning right: ¿Dónde está? Now put a room on the end of it and it becomes the most useful sentence in house-hunting: ¿Dónde está la cocina? — where is the kitchen? Say it with me: ¿Dónde está la cocina? ¿Dónde está el baño? — where is the bathroom? — the question every human asks in every new house, and now you ask it in Spanish. ¿Dónde está el cuarto? — where is the bedroom? ¿Dónde está la puerta? — where is the door? — the question you ask, politely, with a smile, when the visit is over and the house was not the one. Say the whole set with me, like a man walking a viewing with purpose: ¿Dónde está la cocina? ¿Dónde está el baño? ¿Dónde está el cuarto? ¿Dónde está la puerta? Ay, Bill, listen to yourself. Four questions, every room in the house, and not one of them in English. And the answers come back in the two smallest, hardest-working words in this lesson: Aquí — here. Say it: Aquí. Lean where the accent leans, on the end: ah-KEE. Aquí. And Allí — there. Say it: Allí. Also leaning on the end: ah-YEE, with that double l singing its soft y again. Allí. Aquí, allí. Here, there. The agent answers you: La cocina está aquí — the kitchen is here. El baño está allí — the bathroom is there. Say the answers with me, full sentences, pointing with your voice: La cocina está aquí. El baño está allí. La puerta está aquí. La ventana está allí. Good. Question and answer, a complete little machine: you ask ¿dónde está?, the world answers aquí or allí, and you are never lost in a house again. Never lost — in a new house, in a new town, in a new language. That is what we are really building here.

Now the two engines, the part of the lesson that turns a tourist into a buyer. First: Necesito — I need. Say it with me, strong, like a man stating a fact and not begging a favor: Necesito. Again: Necesito. Four clean beats, and the weight lands in the middle: neh-ceh-SEE-toh. Necesito is a complete engine because Spanish hides the I inside the verb itself — necesito already contains yo — I — inside it, so you never need a separate word for I with it. Necesito, full stop, is a whole sentence: I need. Put a house part behind it and it pulls real weight: Necesito una cocina grande — I need a big kitchen. Say it with me: Necesito una cocina grande. Necesito un cuarto grande — I need a big bedroom. Un — a, one, in the form beside an el word, the brother of una. Necesito un cuarto grande. Say it: Necesito un cuarto grande. Necesito una casa nueva — I need a new house. Say it: Necesito una casa nueva. Hear what you are doing: necesito plus a thing plus a description, and you can state any need a house can hold. Necesito una ventana grande — I need a big window — for her morning light. Necesito una calle tranquila — I need a quiet street — for your sleep and your peace. Say those two with me, the tender needs, the real ones: Necesito una ventana grande. Necesito una calle tranquila. A man who can say what he needs, plainly, without apology and without anger, is a man people help gladly. In English you are already that man. Today you became him in Spanish too.

And the second engine, necesito's brother, the hunter's word: Busco — I am looking for, I look for. Say it with me: Busco. Again: Busco. Two beats, strong as a bootstep: BOOS-koh. Busco comes from buscar — to look for, to search — and like necesito, it carries its I inside it: busco is a whole sentence all by itself. What is the difference between my two engines? Listen carefully, because people hear the difference. Necesito states a need — it is firm, it is about what must be true. Busco starts a search — it is open, it is about what you are hunting, and it invites the other person to hunt with you. An agent hears busco and reaches for her keys. A neighbor hears busco and starts thinking of the houses on his street. Lead with busco when you are opening a conversation; lead with necesito when you are drawing a line that matters. Now put it to work: Busco una casa — I am looking for a house. Say it with me, like you are saying it to a real agent on a real morning: Busco una casa. Busco una casa grande — I am looking for a big house. Say both: Busco una casa. Busco una casa grande. Busco una casa nueva — I am looking for a new house. Say it: Busco una casa nueva. And the full dream, the whole sentence, the one I want you to be able to say to any agent, any neighbor, any new friend, without notes and without fear: Busco una casa grande en una calle tranquila — I am looking for a big house on a quiet street. Take it in pieces first: Busco una casa grande… en una calle tranquila. Now whole, at the speed of a man who knows what he wants: Busco una casa grande en una calle tranquila. Again: Busco una casa grande en una calle tranquila. Bill, that sentence, said clearly in a stranger's office, starts houses appearing. Say it one more time and picture her standing beside you when you say it, because in the real version, she is: Busco una casa grande en una calle tranquila.

Time to do it, not just hear it — you know my rule: the doing is taught here, inside the lesson, with me beside you. This is your guided try-it, and we will run a whole viewing together, start to finish. I will be la agente, and you will be you. I open la puerta and wave you in, and first thing, polite as you were raised, you greet the room the way I taught you in lesson one: buenas tardes — good afternoon. Then you look around la sala and you tell me what you see, in a full sentence: La sala es grande. I nod — agents always nod — and you ask your first question, the unlocker: ¿Dónde está la cocina? And I point: La cocina está aquí. You walk in, you look at her face more than the counters — you are learning, Bill — and you say the honest sentence: La cocina es pequeña, pero bonita. Down the hall you go, and you ask again, all by yourself this time: ¿Dónde está el baño? Allí, I say — el baño está allí. You check it: El baño es nuevo. Good news, stated plainly. Then el cuarto, the room that matters most, and you stand in its doorway a moment longer than the others, and you say: El cuarto es grande. And la ventana es grande, and the morning light does the rest of the selling. Then, out on la calle, in the sun, I ask you the question agents ask, and you do not shrug and you do not wave vaguely at the sky. You use your engines. You say what you are hunting: Busco una casa grande en una calle tranquila. And you say the need that is not negotiable, the one you and Jillian agreed on in the car: Necesito una cocina grande. Now run that last part again in your own voice, both engines, out loud, right now: Busco una casa grande en una calle tranquila. Necesito una cocina grande. There it is. That was a viewing — a whole viewing, in Spanish, with you doing the talking. If you can do it in this room with me narrating, you can do it on a Saturday with her hand in yours, and Saturday is coming. And one day, after enough calles and enough puertas and enough ¿dónde está?, the answer will be aquí. Here. This one. Ours. The Spanish for that day is already in your mouth, and we put it there today, word by word, room by room.

So let us gather what you own now, in order, one last time, the whole walk in one breath. La casa — the house, the home, the whole dream in two syllables. The descriptions that ride on it: grande — big, pequeño — small, nuevo — new, and es — is — the little bridge: la casa es grande, la casa es nueva. The walk itself: la puerta — the door, la sala — the living room, la cocina — the kitchen, el baño — the bathroom, el cuarto — the bedroom, la habitación — the room, la ventana — the window, and outside, la calle — the street. The locator brothers: es for what a thing is like, está for where a thing is. The great question: ¿dónde está? — where is it? — with its two faithful answers, aquí — here — and allí — there. And the two engines that make you a buyer and not a bystander: necesito — I need — and busco — I am looking for — standing tall in their full sentences: Busco una casa grande en una calle tranquila. Necesito una cocina grande. And hang them on a hook you already own: necesito lives inside necessary — when you say necesito, you are saying this is necessary to me. That is lesson eight, Bill, and you did not just learn it — you walked it, room by room, out loud. Ay, do not argue with your profesora. I heard every word.

Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions: more than a few, all of them about what I taught you in this lesson and nothing else — nobody will ask you one single thing I did not teach. Most of them give you choices to pick from, and you can also answer out loud or type your answer, whichever door you like — and today you know the Spanish for door, so choose a good one. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and if you want to read any part of it again before you answer, do it with my blessing. Going back to read is learning, not cheating; in this room it has always been learning. Your speaking practice lives in this lesson too: hear me say each phrase, then say it back until it lands. And your real assignment, the one that is not on any screen: the next time you and Jillian talk about houses — and that talk is coming, mi estudiante, sooner than you think — say one line of it in Spanish. Busco una casa grande en una calle tranquila. Say it to her in the car. Watch her face. That face is why we do this. Next lesson, we learn to ask anything — who, what, where, when, how much — every question word in Spanish, as one system, so no door of curiosity stays closed to you. Until then, la puerta of this room is always open to you. Ve — go — and go look at a house with your prometida — your fiancée. She is going to love hunting in two languages.
