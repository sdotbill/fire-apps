# Lesson 6 — Time and days: schedules, appointments, and mañana

Hola, Bill — hello, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, mi estudiante — my student — and look at the clock with me, because today we learn time itself. This is lesson six: time and days — schedules, appointments, and the sweetest word in the Spanish language, mañana — tomorrow. By the end of this half hour you will be able to say what day it is, ask what time it is, say when your appointment is, tell whether you are early or late, and — because this course always has a second mission running under the first — you will own the sweet everyday words for the door of your own house: the good morning, the good day wish, and the I love you that Jillian hears before the day takes her.

Here is why time comes now, at lesson six, and not later. A language without time in it can only talk about this exact second. You can greet, you can eat — lesson five fed you well — but you cannot plan, and your life, Bill, is a plan with a date on it. March the twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-seven, Key West: la boda — the wedding — and I am using that word early today on purpose, because next lesson is built on it. Between now and that date there are appointments, arrivals, dinners, a schedule with her name and yours all over it. A man who can handle days and hours in Spanish can handle a wedding week in Spanish. That is what we are building today: your calendar, in her favorite language.

Same method as always, because it has carried you five lessons without failing: I say it, you say it back to me out loud, the way you mean it, and only then do we move on. When I say say it with me, I want to hear you. And when the lesson ends, the questions wait below — all about what I teach here, nothing else — and the full text stays open beside you while you answer. Reading again is learning, not cheating. You know this. Now, we start where every schedule starts: with the three days you actually live in.

First day, the one you are standing in: Hoy — today. Hoy means today. It is a small word with that silent h doing its invisible work again — hoy sounds like oy, the h written and never heard, exactly as lesson one promised you. Hoy. Say it with me: Hoy. Everything you do before midnight tonight happens hoy. This lesson is happening hoy. Say it once more, and point at the floor under your feet, because that is where today lives: Hoy.

Second day, the famous one, the word this lesson is named for: Mañana — tomorrow. Mañana means tomorrow. And mañana has a secret I want you to hold gently: the same word, mañana, also means morning — la mañana — the morning. Spanish uses one word for tomorrow and for morning, and once you hear why, you will never forget it: tomorrow is just the next morning, arriving. When a Spanish speaker says mañana, they are picturing the sun coming up on the next day. It is the most hopeful word in the language — nothing is ever finished, there is always a mañana. Say it with me, slowly, it is a beautiful word: Mañana. Again: Mañana. And hear the ñ in it — that n with the little wave on top, which sounds like the ny in canyon. Mah-NYAH-nah. Mañana.

Third day, and I will teach this one lightly, the way I promised — just enough to recognize it and use it simply: Ayer — yesterday. Ayer means yesterday. Ayer sounds like ah-YEHR. Say it with me: Ayer. Three days, three words, and your whole life fits inside them: Ayer, hoy, mañana. Yesterday, today, tomorrow. What happened ayer is done. What you do hoy is yours. What you promise for mañana is hope with a date on it. Say the three in order, like a man walking across his own week: Ayer. Hoy. Mañana. Again: Ayer, hoy, mañana.

Now watch how these three words plug into sentences you already own, because Spanish rewards a man who combines. Hoy tengo hambre — today I am hungry — and there is your lesson-five tengo hambre — I am hungry — wearing a day on its front. Days ride at the front of Spanish sentences like a name tag on the day itself: hoy, this; mañana, that. Mañana tengo una cita — tomorrow I have an appointment — and I just spent a phrase we have not built yet, una cita — an appointment — so hold it in your hand for a few minutes; we are coming to it properly, and it is one of today's headline words. First, the week itself, because appointments live in weeks.

La semana — the week. Semana means week. La semana. Say it with me: La semana. A week is seven days standing in a row, and Spanish lines them up starting on Monday — the Spanish week begins with work, and rests at the end, which any working man will tell you is the honest order. Here come los días de la semana — the days of the week — and días you already own from buenos días — good morning — back in lesson one: días means days. Los días de la semana: the days of the week. We will take them one at a time, in order, and I will give each one a small hook, because seven new words in a row is a parade and I want you to remember every soldier in it.

Lunes — Monday. Lunes is the day of la luna — the moon — and luna is right there inside the English word lunar; Monday in Spanish is moonday, plain as the sky. Lunes. Say it: Lunes. Martes — Tuesday. Martes carries Mars, the old war god, inside it — Tuesday was his day; you can hear Mars clearing his throat inside martes. Martes. Say it: Martes. Miércoles — Wednesday. Miércoles is the long one, so walk it in pieces: mee-EHR-koh-les. Miércoles carries Mercury, the fast one, the messenger — a busy middle-of-the-week day with a busy name. Say it in pieces with me: Miér… coles. Miércoles. Again, whole: Miércoles.

Jueves — Thursday. Jueves carries Jupiter, the king — the biggest planet for the day that is almost, almost the weekend. Jueves sounds like HWEH-ves, and yes, that j at the front is a strong breathy h sound in Spanish, nothing like the English j — one more honest sound for your collection. Say it: Jueves. Viernes — Friday. Viernes carries Venus, the goddess of love — and Bill, I have always loved that Spanish puts love's day at the end of the work week, as if the week itself is walking toward her. Viernes. Say it with a smile: Viernes.

And the weekend, the two days the whole week leans toward. Sábado — Saturday. Sábado is the sabbath day, the rest day — you can hear sabbath inside sábado if you listen with your heart. Sábado. Say it: Sábado. And domingo — Sunday. Domingo is the Lord's day, from the old word for the Lord — the quiet day, the family day, the day a man in Michigan might drive to see his dad or sit on a porch doing absolutely nothing, on purpose. Domingo. Say it: Domingo.

Now the whole parade, in order, and I want you to say them with me twice — once slow, once at the speed of a man reading his own calendar. Slow first: Lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes, sábado, domingo. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday. Again, at calendar speed: Lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes, sábado, domingo. Good. And notice what your ear just learned: five of the seven end in that same -es drumbeat — lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes — and the weekend sings a different tune, sábado, domingo. That rhythm is a memory hook all by itself. The week marches, then the week rests.

How do you use a day once you own it? The first use is simply naming today, and Spanish keeps it simple: Hoy es lunes — today is Monday — hoy, today; es, is. Hoy es lunes. Change the day and the sentence follows like a dog: Hoy es viernes — today is Friday. Say both with me: Hoy es lunes. Hoy es viernes. And the second use is planning, where mañana does the heavy lifting: Mañana es sábado — tomorrow is Saturday. Say it: Mañana es sábado. A man who can say what today is and what tomorrow is can hold a whole conversation about a schedule standing on those two sentences alone, and I have heard men do exactly that at hotel desks all over the Spanish-speaking world.

Now, inside each day live the hours, and here is the question that unlocks them — the question you will ask in train stations, hotel lobbies, and your own kitchen: ¿Qué hora es? — what time is it? Word for word: what hour is it. Qué means what. Hora means hour — and hora is a cousin of the English word hour itself, same old root, and it hides another silent h: hora sounds like OH-rah. ¿Qué hora es? Say it with me: ¿Qué hora es? Again, like a man checking whether he is late for the best appointment of his life: ¿Qué hora es?

The answer has a shape, and the shape changes exactly once, at one o'clock, and Spanish is very proud of the tidiness of it. For one o'clock, you say: Es la una — it is one o'clock. Es, it is. La una, the one. Una is one, in the form it takes beside hora — because hora belongs to the la team, the feminine team, and una dresses to match it, the same matching you saw in lesson one with buenos and buenas. Es la una. Say it with me: Es la una. Only one o'clock gets es la — because there is only one of it. One hour, singular, es la una. Hold that: the one is special.

Every other hour stands in a crowd, so the verb grows to match: Son las dos — it is two o'clock. Son means they are, it is, for the plural hours. Las dos — the two. Dos means two. Son las dos. Say it: Son las dos. And the machine just keeps building: Son las tres — it is three o'clock — tres means three. Son las cuatro — it is four o'clock — cuatro means four. Son las cinco — it is five o'clock — cinco means five. Hear the frame holding steady: son las, and then the number rides in the last seat. Son las dos, son las tres, son las cuatro, son las cinco. Say the run with me, up the clock: Son las dos. Son las tres. Son las cuatro. Son las cinco. And step back down to the special one, so your ear owns the difference: Es la una. Son las dos. One is es la; everything after is son las. That is the whole law of the Spanish clock, and you just learned it in one breath.

Let me check your understanding the way I always do, by making you drive. I ask: ¿Qué hora es? — what time is it? — and the clock on the wall says two. You answer: Son las dos. The clock says one — careful now, the special one: Es la una. The clock says five: Son las cinco. Good. Your mouth hesitated at la una and then got it — that hesitation is the sound of a new rule settling in, and it settles faster every time you use it. One more round, quick: three o'clock — Son las tres. Four o'clock — Son las cuatro. One o'clock — Es la una. Perfecto — perfect — and that word you may have just because you earned it.

Now, what are hours for? In a grown man's life, hours are for appointments — the moments where a day and an hour shake hands. Tengo una cita — I have an appointment. Una cita means an appointment — una, an; cita, appointment. And listen to tengo doing its second job of your course: in lesson five, tengo hambre meant I have hunger; here, tengo una cita means I have an appointment. Same tengo — I have — carrying a new load. That is the compound interest of this language, Bill: every strong word you learn keeps paying you in new sentences. Say it with me: Tengo una cita. Again, like a man whose calendar matters: Tengo una cita.

And una cita has a sweet second meaning I will give you honestly, because you will smile when you hear it: cita is also the word for a date — the romantic kind. Una cita can be the dentist, and una cita can be dinner with Jillian. Spanish uses one word for both, and context — and your face — tells everyone which kind you mean. When you say tengo una cita about an evening in Key West, nobody in that restaurant is picturing a dentist. Context is king. Say it once with the appointment meaning, businesslike: Tengo una cita. And once with the other meaning, the Key West meaning, slower: Tengo una cita. Hear that? Same words. Different evening entirely.

Now build a real schedule sentence, the kind a wedding week runs on, stacking today's bricks in order: day, then the cita, then the hour. Mañana tengo una cita — tomorrow I have an appointment. Add the hour with a — at — the little word a means at when it stands before a time: a las tres — at three o'clock. Mañana tengo una cita a las tres — tomorrow I have an appointment at three o'clock. Say it with me in pieces: Mañana… tengo una cita… a las tres. Whole now: Mañana tengo una cita a las tres. Again: Mañana tengo una cita a las tres. Bill, that is a sentence a man's whole week can hang on. And it is wedding-week true, by the way — there will be a rehearsal, a license errand, a dinner reservation, a hundred citas, and you can now name every one of them in Spanish.

Two words remain in the time toolkit, and they are the two that decide whether a man is calm or running: Temprano — early. Temprano means early. Tem-PRAH-no. Say it: Temprano. And its opposite: Tarde — late. Tarde means late — and tarde is a double agent you have actually met before: buenas tardes — good afternoon — from lesson one, because tarde also means afternoon, the late part of the day. Same word, two jobs, and the connection is honest: the afternoon is the day's late part, and arriving tarde is arriving in the day's late part of where you were supposed to be. You will never confuse them in real life; the sentence around the word tells you which job it is doing. Say the pair: Temprano. Tarde. Early. Late. Temprano, tarde.

Use them where they live. A man checks his watch outside an office and says, with relief: Es temprano — it is early — es, it is; temprano, early. Or the other truth, said at a run: Es tarde — it is late. And for the wedding week, here is the sentence I actually want in your pocket, because grooms earn it: the morning of la boda, somebody will ask if there is time, and you, calm as a lake, will answer with the clock and the calm together. But that scene belongs to next lesson. Today, just own the pair cold: temprano, early; tarde, late. Say them once more, and put the feeling in each: Temprano — relaxed. Tarde — hurrying. There. Your body knows them now too.

Time to change rooms — from the calendar to the front door of your own house — because here is where the Spanish-for-Jillian strand of this lesson lives, and it is the part of today you will use first, tomorrow morning, before any appointment on any calendar. The scene: early morning, your kitchen, Jillian heading out to the hospital or just starting her day, and you are the man at the door. Spanish has a whole little ceremony for this, and you already own its first line from lesson one. Buenos días, mi amor — good morning, my love — buenos días, good morning; mi amor, my love. Say it with me, at kitchen-in-the-morning volume: Buenos días, mi amor. Again, like you have been waiting to say it: Buenos días, mi amor.

Second line of the ceremony, a wish you send out the door with her: Que tengas un buen día — have a good day. Word for word, it is a wish: may you have a good day. Que tengas — may you have. Un buen día — a good day — and there is día, day, from lesson one, and buen, good, the short strong form of the word you met as buenos. Que tengas un buen día. It is the longest sentence in today's strand, so take it in pieces: Que tengas… un buen día. Whole: Que tengas un buen día. Say it again, to the picture of her with her bag on her shoulder: Que tengas un buen día. That sentence, Bill, is a man blessing his woman's day before she walks into a hard one — and nurses' days are hard ones. She will carry it into that hospital like a note in her pocket.

Third line, the one you already own and love, now placed exactly where it lives in a real day — at the door, as she leaves: Te quiero — I love you — the everyday I love you, the one from lesson one, the one that lives in the house. Te quiero at the door is not a small thing. Te quiero at the door, said every morning, is a marriage being built one ordinary day at a time, and the wedding in March is just the day everybody else finds out. Say it the way it will actually be said, quick and warm and certain, because she is already halfway to the car: Te quiero. Again: Te quiero. And if she is mi amor in the morning greeting, she can be the full sentence too: Te quiero, mi amor — I love you, my love. Say it: Te quiero, mi amor.

And when she comes home, or when you are the one leaving and the goodbye is only for a few hours, Spanish keeps the door gentle: Hasta luego — see you later. Hasta means until. Luego means later, soon. Hasta luego: until later. It is the softest goodbye in the language — not an ending, a pause. Nobody says goodbye forever at a kitchen door at seven in the morning; you say hasta luego, because you will see her at dinner and you both know it. Say it with me: Hasta luego. Again, with a hand raised at the door: Hasta luego.

And the last sweet word of the strand, for the end of the day or the quiet moment in it: Mi corazón — my heart — from lesson one, the word that means it literally, the organ in your chest. Buenos días, mi corazón — good morning, my heart — works in the morning. Te quiero, mi corazón — I love you, my heart — works at the door. The strand gives you two names for her, mi amor and mi corazón, my love and my heart, and a man rotates them the way the day rotates: amor for the morning, corazón for the night, or the other way around — she will not be grading the rotation, Bill. She will be hearing that her name, in your mouth, in Spanish, is love and heart and nothing else. Say both names now, one after the other: Mi amor. Mi corazón.

Now let us run the whole door ceremony, start to finish, the way tomorrow morning can actually go, and I will narrate while you speak your lines out loud. She comes into the kitchen, bag in hand, keys in hand, the day already pulling at her. You are ready. Line one: Buenos días, mi amor — good morning, my love. She smiles — she will smile. Line two, the blessing for her day: Que tengas un buen día — have a good day. Line three, at the door itself, the one that lives in the house: Te quiero — I love you. And as the door opens and the morning takes her, the soft goodbye: Hasta luego — see you later. Four lines. Buenos días, mi amor. Que tengas un buen día. Te quiero. Hasta luego. Run it once more, all four, in your own voice, no narration from me. … That, Bill, is a morning. Not a lesson exercise — a morning, in your house, starting mañana, if you want it. Mañana. Tomorrow. See how the word keeps its promise?

Now your guided try-it, the schedule round, because I am your profesora and the calendar must be driven, not just admired. I am the front desk of a hotel in Key West, wedding week, and you are the groom with arrangements to make. I ask you: ¿Qué día es hoy? — what day is it today? — qué, what; día, day; hoy, today — a new question, and you already own its answer's shape. The calendar says Wednesday. Answer me: Hoy es miércoles — today is Wednesday. Say it: Hoy es miércoles. Good. Now you have an errand — the marriage license office, let us say, because grooms have errands — and you tell me when: Mañana tengo una cita — tomorrow I have an appointment. Say it: Mañana tengo una cita. At what hour? The office said three. Add it: A las tres — at three o'clock. Whole sentence now, groom, all of it: Mañana tengo una cita a las tres. And I, the desk, check the clock and ask: ¿Qué hora es? It says one. Careful — the special one. Answer: Es la una. And because it is one and your cita is tomorrow, you are not late, you are the other word: Temprano — early. Say the verdict with the relief it deserves: Temprano. Scene complete. You just ran a hotel desk in Spanish, and every arrangement survived.

One more drive around the block, faster, mixing the calendar and the door, because real days mix them. It is Friday morning — hoy es viernes — today is Friday. She is leaving for the hospital. The door ceremony, quick and warm: Buenos días, mi amor. Que tengas un buen día. Te quiero. Hasta luego. The day turns. Your phone buzzes: a cita, hoy, a las cinco — today, at five o'clock — hoy, today; a las cinco, at five. You check the time: ¿Qué hora es? — son las dos — it is two o'clock — so you have hours, you are temprano, early, a man ahead of his own day. Evening comes, she is home, and the day closes the way lesson one taught you it could. That is a whole day run in Spanish, Bill — calendar, clock, appointment, and heart — and you drove every mile of it.

Hooks, so today sticks. Mañana and morning are one word because tomorrow is the next morning — you will never lose that one. Hora is hour with its h asleep — the same root, the same word, wearing Spanish clothes. Cita — appointment — sounds a little like city, and a man's citas do tend to happen where the buildings are; better hook: a cita is a citation to appear — the court cites you, the calendar cites you, and the romantic cita is the one appointment you are happy to be summoned to. Temprano — early — I remember it as the temper of a calm man: the temprano man is never in a temper, because he is never late. And tarde you already own twice — afternoon and late — the day's late part, both times. Hang the words on what you already know, and they stay furniture, not homework.

Let us gather the lesson in order, one last time. Hoy — today. Mañana — tomorrow, and also morning, the next morning arriving. Ayer — yesterday, lightly held. La semana — the week, and los días de la semana — the days of the week: lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes, sábado, domingo — Monday through Sunday. Hoy es lunes — today is Monday — the shape for naming any day. ¿Qué hora es? — what time is it? Es la una — it is one o'clock, the special one. Son las dos, son las tres, son las cuatro, son las cinco — it is two, three, four, five o'clock, the crowd pattern. Tengo una cita — I have an appointment, and maybe, in Key West, a date. Temprano — early. Tarde — late. And the door strand, the heart of the day: Buenos días, mi amor — good morning, my love. Que tengas un buen día — have a good day. Te quiero — I love you, at the door. Hasta luego — see you later. Mi corazón — my heart.

That is lesson six, and you did it the way you do everything that matters, Bill — all the way through. Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions, all of them about what I taught you here: the three days, the week, the clock and its special one o'clock, the cita, early and late, and the words for the door. Nothing I did not teach. Most give you choices; answer out loud or type, whichever door you like. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and reading any of it again is learning, not cheating — that is what it is there for. Your speaking practice is here too: hear each phrase, say it back, until the green check lands. And your real-world assignment is the door ceremony, tomorrow morning, all four lines, to her, at the actual door. Mañana, Bill. The word is a promise, and so is the assignment. Lesson seven is the big one — la boda, the wedding itself, in Key West, and the deepest words I will ever teach you. Sleep well tonight. Hasta luego, mi estudiante. Mañana we marry off your Spanish.
