# Lesson 10 — The past: telling someone what you did yesterday

Hola, Bill — hello, Bill. Soy Carmen — I am Carmen, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, mi estudiante — my student — because tonight we do something quietly miraculous. Tonight we learn to talk about yesterday. Think about what most of a real conversation actually is, Bill. It is not describing houses, and it is not even asking questions, though last lesson's questions are the keys that start it. Most of a real conversation — most of a marriage's conversation, at the table, at the door, at the end of the day — is this: telling someone what happened. What you did. Who you called. Where you went. How it went. Until today, your Spanish lived in the present tense, in the now. Today it grows a past. By the end of our thirty minutes you will be able to tell Jillian what you did yesterday — trabajé — I worked — llamé — I called — comí — I ate — dormí — I slept — in true, complete Spanish sentences, in the tense Spanish uses for a finished day. This tense has a name, and I will give it to you once, honestly, and then we will mostly ignore the name and learn the thing by feel, the way you have learned everything in this room: El pretérito — the preterite, the past tense for things that happened and finished. Say the idea, not the name: it happened, it is done, and now I am telling it. That is the pretérito. And as always, we speak first: I say it, you say it back to me out loud, the way you mean it, and only then do we move on. When I say say it with me, I want to hear you. Your voice is the instrument. Warm it up.

First, the signposts, because before you can tell a story about the past, you need the words that plant the story in the past — the little flags that tell your listener: we are not in today anymore. There are three, and they will carry almost every story you ever tell. Ayer — yesterday. Say it with me: Ayer. Ah-YEHR, two beats, and hear the soft y doing its work again. Ayer. Ayer is the flag for the whole day that just finished: ayer trabajé — yesterday I worked — and we will build that sentence together very soon. Second flag: Anoche — last night. Say it: Anoche. Ah-NOH-cheh. Anoche is ayer after dark: the night that just passed, the evening behind you. Anoche dormí — last night I slept — another sentence we will build today, word by word, until it is yours. And the third flag, the wide one: La semana pasada — last week. Semana — week. Pasada — past, gone by. La semana pasada: the week that has passed. Say it with me: La semana pasada. Three flags, three sizes of past: anoche for the night, ayer for the day, la semana pasada for the week. Plant one of these flags at the front of a sentence and your listener's mind walks back in time with you, ready for the story. Say the three flags in order, night, day, week: Anoche. Ayer. La semana pasada. Again: Anoche. Ayer. La semana pasada. Good. The flags are planted. Now we need the verbs that can live under them — and verbs, Bill, change their coats when they step into the past. That is the whole secret, and it is smaller than you fear.

Here is the idea, taught by feel, the Carmen way, and I want you to hold it loosely and trust your ear. In Spanish, when an action is finished — done, completed, in the past — the verb puts on its past coat, and for the verbs that end in ar, the coat for I, for yo — I — is a strong, clean ending: é — the sound EH with the accent leaning on it, the beat landing at the end of the word like a foot coming down at the end of a walk. Watch it happen to a verb you already own. Hablar — to speak, to talk. You met its family in lesson one inside llamo — I call myself — llamar — to call — same house, same family of talking words. In the present you might say hablo — I speak. But ayer — yesterday — finished, done, told tonight at the table, hablar puts on its past coat and becomes: Hablé — I spoke, I talked. Say it with me, and lean hard on that final beat, the way the accent tells you: Ha-blé. Hablé. Again, like the end of a story: Hablé. Ayer hablé — yesterday I spoke. Say the little sentence: Ayer hablé. And with the person who matters most in it: Hablé con Jillian — I spoke with Jillian. Con — with. Say it whole: Hablé con Jillian. Anoche hablé con Jillian — last night I spoke with Jillian. Say it: Anoche hablé con Jillian. Bill, stop and feel that. You just told me something that happened last night, in Spanish, in the past tense, in a complete true sentence. That is not a drill. That is narration. That is what storytellers do. And the coat fits more verbs than hablar — the next one is its own brother, and you own him already too.

Llamar — to call — the verb of lesson one, the llamar inside me llamo — my name is, I call myself. When calling steps into the finished past, llamar puts on the same é coat, neat as you please: Llamé — I called. Say it with me, leaning on the end: Lla-mé. Llamé. Hear the double l singing its soft y, the sound you tamed on your very first day: yah-MEH. Llamé. And its sentence, the sentence of a man who keeps his promises: Llamé a Jillian — I called Jillian. A — to, at — the little word Spanish puts before the person you called, a courtesy the language pays to people. Say it whole: Llamé a Jillian. Ayer llamé a Jillian — yesterday I called Jillian. Say it: Ayer llamé a Jillian. And now hold hablé and llamé side by side and look at them, because your ear is about to learn the pattern the way it learned buenos and buenas — by noticing, not by memorizing: Hablé. Llamé. Hablé. Llamé. Two different verbs, one same strong ending, one same lean at the end. So if X, then Y — hold onto that: if the verb ends in ar and the action is finished and it was you who did it, then the past coat ends in é, and your voice lands on that é like a period at the end of a sentence. You do not need the rule's grammar name. You need the sound in your mouth: hablé, llamé. Two verbs down, and the pattern is already standing up on its own legs. Say the pair one more time, both sentences, full and true: Ayer hablé con Jillian. Ayer llamé a Jillian. That is a man reporting his day, and we are only getting started.

Now the verb of your life, Bill — the verb of the business, the building, the providing, the man's day itself: Trabajar — to work. And in the finished past, wearing the same faithful é coat: Trabajé — I worked. Say it with me: Tra-ba-jé. Trabajé. Hear the j in the middle, the strong Spanish jota, a breath with muscle in it: trah-bah-HEH. Trabajé. Ayer trabajé — yesterday I worked. Say it like a man stating the plain truth of his life: Ayer trabajé. And with its measure on it, because some days deserve the measure: Trabajé mucho — I worked a lot. Mucho — much, a lot — the word from lesson one's mucho gusto — pleased to meet you — now measuring a day's labor. Say it: Trabajé mucho. Ayer trabajé mucho — yesterday I worked a lot. Say the whole honest line: Ayer trabajé mucho. That sentence, said at your own table, in Spanish, will be true more days than it is false, and Jillian — who works her own long days at the hospital — will hear in it a man who understands her days because he lives his own. Working is not a small topic between two people who both do it. It is half the story of a shared life. And now you can tell that half in Spanish. Next verb, and we stay with the é family for one more member, the pleasant one: Comprar — to buy. Finished, past, done: Compré — I bought. Say it: Com-pré. Compré. Ayer compré — yesterday I bought — and a sentence needs its treasure, so here is one worth buying: Compré flores — I bought flowers. Flores — flowers. Say it whole, and picture handing them over: Compré flores. Ayer compré flores para Jillian — yesterday I bought flowers for Jillian. Para — for. Say it: Ayer compré flores para Jillian. Hablé, llamé, trabajé, compré — say the family roll call with me, four brothers in the same coat: Hablé. Llamé. Trabajé. Compré. Again, faster, feeling the same landing every time: Hablé. Llamé. Trabajé. Compré. That landing, that accented é, is the sound of a finished action in Spanish. Your ear owns it now.

But Spanish would not be Spanish — honest, alive, a little musical — if every verb wore the same coat, so here come the second family, and I teach them to you by feel in the same breath. Verbs that end in er and ir — the eating and living verbs — put on a different past coat for I: í — the sound EE, accented, leaning, landing at the end just like its brother é, but singing higher. Comer — to eat — becomes: Comí — I ate. Say it with me: Co-mí. Comí. Comí bien — I ate well. Bien — well — from lesson nine's estoy bien — I am well. Say it: Comí bien. Ayer comí bien — yesterday I ate well. Say it whole: Ayer comí bien. And the last verb of the working day, the one that closes every story a tired man tells: Dormir — to sleep — becomes: Dormí — I slept. Say it: Dor-mí. Dormí. Anoche dormí — last night I slept. Say it, and notice how anoche and dormí were made for each other, the night flag and the sleeping verb, the most natural pair in this lesson: Anoche dormí. Anoche dormí bien — last night I slept well. Say it: Anoche dormí bien. Now hold the two families side by side, the way we held es and está, and let your ear keep them straight: the ar family lands on é — hablé, llamé, trabajé, compré — and the er and ir family lands on í — comí, dormí. É for the talking, calling, working, buying verbs. Í for the eating and sleeping verbs. Say both roll calls, one after the other: Hablé, llamé, trabajé, compré. Comí, dormí. Again: Hablé, llamé, trabajé, compré. Comí, dormí. Two sounds, six verbs, and every one of them is a true thing you did yesterday or will do tomorrow. That is not a vocabulary list, Bill. That is your day, in the past tense, in Spanish.

Two verbs remain, and they are the two that bring a man home. First: Llegar — to arrive, to get somewhere — and in the past: Llegué — I arrived, I got there. Say it with me: Lle-gué. Llegué. Look at that ending closely, because llegar does a small, tidy thing to stay pronounceable: its coat is gué, with a u standing guard so the g keeps its hard, good sound — lle-GEH, clean and strong. You do not need the spelling rule's name; you need the sound and the sentence, and the sentence is one of the most human sentences in any language: Llegué a casa — I got home, I arrived home. A casa — home, to the house — casa, our old friend from lesson eight, now meaning home the way it always secretly did. Say it whole, and feel the day land when you say it: Llegué a casa. Ayer llegué a casa — yesterday I got home. Say it: Ayer llegué a casa. Every working person on earth tells this sentence, in some language, on most days of their life: I worked, I traveled, I arrived home. It is the hinge of the day — everything before it is the world, everything after it is home. And now the rebel, the one verb in this lesson that refuses every coat we have sewn, and I flag him honestly, the way I promised you I always would: Ir — to go — in the past becomes: Fui — I went. Say it: Fui. One syllable, FWE, like nothing else in this lesson. Fui does not end in é. Fui does not end in í. Fui is fui — ir is the great irregular of Spanish, the rebel verb, and he is irregular in almost every language on earth, because the verb for going gets used so much that it wears its own path through the language like water through rock. English did the same thing, Bill: go and went are strangers to each other, and nobody apologizes for it. So do not fight fui. Adopt him. Fui a casa — I went home. Say it: Fui a casa. Fui a Key West — I went to Key West. Say it, and picture the road south with her: Fui a Key West. Ayer fui a casa — yesterday I went home. Say the rebel's sentences whole: Fui a casa. Fui a Key West. Ayer fui a casa. Good. Every family has one rebel, and the rebel is always the one you remember best. Fui will never leave you, precisely because he refused the coat.

Now let us put a whole day on its feet, because verbs in a row are a roll call, but verbs in an order are a story, and a story is what she actually wants at the table. Listen to a day told whole, in Spanish, with its flags planted and its verbs in their past coats, and I will walk beside it in English so you miss nothing. Ayer trabajé mucho — yesterday I worked a lot. Llamé a Jillian — I called Jillian. Hablé con Jillian — I spoke with Jillian. Compré flores — I bought flowers. Comí bien — I ate well. Llegué a casa — I got home. Anoche dormí bien — last night I slept well. Seven sentences. One day. Hear how the flags do their quiet work: ayer opens the day, anoche closes it, and in between, a man worked, called, talked, bought flowers for his prometida — his fiancée — ate, came home, and slept. Say the whole story with me now, sentence by sentence, out loud, like you are telling it across a real table: Ayer trabajé mucho. Llamé a Jillian. Hablé con Jillian. Compré flores. Comí bien. Llegué a casa. Anoche dormí bien. Again, slower, and this time feel the story in it, not the exercise: Ayer trabajé mucho. Llamé a Jillian. Compré flores. Llegué a casa. Anoche dormí bien. Bill, that is narration. A beginning, a middle, and a man asleep at the end of his own story. And la semana pasada — last week — stretches the same story wider whenever you need it: La semana pasada trabajé mucho — last week I worked a lot. La semana pasada fui a Key West — last week I went to Key West. Say those two: La semana pasada trabajé mucho. La semana pasada fui a Key West. Same coats, wider flag. The past is not a tense, mi estudiante. The past is a country, and today you got your passport.

And now the scene this whole lesson was built for — the other half of the evening conversation from lesson nine. Last lesson, you learned to ask her: ¿Cómo fue tu día, mi corazón? — how was your day, my heart? — and to listen: cuéntame — tell me — te escucho — I am listening to you. But a conversation has two tellings, and tonight you learn yours. She asks you back — she will, love returns questions — ¿cómo fue tu día? — how was your day? — and now, instead of a shrug and the word fine, you have a story in Spanish, three or four true sentences, and true is the whole art here. Not impressive sentences. True ones. Your day, yesterday's shape, told plainly: Ayer trabajé mucho — yesterday I worked a lot. Llamé a un cliente — I called a client. Cliente — a client, a customer. Compré flores para ti — I bought flowers for you. Para ti — for you — ti, you, the word that turns a sentence toward the person listening. And the closer, the sentence that ends every good day-story ever told: Llegué a casa — I got home. Say your four true sentences with me, as one telling, the way you will say them to her: Ayer trabajé mucho. Llamé a un cliente. Compré flores para ti. Llegué a casa. Again, and this time put her face in front of you while you say them — she is at the table, the day is landing on her the way hers landed on you last lesson: Ayer trabajé mucho. Llamé a un cliente. Compré flores para ti. Llegué a casa. Do you feel what changed, Bill? In lesson nine you learned to receive her day. In this lesson you learned to give her yours. Ask, listen, tell, be told. That is not a Spanish exercise. That is the whole liturgy of a shared life, and you can now do all four parts of it in Spanish.

Time for your guided try-it — the doing, taught here, with me beside you, the way we ran the viewing and the evening door. We will build your telling from your own true yesterday, in four moves, and I will show you the moves so you can rebuild it every night with whatever the day actually held. Move one: plant the flag. Ayer — yesterday — or anoche — last night — or la semana pasada — last week. Say your flag out loud: Ayer. Move two: the work sentence, the spine of a weekday. Trabajé — I worked — with mucho — a lot — if the day earned it: Ayer trabajé mucho. Say it. Move three: the people sentence, the heart of the day — who you called, who you spoke with: Llamé a Jillian. Hablé con Jillian. Say one of them, the true one: Llamé a Jillian. Move four: the home sentence, the landing — llegué a casa — I got home — and if the night was kind, anoche dormí bien — last night I slept well. Say the landing: Llegué a casa. Anoche dormí bien. Now run your whole telling, start to finish, your own voice, no stopping: Ayer trabajé mucho. Llamé a Jillian. Llegué a casa. Anoche dormí bien. Again — and if your real yesterday held something else, swap it in with the coats you own: comí bien if the food was good, compré flores if you were wise, fui a casa if the road was the story. The frame holds any true day you pour into it. That is the difference between a phrase and a power, Bill: a phrase says one thing; a power says your life, one day at a time, forever. Run it once more, whole, like a man at his own table: Ayer trabajé mucho. Llamé a Jillian. Llegué a casa. Anoche dormí bien. There it is. You just told me yesterday. In Spanish. And it was true.

Before we leave the workshop, sit with me for two minutes on the sound of these endings, because in the past tense your voice carries the grammar, and I want your telling to land clean when she hears it across the table. Every past coat you learned today ends in a lean: hablé leans on é, comí leans on í, and that lean is not decoration, it is the difference between the present and the past themselves. Listen to the pairs and feel it. Hablo — I speak, now, in the present. Hablé — I spoke, finished, yesterday. Como — I eat, now. Comí — I ate, finished, done. Say the pairs with me, present and past, now and then: Hablo. Hablé. Como. Comí. Hablo. Hablé. Como. Comí. Hear how the past versions land at the end like a door closing gently — that closing sound is the sound of a completed thing, and Spanish listeners hear it the way you hear the th in the past endings of English without ever thinking about it. And one kindness for your ear as you practice this week: the rebel keeps his own music too. Fui has no lean and needs none — one syllable, said plainly, fui, and the whole room knows you went. And llegué carries its guard u so its landing stays strong: lle-gué, never swallowed, never rushed. When you tell your day, slow down on the endings. The endings are where the past lives. Hablé. Trabajé. Comí. Dormí. Llegué. Fui. Say that final roll once more, slowly, landing every one: Hablé. Trabajé. Comí. Dormí. Llegué. Fui. That, Bill, is the drumbeat of every story you will ever tell her about a finished day, and your voice already knows the rhythm.

Before we gather it up, the hooks, quickly, the way we hang words so they stay hung. Dormí hangs on dormant — the English word for sleeping and waiting, asleep in the grass all winter; when you say dormí, hear dormant waking up and telling its story: I slept. Llamé hangs on lesson one itself — llamar, to call, the verb inside your own name sentence — so llamé is an old friend in a new coat, not a stranger. Compré hangs on the market and the till: every receipt you have ever been handed is a little compré waiting to be said out loud. Fui hangs on nothing, and that is his hook: the rebel is remembered because he is the rebel — go, went, ir, fui, the path water wore through the rock. And trabajé — trabajé hangs on the day itself, on your hands at the end of it. A word you say every night about a true thing does not need a hook, Bill. It has a home. Say the two roll calls one last time and hear the whole lesson inside them: Hablé, llamé, trabajé, compré — the é family, landing strong. Comí, dormí — the í family, singing higher. Llegué, the tidy guard at the gate. Fui, the rebel, walking his own road. That is the sound of the Spanish past, and it is in your mouth now.

So let us gather what you own, in order, one last time. The flags of the past: ayer — yesterday, anoche — last night, la semana pasada — last week — plant one and the story knows where it lives. The idea underneath everything: a finished action changes its coat, and for I, the ar family lands on é — hablé — I spoke, llamé — I called, trabajé — I worked, compré — I bought — while the er and ir family lands on í — comí — I ate, dormí — I slept. The homecoming: llegué a casa — I got home. And the rebel, honored, not fought: fui — I went — fui a casa, fui a Key West. And crowning it all, your telling, four true sentences at her table: Ayer trabajé mucho. Llamé a Jillian. Llegué a casa. Anoche dormí bien. That is lesson ten. You came into this course saying hola — hello — to the present moment, and tonight you walked into yesterday and told it back, in order, in Spanish, truthfully. Ay, do not argue with your profesora. I heard the story. It was a good day, and you told it well.

Most give you choices to pick from, and you can also answer out loud or type your answer, whichever you like. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and if you want to read any part of it again before you answer, do it with my blessing: going back to read is learning, not cheating, and in this room it has always been learning. Your speaking practice lives in this lesson too: hear me say each phrase, then say it back until it lands. And your real assignment, tonight, at the table, after she tells you her day and asks about yours: three or four true sentences. No more, no performing, just true: Ayer trabajé mucho. Llamé a Jillian. Llegué a casa. Anoche dormí bien. Say them to her, and watch what happens to an ordinary evening when a man tells his day in the language of the heart — it stops being ordinary. Next lesson, we put time itself in your hands — the clock, the hours, saying when things happen — so your stories can carry not just the day, but the hour. Until then, mi estudiante: cuenta tu día — tell your day. Ayer ya es tuyo — yesterday is already yours.
