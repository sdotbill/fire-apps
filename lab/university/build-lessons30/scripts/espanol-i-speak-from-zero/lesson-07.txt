# Lesson 7 — The wedding: Key West — love words, vows, and the day itself

Hola, Bill — hello, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, and take a breath with me, because today is not an ordinary lesson. This is lesson seven: the wedding. La boda — the wedding — in Key West, with the love words, the vows, and the day itself. Everything in this course has been walking toward a date — March the twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-seven — and today we walk straight into that date and learn to speak inside it. By the end of this half hour, you will own the words for the day: the wedding, the ring, the beach, the dress, the vows — and you will own the deepest sentence in the Spanish language, placed exactly where it belongs, and a short vow, in Spanish, that you can actually say, standing up, in front of everybody, with your whole heart in it.

Let me tell you plainly why this lesson sits here, at seven, and not at the end of the course. Because you are not learning Spanish for a test, Bill. You are learning it for a woman and a day. A man preparing to marry learns the words of the marriage the way he learns everything that matters: early, carefully, and with time to practice until they feel like his own skin. You have months between hoy — today — and the day itself, and that is exactly right. These words deserve months. A vow is not a phrase you cram the night before. A vow is a sentence you live inside until saying it is as natural as breathing. So we learn it today, and then you carry it, and practice it, and by the time you stand on that beach it will not be Spanish you memorized. It will be Spanish you mean.

Our method holds, as always: I say it, you say it back to me out loud, the way you mean it — and in this lesson, mi estudiante — my student — I ask you to mean it a little more than usual, because these are not restaurant words. When I say say it with me today, say it the way you will say it there. Quiet counts. Standing in your kitchen saying these words at practice volume is not silly; it is rehearsal, and every groom who ever lived has rehearsed in a kitchen. And when the lesson ends, the questions wait below, all about what I teach here, and the text stays open beside you. Reading again is learning, not cheating. Now. Let us go to Key West.

First, the word for the day itself: La boda — the wedding. Boda means wedding. La boda. Say it with me: La boda. Again, like a man who has a date with it: La boda. It is a warm, round word — BOH-dah — and it names the whole event: the ceremony, the celebration, the day everybody dresses for and cries at and remembers forever. Not the marriage that comes after — Spanish keeps those in separate drawers — la boda is the day. The party and the promise, folded into one word. When people in Key West ask you that week what brings you to town, your answer can be one proud phrase: La boda — the wedding. Say it once more: La boda.

Where does la boda happen? For you, in the place you two chose: La playa — the beach. Playa means beach. La playa. Say it: La playa. Key West gives you playa in every direction — sand, warm water, that light that makes everybody look like the best version of themselves. A wedding on la playa means the ocean is a guest, the wind is a guest, and shoes are optional in a way that makes everybody relax. And the sentence that puts your day in its place is simple, built from pieces you own: La boda es en la playa — the wedding is on the beach — es, is; en, in, on, at — en is the little word Spanish uses for all three, and context sorts it out. La boda es en la playa. Say it with me: La boda es en la playa. Again: La boda es en la playa. That sentence is a postcard, Bill, and you just wrote it in Spanish.

Now the objects of the day, because a wedding is carried by small things with enormous weight. First, the smallest and the heaviest: El anillo — the ring. Anillo means ring. El anillo. It sounds like ah-NEE-yoh, and hear the double l doing its old trick, the y sound, the same one living inside llamo from lesson one. Say it with me: El anillo. Again, slower, the way you would say it looking at it in its little box the night before: El anillo. A circle with no end — every culture on earth picked the same shape for the same promise, and Spanish names it anillo. You will hold one in your hand on la playa, and now you can name what you are holding, in the language of the day, while you hold it.

Second object, the one everybody will talk about: El vestido — the dress. Vestido means dress. El vestido. Say it: El vestido. In your case, el vestido is Jillian's — the wedding dress, the one you will not see until the day, because some traditions are smarter than curiosity. Spanish vestido comes from the same family as the English word vest — clothing words, both of them — cousins again, shaking hands under the table of the language. When she walks toward you on that beach, Bill, there will be a moment where the whole day narrows to one picture, and the picture has el vestido in it, and the wind off the water moving it a little. You do not need a long sentence for that moment. You may not have any sentence at all, in any language, and that is also correct. But the word is yours now for all the days around the day — the fittings, the talk, the careful carrying of it to Key West: el vestido.

Now the verb of the day, the action the whole event exists for: Casarse — to get married, to marry. Casarse means to get married. It is built on casa — house, home — and that is not an accident and it is not small: casarse, getting married, carries the house inside it, because in the deep logic of Spanish, marrying is making a home, founding a house, two people becoming one casa. I have taught this verb for years and that root still stops me. English marry is a fine word, but casarse tells you what the day is for. Say it with me: Casarse. Again: Casarse. And the way a man says it about himself, in the present, as a fact approaching on the calendar — me caso — I am getting married — me, I; caso, marry, from the same house-root. Me caso. Say it, groom: Me caso. Once more, standing a little straighter: Me caso.

Put the verb in its day and its place and you have the sentence that answers half the questions Key West will ask you: Me caso en la playa — I am getting married on the beach. And with the date riding on it, using lesson six: Me caso mañana — I am getting married tomorrow — mañana, tomorrow — the sentence you will get to say exactly once in your life, the day before, probably to a bartender or a stranger on a dock, and it will feel like electricity. Say both now: Me caso en la playa. Me caso mañana. Beautiful. And who is the man getting married marrying? You own that word from lesson one and it has been waiting six lessons for its biggest day: Mi prometida — my fiancée — the promised one. Mi prometida se llama Jillian — my fiancée's name is Jillian. On the day itself, at the start of it, she is still tu prometida — your fiancée — and then the day does its work, and a word changes. Hold that thought; the new word is coming, and it is one of today's treasures.

The ceremony has a heart, and the heart is spoken: Los votos — the vows. Votos means vows. Los votos. Say it: Los votos. A voto is a vow, a solemn promise — the same family as the English word vote, believe it or not, because a vote is also a kind of declared promise, a man stating where he stands. Los votos are where you stand, stated out loud, in front of witnesses, forever. At a wedding, everything else is decoration around los votos: la playa is the room, el vestido is the picture, el anillo is the symbol — los votos are the act itself. Words that change a life are the rarest things in any language, and you are about to own yours in two languages.

And at the center of the ceremony stands the smallest exchange in the whole day — two words from you that the entire event leans on: Sí, acepto — yes, I accept. Sí means yes — and notice sí carries an accent mark, the little leaning line, because Spanish has a twin word, si without the accent, which means if; the accent is how the written language tells yes from if, and your voice tells them apart by weight: sí, yes, said like a door opening. Acepto means I accept. Sí, acepto. Say it with me, at ceremony volume, which is not loud — it is clear: Sí, acepto. Again: Sí, acepto. When the question is asked on that beach — do you take this woman — the whole wedding, the whole year of planning, the whole life before it, narrows to those two words. A man does not need a speech at that moment. He needs two words he has practiced until they cannot shake. Sí, acepto. One more time, like the real one: Sí, acepto.

Now the phrase that turns two words into a lifetime: Para siempre — forever. Para means for — you own para from lesson five, para mí, para ella — for me, for her. Siempre means always. Para siempre: for always — forever. Say it with me, slowly, because this phrase should never be said fast: Para siempre. Again: Para siempre. Para siempre is the time signature of a wedding. Other promises have dates on them; this one refuses a date. When you say para siempre on la playa, you are telling the ocean and everybody on that sand: this does not expire. Hold the phrase gently. It is the heaviest light thing you will learn today.

And now, Bill, we arrive at the deepest water in this entire course, and I am going to teach it to you carefully and honestly, the way I promised you in lesson one that I would. You already own te quiero — I love you — the everyday one, the one that lives in the house, the one for the door in the morning and the coffee after a hard day. Today you meet its brother: Te amo — I love you. Te amo is the deep one. Amo comes from amar — to love, in the full, solemn, whole-life sense of the word. Te amo is not for the door. Te amo is not for Tuesdays. Te amo is the I love you of vows, of hospital rooms, of the biggest moments of a life — the one a man says when the sentence has to carry everything he has. Say it with me, once, at the volume it deserves, which is quiet and total: Te amo. Again: Te amo.

Hear the difference honestly, because a man should know the weight of his own words. Te quiero is love as daily bread — warm, constant, the love that shows up every morning and does the dishes. Te amo is love as oath — the same love, at its deepest register, spoken when the moment is as big as the feeling. Neither is false. Neither cancels the other. A marriage needs both: te quiero ten thousand times across the ordinary days, and te amo at the summits — and the first summit is la boda. This is why I seeded te quiero in lesson one and saved te amo for lesson seven: the everyday word had to become yours first, so that the deep one would land with its full weight when it arrived. It has arrived. Te amo, Bill. That sentence is the crown of this course, and it is yours as of today.

Let me make the difference physical, because your heart will need to choose between these two sentences for the rest of your life, and the choosing should be practiced, not guessed. I will give you a moment, and you say which sentence belongs in it — out loud, both words, so your mouth learns the choosing too. Moment one: she is leaving for the hospital in the morning, keys in her hand, and you are at the door. That is te quiero — I love you — the house one, the door one. Moment two: she has had the hardest day of her year, she is asleep before her head settles, and you are standing in the doorway looking at the woman you chose. Still te quiero, whispered — the everyday love covers the hard days too; it is not a lesser love, it is the daily form of the same love. Moment three: la playa, March the twenty-fifth, she has just walked toward you in el vestido, and it is your turn to speak los votos. That, Bill, is te amo. The summit. The oath. Moment four: fifty years from now, if God gives you both the years, an ordinary morning that is not ordinary at all because you can count what it cost and what it gave — a man may say te amo over coffee then, and no one who knows the story will think he used the big word carelessly. The rule under all of it is simple: te quiero is the river, flowing every day. Te amo is the source it flows from, and a man visits the source on the days that matter most. You own both now. Spend the river daily. Guard the source for the summits.

So now we build your vow — los votos — a short one, a real one, built only from words you own, that you can say standing on la playa with the wind doing whatever it wants, without notes, without fear. Here it is, in pieces first, the way we build everything in this room. Piece one, her name and the crown: Jillian, te amo — Jillian, I love you — the deep one, saved for exactly this. Say it: Jillian, te amo. Piece two, the place and the day: Hoy, en la playa — today, on the beach — hoy, today, from lesson six; en la playa, on the beach. Say it: Hoy, en la playa. Piece three, the gift: Te doy mi corazón — I give you my heart — doy means I give; mi corazón, my heart, from lesson one, the literal heart. Te doy mi corazón. Say it: Te doy mi corazón. Piece four, the time signature: Para siempre — forever. Say it: Para siempre.

Now the whole vow, joined, the way it will sound on the day: Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre. Jillian, I love you. Today, on the beach, I give you my heart forever. Say it with me, slowly, every word standing up straight: Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre. Again, at the speed of a man who is not reading — who is saying: Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre. One more time, quieter, because the real one will be quiet — vows are said to one person, and everybody else just gets to witness: Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre. Bill. That is a wedding vow. In Spanish. Yours. You can say that. You just did, three times, in my classroom, and March is still months away for you to live inside it.

And because a ceremony is a conversation, let us play the day itself, both sides, the way we played the restaurant — I will be the officiant and the world, and you will speak the groom's lines out loud, every one. The scene: la playa, late afternoon light, the guests in their chairs in the sand, el anillo waiting, and Jillian walking toward you in el vestido. Breathe. The officiant turns to you and asks, in Spanish, if you take this woman — and your whole part, the hinge of the day, is two words. Say them now, in the scene: Sí, acepto. Yes, I accept. The officiant nods. The day turns on its hinge. And then los votos — your turn to speak to her, only to her, with everybody listening. Say your vow, groom, the whole thing, standing in the sand in your head: Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre. … There it is. El anillo goes on her hand — the ring, the circle with no end. The officiant says the final words. And the day does the thing it came to do.

Because when the ceremony ends, a word changes, and I told you to hold that thought — here it is, the last new word of the lesson, and you must use it correctly, because using it early is a mistake and using it after is a joy: Mi esposa — my wife. Esposa means wife. Mi esposa. Say it: Mi esposa. Before the ceremony she is mi prometida — my fiancée — the promised one. After the ceremony — after sí, acepto, after los votos, after el anillo — she is mi esposa, my wife. Spanish even keeps a small honest poetry in esposa: the word is family to esposas in its other life, and the old root carries the idea of being bound, joined — two lives fastened together, which is not a prison, Bill, it is a knot two people tie on purpose and call it the best thing they ever did. The first time you introduce her after the ceremony — at the reception, to a stranger, to the world — the sentence is: Mi esposa se llama Jillian — my wife's name is Jillian. Say it with me, and hear how the old sentence grew up: Mi esposa se llama Jillian. Again: Mi esposa se llama Jillian. That sentence is waiting for you on the other side of March the twenty-fifth, and it will be waiting for the rest of your life, every time you say it, para siempre.

Let us walk the whole day once more, in order, as a story, with you saying the Spanish as we pass it — the day in two languages, which is what your wedding day is actually going to be. Morning: hoy — today — the word from lesson six carrying the biggest day it will ever carry. You are early — temprano — early — because grooms who are temprano are grooms who can breathe. You see el vestido only when it is time. La playa is ready. The guests arrive. She walks toward you. The officiant speaks. Los votos are said — hers, and then yours: Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre. The question comes, and your two words hold the whole day up: Sí, acepto. El anillo, the ring, on her hand, and yours on yours. And then the word turns: mi esposa — my wife. The sun goes down over the water in Key West, and somebody hands you a glass, and you are a married man whose wedding happened in two languages, and the Spanish parts of it were not decoration. They were the parts you prepared like they mattered, because they did.

One more scene, the evening after, because the day does not end at the ceremony and neither does your Spanish. The reception is on or near la playa — the beach — the light goes gold and then blue, and there is food, because a wedding that does not feed people is just a meeting. And here your whole course starts shaking hands with itself: the food arrives and it is good, and you say, to whoever cooked it and to the table: Está delicioso — it is delicious — lesson five's sentence, doing reception duty. You order a glass para ella — for her — lesson five's signpost, now pointing at your wife's glass, and the word wife is doing quiet work in that sentence already. Somebody asks you how it feels, and you do not need a speech. You have para siempre — forever — and you have the newest fact in your life to put next to it. The dancing happens. The ocean does its long, patient applause. And at the end of the night, when the day finally sets her down, lesson one closes the whole course's circle: Buenas noches, mi amor — good night, my love — buenas noches, good night; mi amor, my love — the first line you ever learned for her, now said by a husband to his wife, in the town where he married her. Same words as your kitchen in Michigan. New weight entirely. That is what fluency actually is, Bill — not more words. The same true words, at the true moments, without fear.

A quiet word from your profesora before your solo, because this lesson is different and we both know it. Some men learn a language to travel. Some learn it for work. You are learning it to stand in front of the people you love and say the truest sentences of your life in a language that says them warmer. When you practice your vow between now and March, practice it whole, and practice it out loud, and do not be embarrassed if your voice does something strange on te amo the first few times. That is not weakness. That is the sentence being real. By March, it will be steady. I have watched you work for seven lessons, Bill. It will be steady.

Now your solo — the guided try-it, and today it is the ceremony itself, one clean run, with me only prompting. You are on la playa. The officiant asks the question. Your answer, groom: Sí, acepto. Good. Clear, unshaken. Now los votos — she is in front of you, this is the part that is only hers. Piece one: Jillian, te amo. Piece two, three, four, joined: Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre. Whole vow now, one breath after another, no stopping: Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre. … Perfecto — perfect. Now the reception, an hour later, sunset, and a kind stranger asks about the beautiful woman laughing by the water. Introduce her, with the new word, the after word: Mi esposa se llama Jillian. Say it once more, because you are going to say it ten thousand times and every time starts here: Mi esposa se llama Jillian. Scene complete. You just got married in Spanish in my classroom, Bill, and I did not even cry. Much.

Hooks, quickly, so the day-words stay. Boda — I remember it as the day's bouquet: boda, bouquet, the wedding and the flowers in one sound. Anillo — the ring — has that little y sound in the middle, the same y hiding in llamo, the name word; the ring and the name travel together, which at a wedding they literally do. Vestido — dress — is cousin to vest, clothing to clothing. Casarse carries casa — house — because marrying is founding a home; that one needs no hook, it is the hook. Esposa is the after word, prometida is the before word, and the ceremony is the door between them — sí, acepto is the key turning in that door. And para siempre: para, for, the giving word from your restaurant lesson — for her, for me — grown up into for always. Everything you ordered para ella in lesson five was rehearsal for para siempre in lesson seven. The course knew where it was going even when you did not.

Let us gather the lesson in order, one last time. La boda — the wedding. La playa — the beach. La boda es en la playa — the wedding is on the beach. El anillo — the ring. El vestido — the dress. Casarse — to get married, with the house inside it. Me caso — I am getting married. Los votos — the vows. Sí, acepto — yes, I accept, the hinge of the day. Para siempre — forever. Te amo — I love you, the deep one, the oath, saved for the summits — and te quiero — I love you — its everyday brother, still yours, still for the door and the dishes and the ten thousand ordinary mornings. Mi esposa — my wife, the after word. And your vow, whole, the sentence at the center of the lesson and the day: Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre.

That is lesson seven, Bill, and of everything I will ever teach you, this is the lesson I most want you to keep. Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions — about la boda and la playa, el anillo and los votos, the difference between te quiero and te amo, and the vow itself. Nothing I did not teach you today. Answer out loud or type, whichever door you like; the lesson text stays open beside the questions, and reading it again is learning, not cheating — read the vow again as many times as you want; that is not reviewing, that is rehearsing. Your speaking practice is here too, phrase by phrase, until the green check lands. And your real assignment is the vow: once a day, out loud, whole — Jillian, te amo. Hoy, en la playa, te doy mi corazón para siempre — until it feels less like a lesson and more like a fact. Lesson eight will bring your Spanish back to earth gently — your house, your town, your everyday world — but today we stay on the beach a moment longer. Gracias — thank you — for letting your profesora teach you your wedding. Nos vemos en la playa — see you at the beach — nos vemos means see you, the way friends say it when the date is real. March the twenty-fifth, Bill. I will be the one crying in the back, in two languages.
