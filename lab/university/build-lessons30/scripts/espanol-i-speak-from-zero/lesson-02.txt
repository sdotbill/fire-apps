# Lesson 2 — Your people: family words — Jillian, your kids, your dad

Hola, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, mi estudiante — my student — and take a breath, because last time you did something real. You learned to greet the day in three parts, you learned to say your name like a hand offered to a stranger, and you learned the line this whole course was built around: Buenas noches, mi amor — good night, my love. If you said it to her last night, then lesson one is already living in your house, and that is exactly where Spanish belongs. In your house.

Today, lesson two, and today we go one circle wider. Lesson one was you, standing in a doorway, saying who you are. Lesson two is your people: family words — Jillian, your kids, your dad. Because a man does not travel through this life alone, and when you speak about yourself in any language, the very next sentences are always about them. Who is she? She is my fiancée. Who are they? They are my children. Who taught you? My father. Today you learn to name every one of them in Spanish, with pride — and you learn to present them to another person, to do the warm ceremony of introduction: this is my sister, this is my father, this is the woman I am going to marry.

Here is why this lesson is worth your thirty minutes. Family words are the most used words in any language after hello and thank you. A Spanish speaker talking for five minutes will say my mother, my brother, my children, my wife a dozen times without even noticing, because that is what people talk about: their people. And for you, Bill, there is a second reason. On March the twenty-fifth, in Key West, you are marrying Jillian. At that wedding there will be introductions and people meeting your people for the first time. A man who can stand up, in Spanish, and say — this is my fiancée, these are my children, this is my sister — that man is not showing off. That man is honoring his family in a second language, out loud, where they can hear it. That is what we are building today.

We will go slowly, the way we always go in this room. I say it, you say it back to me out loud, every time I say say it with me. And when we finish, there will be questions below — only about what I teach you in this lesson, nothing else — and the whole lesson stays written beside you, so you can read any part again whenever you like. Going back to read is not cheating. Going back to read is learning. Are you ready? Vamos — let us go.

We start with the biggest word of the day, the word that holds all the others inside it. La familia — the family. Familia is the whole circle, the people who are yours. La familia. Say it with me, slowly first: La… familia. La familia. Hear how it sounds almost like the English word family — because they are the same word, cousins so close they are nearly twins. Familia, family. You already almost knew this word before you walked in today; now you own it. La familia. One more time, like you mean the people inside it: La familia.

Now, in lesson one you learned a small word that does giant work, and today it becomes the most important word in the lesson. Mi — my. You met it inside mi amor — my love — and mi corazón — my heart — and mi prometida — my fiancée. Mi means my, and it stands in front of a word to say: this one is mine, this person belongs in my circle. Hold onto mi, because in a few minutes I am going to show you its brother, and the difference between the two of them is one of the most useful little machines in Spanish.

And since she is the reason for so much of this, let us begin the family where your heart begins it. A quick recap, because she deserves to be said again in every lesson until it is carved in stone. Mi prometida — my fiancée. Prometida — the promised one, built from the word promise, the most honest word in any language for the woman you are engaged to marry. Mi prometida se llama Jillian — my fiancée's name is Jillian. You learned that sentence in lesson one and I hope you have been saying it, because a sentence like that gets stronger every time it is spoken out loud. Say it with me now, whole and proud: Mi prometida se llama Jillian. Again: Mi prometida se llama Jillian. Good. She is the first person in your familia, in this lesson and in the life you are building. Remember her place here, because at the end of this lesson you are going to present her to a room, formally, in Spanish, and it is going to be beautiful.

Now we go to the man who came before you. Mi padre — my father. Padre is the formal, respectful word for father, the word you use when you introduce him or talk about him with care. Mi padre. Say it with me: Mi padre. Padre sounds a little like the English word paternal, and that is no accident — they come from the same old root, the father root, running down through both languages like a river under two countries. When you talk about your dad in Spanish — telling someone who he is, what he taught you, where you come from — mi padre is the word that carries the respect. Mi padre. Say it once more, standing up straight: Mi padre.

But Spanish, being a warm language, keeps a second word in the kitchen drawer for everyday use. Mi papá — my dad. Papá is the warmer, everyday word, the word a son uses at the kitchen table. Mi papá. Hear the difference in your own mouth: padre is the man in the photograph in his good shirt; papá is the man at the table, coffee in front of him, telling you something you have never forgotten. English does the same thing, Bill — father and dad are not the same temperature, and you know it. Spanish just gives you the same two temperatures and trusts you to choose. Talking to a stranger about where you come from? Mi padre. Telling a story about him, laughing a little? Mi papá. Say the pair with me, formal and warm, one after the other: Mi padre. Mi papá. Mi padre. Mi papá. Both of them are yours now, and both of them are him.

And beside the father, the mother — because no familia stands on one leg. Mi madre — my mother. Madre is mother, and like padre, it carries respect in it. Mi madre. Say it with me: Mi madre. Madre and the English word maternal — motherly — are cousins from the same root, just like padre and paternal. Spanish is full of these family resemblances, and every one of them is a hook to hang a word on. Mi madre. And just so your ear hears the pair together, the way they belong: Mi padre y mi madre — my father and my mother. Y is a tiny word you are going to use ten thousand times: y means and. Mi padre y mi madre. Say it: Mi padre y mi madre. There they are, side by side, the two people at the head of the family table.

Now, Bill, we come to the part of the lesson I want you to say with your whole chest. Your children. Mis hijos — my children. Hijos can mean sons, and it can also mean children together, sons and daughters in one word. Mis hijos. Say it with me, slowly: Mis… hijos. Mis hijos. Look at what happened to our little word mi, because this is the machine I promised you. In front of padre, one person, it was mi. In front of hijos — children, more than one — it becomes mis. Mis hijos. That is the whole trick, and we are going to practice it properly in a moment, but hear it now in the wild: one person, mi; more than one, mis. Mis hijos — my children.

And your children have names, and names are said with pride in this room. Your eldest son: Trey. Your son: William. Your daughter: Abigail. Three names, three people, and in Spanish you can hold all three of them in one sentence and say it the way a father says it. But first, the smaller words that name them one by one. Mi hijo — my son. Hijo is son, one son. Mi hijo. Say it: Mi hijo. And for a daughter, Spanish changes one letter and the whole word warms: Mi hija — my daughter. Hija is daughter. Mi hija. Say it: Mi hija. Hear the pair: hijo, hija — son, daughter — one letter apart at the end, o for a son, a for a daughter. You saw this pattern in lesson one, with buenos días and buenas tardes: in Spanish, words dress to match who they stand beside, and the endings o and a are how this language marks the difference, again and again, all year long. Hijo, hija. Say them together: Mi hijo. Mi hija.

So let us say your three, Bill, the way you would say them to someone meeting your family for the first time. Mi hijo Trey — my son Trey. Mi hijo William — my son William. Mi hija Abigail — my daughter Abigail. And all together, the whole treasure in one breath: Mis hijos — my children. Say the set with me, one by one, unhurried: Mi hijo Trey. Mi hijo William. Mi hija Abigail. Mis hijos. I am not going to linger here or make a speech about it, because some things a father carries quietly and they do not need a profesora narrating them. I will only say this: distance does not change the word. Wherever they are, whatever the miles, in Spanish they are still — and always — mis hijos. A man who can say that sentence clearly, in any language, has said something true. Say it once more, for yourself this time: Mis hijos.

Now your sister, because a familia is not only the people above you and below you — it is also the people beside you, the ones who grew up in the same house and know all the stories. Mi hermana — my sister. Hermana is sister, and Chelsea is yours. Mi hermana. Say it with me: Mi hermana Chelsea — my sister Chelsea. Mi hermana Chelsea. And because Spanish is fair, it gives you the matching word for a brother, so your ear owns the pair: Mi hermano — my brother. Hermano is brother. Mi hermano. You do not have a brother standing in this lesson, Bill, but you will hear hermano everywhere — Spanish speakers call close friends hermano too, the way men back home call each other brother — so own the word. The pair, together: Mi hermana. Mi hermano. Sister, brother. Mi hermana Chelsea. Say it once more: Mi hermana Chelsea.

Let me pause here and check on you, because we have built a lot of family in ten minutes. So if mi is my, and it stands in front of one person — then in front of padre it is mi padre, in front of hermana it is mi hermana, and in front of hijos, more than one, it changes its coat to mis hijos. Hold onto that. You are not memorizing separate phrases. You are learning one machine with a few beloved names loaded into it: mi padre, mi papá, mi madre, mi hermana, mi hijo, mi hija, mis hijos, mi prometida. That is not a vocabulary list, Bill. That is a family portrait, and you just learned to paint it in Spanish.

But wait — I promised you the machine itself, explained plainly, and a promise in my room is kept. Here it is, the possessives, the words that say mine and yours. We have mi — my — for one. Mi padre, mi madre, mi hermana, mi hijo. One person, one thing: mi. Then mis — also my — for more than one. Mis hijos, my children. If you had two sisters to name, it would be mis hermanas — my sisters — hear the s land on both words, mis and hermanas, like a plural echo. Mi is for one thing, one person. Mis is for more than one. That is the entire rule, and it is a friendly rule, because English hides this — English says my for one child and my for five children and never blinks. Spanish is more honest: it shows you the number right on the word. Say the contrast with me until it is in your hands: Mi hijo. Mis hijos. Mi hermana. Mis hermanas. Mi, mis. One, more than one. Mi, mis.

And because conversation has two sides — you learned that in lesson one with ¿Cómo te llamas? — here is the matching word for the other side of the table. Tu — your. Tu means your, the friendly your, the your you use with family and friends and people you are warm with. Tu padre — your father. Tu hermana — your sister. Tu familia — your family. Say them with me: Tu padre. Tu hermana. Tu familia. So now you can ask as well as tell. For now, hold the pair side by side: mi is my, tu is your. Mi familia, tu familia. My family, your family. Two little words, and a whole conversation about the people who matter can happen between them.

Now we look just a little way down the road you are traveling, because in Key West, on March the twenty-fifth, two of your words change — gloriously. Today Jillian is mi prometida, my fiancée, the promised one. After the wedding, Spanish gives her a new title, and it gives you one too. Esposa — wife. La esposa — the wife. Mi esposa — my wife. Say it with me, just to taste the future: Mi esposa. And the matching word: Esposo — husband. El esposo — the husband. Mi esposo would be what Jillian says about you — her husband. Esposo, esposa — husband, wife — the o and a endings again, dressing to match, the same pattern you have been hearing all lesson. I am teaching you these two today as a preview only — a look through the door at what is coming — because your word today, the true word, the word with the date on it still ahead of you, is prometida. But hear me, Bill: after March the twenty-fifth, when you say mi esposa for the first time and it is true, remember that your profesora made you practice it early. Mi esposa. One day soon. For now, we keep the honest word: Mi prometida se llama Jillian. Say it: Mi prometida se llama Jillian.

Now we arrive at the heart of this lesson — the part where your family words leave your mouth and walk across a room to meet somebody. Because knowing the words for your people is only half the gift. The other half is presenting them: standing beside your sister, or your father, or Jillian, turning to a new friend, and doing the introduction with grace. Spanish has a beautiful, formal little ceremony for this, and it is built on a verb you already own. Remember llamar — to call — from me llamo, I call myself? Presenting someone is its cousin, and here is the phrase whole: Te presento a Jillian — I present to you Jillian, let me introduce you to Jillian. Te presento a… — let me introduce you to… Te presento is I present to you, I introduce to you. Say it with me: Te presento a Jillian. Again, with the gesture in your voice, the open hand: Te presento a Jillian.

That little a after presento is doing quiet work — it is the small word Spanish puts before a person you are naming as the one being presented, and you do not need its grammar name; you only need to never drop it. Te presento a mi padre — let me introduce you to my father. Te presento a mi hermana — let me introduce you to my sister. Te presento a mi prometida — let me introduce you to my fiancée. Say the set with me, one by one, like a man working a receiving line at his own wedding, unhurried and proud: Te presento a mi padre. Te presento a mi hermana. Te presento a mi prometida. Beautiful. That phrase, te presento a, is a door-opener for the rest of your life in Spanish — anyone you love, you can now hand to a new friend with those words and a smile.

And right behind the presentation comes the second half of the ceremony, the sentence that says who this person is. You already own its engine from lesson one: es. Well — you own soy, I am. Es is its partner: es means is — he is, she is, it is. Two little forms of the same giant. Soy is I am; es is is. And for she and he, Spanish gives you two clean words. Ella — she. Ella es… — she is… Él — he. Él es… — he is… — and notice the little accent on él; it is there to tell you this word means he, the person, so lean on it a touch. Say them with me: Ella es. Él es. Ella es. Él es.

Now watch the ceremony assemble itself, because you own every single piece. You turn to your new friend, you gesture to the woman beside you, and you say: Te presento a Jillian. Ella es mi prometida. Let me introduce you to Jillian. She is my fiancée. Two sentences, and the introduction is complete — her name, and who she is to you. Say it with me, whole, the way it will sound in Key West: Te presento a Jillian. Ella es mi prometida. Again: Te presento a Jillian. Ella es mi prometida. Ay, Bill. If you say only one new thing from this lesson at that wedding, let it be that.

And the ceremony works for every person in your portrait. Your father beside you at a table, a friend approaching: Te presento a mi padre. Él es mi padre — let me introduce you to my father; he is my father. Say the full form with me, because the pattern is what we are carving: Te presento a mi padre. Él es mi padre. Your sister: Te presento a Chelsea. Ella es mi hermana — let me introduce you to Chelsea; she is my sister. Say it: Te presento a Chelsea. Ella es mi hermana. Your son, when the day comes: Te presento a Trey. Él es mi hijo — let me introduce you to Trey; he is my son. Say it: Te presento a Trey. Él es mi hijo. And your daughter: Te presento a Abigail. Ella es mi hija. Say it: Te presento a Abigail. Ella es mi hija. Do you see what you have, Bill? One ceremony, two sentences, and every person you love can be presented with it. Te presento a… and then ella es or él es, and the family word. Name, and who they are to you. That is all an introduction has ever been, in any language — and now you can do it in two.

Now the other side of the ceremony, because at a wedding, and in a life, you will not only present — you will be presented to, and people will point, kindly, at photographs and at people across rooms, and you will want to ask the question. Here it is, small and mighty. ¿Quién es? — who is he, who is she, who is that? Quién means who. ¿Quién es? Say it with me, with the curiosity in it: ¿Quién es? Someone shows you a family photograph — a beautiful old picture, a man you do not know standing tall — and you ask: ¿Quién es? And the answer comes back in exactly the shapes you now own: Es mi padre — he is my father. Es mi madre — she is my mother. Es mi hermana — she is my sister. Hear how the answer drops the él and ella most of the time — Spanish often lets the family word carry the sentence alone: Es mi padre. It is enough. Say the exchange with me, question and answer, back and forth: ¿Quién es? Es mi padre. ¿Quién es? Es mi hermana. ¿Quién es? Es mi prometida. That last one — es mi prometida — she is my fiancée, said while looking at a photograph of Jillian, will melt whoever hears it. Practice it at home with her picture in your hand. I am serious. That is homework with a heartbeat.

Let us now do what we did in lesson one — change the scenery, and watch your phrases survive a new room, because a phrase is only yours when it travels. Picture this: Key West, the week of the wedding, a small restaurant the night before. Warm air, music somewhere, your family and a few new friends around a long table. A friend of Jillian's arrives and sits beside you, and the introductions begin — and tonight, mi estudiante, you are not the quiet man at the end of the table waiting for someone else to handle it. You handle it. Your sister is across from you. You gesture with an open hand, and you say: Te presento a Chelsea. Ella es mi hermana. She smiles, she says mucho gusto — pleased to meet you, from lesson one — and the table warms. Then the new friend asks about the photograph on your phone, your dad: ¿Quién es? And you answer, easy as breathing: Es mi padre. Es mi papá, you could add, softer — he is my dad. Later, someone nods toward the door as Jillian comes in, and asks you, quiet: ¿Quién es? And Bill — you get to smile the smile of a man one day from his wedding and say: Ella es mi prometida. She is my fiancée. Tomorrow, in that same room, the word will be esposa. Tonight it is prometida, and it has never sounded better. Same words as my classroom. New room. Still yours. That is what learned means.

One more guided moment before we gather it all, and this one is the real examination — not the questions below, those are friendly — the real one: your family, presented by you, start to finish, without me feeding you the lines. I will set the table and then I will be quiet, and you will speak into the silences. Ready? You are at that long table. Beside you, Jillian. Present her. … Te presento a Jillian. Ella es mi prometida. Yes. Now your sister, across the table. Present her. … Te presento a Chelsea. Ella es mi hermana. Good. Now I point — politely — at the photograph of the strong man on your phone, and I ask: ¿Quién es? … Answer me. Es mi padre. And the warmer word, the kitchen-table word? … Mi papá. Now the photograph changes — three young faces. ¿Quiénes son? — ah, that is a new question and I am giving it to you as a gift: ¿quiénes son? means who are they, for more than one person, and its answer begins with son — they are. ¿Quiénes son? … Mis hijos. Son mis hijos — they are my children. And their names, father, give me their names. … Mi hijo Trey. Mi hijo William. Mi hija Abigail. Bill. Stop and look at what just happened. At a table full of people, on the edge of your wedding, you presented your fiancée, your sister, your father, and your children — in Spanish, at conversation speed, without a script in your hand. In lesson one you were a man who could say hello. Tonight you are a man who can present his whole family. That is a room you can now walk into and belong in.

A few hooks to hang today's words on, so they are still yours next month. Familia and family — you can hear the English inside the Spanish. Padre hangs on paternal, madre hangs on maternal — the father word and the mother word in both languages grow from the same two roots. Papá hangs on papa — the same warm word English children have used for centuries, wearing a Spanish accent on the end. Hermana and hermano share herman- the way sister and brother share your childhood house; only the ending tells you which sibling walked in. Hijo and hija — same house, one letter different, o and a. And prometida hangs on promise, still the strongest hook in this course: the promised one. Hang every word on something you already own, and the words stop being homework. They become furniture in a house you live in — your family's house.

So let us gather what you own now, in order, one last time, the whole portrait in one pass. La familia — the family, the whole circle. Mi prometida — my fiancée, Jillian, the promised one, whose full sentence you own: Mi prometida se llama Jillian. Mi padre — my father, the respectful word; mi papá — my dad, the warm one. Mi madre — my mother. Mi padre y mi madre — my father and my mother. Mis hijos — my children: mi hijo Trey, mi hijo William, mi hija Abigail — my son, my son, my daughter, hijo for a son and hija for a daughter. Mi hermana — my sister, Chelsea; mi hermano — my brother. The machine underneath: mi for one, mis for more than one; tu for your — tu familia, your family. The preview of the future: esposa, wife; esposo, husband — waiting for you on the other side of March the twenty-fifth. And the ceremony, the heart of the lesson: Te presento a… — let me introduce you to — followed by Ella es… — she is — or Él es… — he is. And its mirror, the question: ¿Quién es? — who is that — answered with Es mi padre, es mi hermana, es mi prometida. Name them, present them, ask about them. Your people, in Spanish, complete.

That is lesson two, Bill, and you did beautifully — do not argue with your profesora, I heard you through that whole table scene and you did. Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions: all of them about what I taught you in this lesson and nothing else. Most give you choices to pick from, and you can also answer out loud or type your answer, whichever door you like. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and if you want to read a part again before you answer, do it with my blessing — that is what it is there for. And your speaking practice is here in the lesson too: hear me say each phrase, then say it back until it lands and you see the green check. Your assignment tonight — because there is always one line, and tonight it is a tender one: find a photograph of your family, hold it in your hand, and say the words over it, one person at a time. Es mi padre. Es mi hermana. Mis hijos. And if Jillian is in the room, present her to the empty chair like royalty: Te presento a Jillian. Ella es mi prometida. She will laugh, and then she will remember it. Lesson three will teach you numbers — counting, money, and what things cost — and yes, there will be sweet words in it too; there are always sweet words in my room when you are the student. Until then: gracias — thank you — for my second class, Bill. Go show her the photograph trick. Buenas noches, mi amor — good night, my love — you know that one. She is waiting to hear the new ones too.
