# Lesson 1 — Hola: greetings, your name, and your first real conversation

Hola, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and this is your Spanish room. Sit down, get comfortable, and do not worry about a single thing, because we start from zero — from before zero — and we start together. In this room we speak first. That is my whole method, and it has never failed anyone who actually opened their mouth: I say it, you say it back to me out loud, the way you mean it, and only then do we move on. Reading about Spanish is not learning Spanish, the same way reading about swimming is not swimming. Your voice is the instrument here, so warm it up.

Here is how this lesson works. For the next thirty minutes or so, I teach, and you listen and speak along with me — every time I say "say it with me," I want to hear you, out loud, wherever you are. Do not be shy with me, mi estudiante. Nobody is grading your accent today; we are building it. When the lesson is finished, there will be questions — real ones, about what I actually taught you in this lesson and nothing else — and the whole lesson stays written out beside you, so you can read any part of it again whenever you like. Going back to read is not cheating. Going back to read is learning.

And because I know you, Bill, let me tell you the secret mission of this whole course, which is not very secret. By your wedding day — March the twenty-fifth, in Key West — you are going to hold a real, gentle conversation with Jillian in Spanish. Not a performance. Not a party trick. A conversation: good morning, my love; how was your day; I love you; good night, my heart — said the way a man says things he means. That is where this road goes, and today we take the first real steps on it: how to greet anyone at any hour, how to say your name, how to be polite, and how to say the first sweet words. Everything in Spanish starts here.

Before the first word, thirty seconds on how Spanish sounds, because this will make everything easier. Spanish is an honest language. English hides its sounds — one letter can make five different noises and you just have to memorize which. Spanish almost never does that to you. It has five vowel sounds — a, e, i, o, u — and each one says its sound every single time, the same way, in every word. A sounds like the a in "father." E sounds like the e in "met." I sounds like the ee in "see." O sounds like the o in "go." U sounds like the oo in "moon." Five sounds, no surprises. If you can read a Spanish word letter by letter, you can say it — that is the promise of this language, and it keeps its promise almost every time.

One letter breaks the pattern, and it breaks it by doing nothing at all: the letter h. In Spanish, the h is silent — completely silent, always. It is written, but it is never heard. Hold that thought, because your very first word is hiding one.

Here it is. Your first word in Spanish, and the most useful word in any language on earth: Hola. That is hello — for any hour, any person, any door you ever walk through. Because of that silent h, it sounds like "OH-lah" — two clean beats, OH-lah. Say it with me, out loud: Hola. Again, and smile when you say it, Spanish can hear a smile: Hola. One more, like you just walked into a room full of people who are glad you came: Hola. That is it. You have spoken Spanish. Whatever happens for the rest of this course, that word is yours now, and nobody can take it back.

Now, hola is friendly and universal, but Spanish also greets the day itself, and it does it in three parts, because the day has three parts. In the morning, you say: Buenos días — good morning. Buenos días. Let me take it apart for you, because you will see this pattern all year. Día means day, and días is days — and buenos means good, in the form it takes next to a word like días. So buenos días is, word for word, "good days" — Spanish wishes you more than one good day at a time, which I have always thought was generous. You use buenos días from the moment you wake up until about midday. Say it with me: Buenos días. Once more, slower: Buenos… días. Buenos días.

When the afternoon comes, the greeting changes its coat: Buenas tardes — good afternoon. Tardes means afternoons, or the late part of the day. And notice what happened to the word for good: next to días it was buenos, and next to tardes it is buenas. That is not a trick and it is not two words to memorize — it is one idea wearing two endings. In Spanish, describing words dress to match the word they stand beside. Días takes the ending -os; tardes takes the ending -as. You do not need the rule's name and you do not need a grammar book tonight; you only need your ear to notice: buenos días, buenas tardes. The ending follows its partner. Say the pair with me, morning and afternoon, one after the other: Buenos días. Buenas tardes. Buenos días. Buenas tardes. Good. Your ear is already doing grammar, and we have not opened a single book.

And when evening comes — from about the time the light goes, all the way to bed — you say: Buenas noches — good evening, and also good night. Noches means nights. Buenas noches is the hardest-working greeting in Spanish, because it does two jobs: it is what you say when you arrive somewhere in the evening, hello, and it is what you say when you leave at night, good night, sleep well, all folded into the same two words. Remember that double job — before this lesson ends, you are going to use buenas noches in the most important sentence of your day. Say it with me now: Buenas noches. Softer, the way you would say it at a bedroom door: Buenas noches.

So here is your greeting compass, and it is the whole map: hola works always. Buenos días in the morning. Buenas tardes in the afternoon. Buenas noches in the evening and at night. Four greetings, and you can walk into any room in the Spanish-speaking world, at any hour, and open your mouth like you belong there — because with these, you do.

Now we give the greeting a person to land on. After hello comes the most useful sentence in any language: saying who you are. In Spanish, you say: Me llamo Bill. That is "my name is Bill" — but look at what it literally says, because Spanish is charming here. Llamo comes from llamar, to call. Me llamo is "I call myself." Me llamo Bill: I call myself Bill. I love teaching this, because it is true in a way the English version is not: a name is what you call yourself, what you answer to, what you offer a stranger like a hand. Say it with me, piece by piece first: Me… llamo… Bill. Me llamo Bill. Again, at full speed, like a man introducing himself at his own wedding: Me llamo Bill.

And because a conversation has two sides, here is the question that turns your sentence around: ¿Cómo te llamas? — what is your name? Word for word: "How do you call yourself?" Cómo means how. Te llamas is "you call yourself." ¿Cómo te llamas? Say it with me: ¿Cómo te llamas? When someone answers you with their name, Spanish has a small, warm ceremony for what you say back: Mucho gusto — pleased to meet you. Mucho is much, a lot. Gusto is pleasure. "Much pleasure" — it is my great pleasure to meet you. That is the temperature of this language, Bill: even its manners run warm. Say it: Mucho gusto. With the smile again: Mucho gusto.

So let us put the first exchange together, the whole little dance, and I will play both parts while you shadow me. I walk in: Hola, buenos días. Me llamo Carmen. ¿Cómo te llamas? And you answer: Hola. Me llamo Bill. Mucho gusto. Four lines, and you have had a conversation — a real one, with a beginning, a meeting, and warmth in it. Run it once more in your own voice, both your lines out loud: Hola. Me llamo Bill. Mucho gusto. Ay, Bill — so close to perfect, and we are only halfway through your first lesson.

Two small words now, and do not let their size fool you: they are the two keys that open every door in Spanish. The first key: Por favor — please. Por favor. Use it everywhere, attach it to everything; in Spanish a request without por favor is a door knocked on with a closed fist. The second key: Gracias — thank you. Gracias. And when someone thanks you, Spanish answers: De nada — you are welcome, which word for word is "of nothing" — it was nothing, think nothing of it. Por favor and gracias: please and thank you. A man who carries these two words can travel anywhere on earth and be treated like a gentleman. Say the pair with me: Por favor. Gracias. Por favor. Gracias. De nada.

Next, where you are from and what you do — because after your name, that is what people ask, and you are going to answer like you own it. Soy de Michigan — I am from Michigan. Soy is the giant here: soy means "I am." It is the first word of a man's description of himself, so say it like one: Soy. De means from. Soy de Michigan: I am from Michigan. Say it: Soy de Michigan. And what you do: Soy empresario — I am a businessman. Empresario: a man of enterprise, a man who builds things and employs people. Soy empresario. Notice that soy does both jobs — where you are from and what you are — because in Spanish, who you are and where you are from are the same kind of fact. Say both lines together now, your whole introduction in two breaths: Soy de Michigan. Soy empresario.

Now the line I have been saving, because it is the reason this course exists. You are going to learn to talk about her. Mi prometida se llama Jillian — my fiancée's name is Jillian. Take it apart slowly. Mi means my. Prometida means fiancée — your promised one; in Spanish the word for the woman you are engaged to is built from the word promise, and I have never heard a more honest word for it. Se llama — there is your llamar again, turned around: se llama is "she calls herself," her name is. Mi prometida se llama Jillian. Say it with me, slowly first: Mi prometida… se llama… Jillian. And now whole, at the speed of a man who is proud of the sentence: Mi prometida se llama Jillian. That sentence, Bill, is worth the whole lesson by itself. A man who can say that sentence in her presence, in Spanish, clearly and without fumbling — that man has given his prometida a gift she will remember on the hard days.

And now the sweet words — las palabras dulces — because Spanish is not only a language for doors and introductions; it is the language the world reaches for when it wants to say the tender things. We start tonight, in lesson one, because I know exactly why you are really in this room, and it is not for the grammar. First: Mi amor — my love. Amor is love, the noun, the thing itself. Mi amor is what you call her. Not a description — a name you give her, the way her name is Jillian and also, in your house, mi amor. Say it the way it deserves to be said, slowly: Mi amor.

Second: Mi corazón — my heart. Corazón means heart — the actual word, the organ, the thing in your chest. Spanish does not hide behind poetry here; it means it literally. When you call her mi corazón, you are saying: you are my heart, the thing that beats in me. Spanish speakers have called the people they love mi corazón for centuries, fathers to daughters, grandmothers to grandchildren, and a man to the woman he is going to marry. Say it: Mi corazón. Once more, quieter: Mi corazón.

And now the sentence itself: Te quiero — I love you. This is the everyday way to say it in Spanish, and I choose those words carefully. Spanish has more than one way to say I love you — later in this course you will meet te amo, which is deeper, heavier, saved for the biggest moments of a life. Te quiero is the one that lives in the house. Te quiero is what you say at the door in the morning. Te quiero is what you say when she has had a hard day at the hospital and you hand her coffee. It comes from querer, which means both to want and to love — in Spanish, wanting her and loving her were never separate ideas. Te quiero. Say it with me, and mean it at the picture of her in your head: Te quiero. And with her name on it, the way it will actually be said in your kitchen: Te quiero, Jillian. Te quiero, Jillian.

Now watch what happens when we put the whole day together, because this is the moment your first lesson becomes your real life. Evening. She has come home. The day is done, the house is quiet, and you say the line this entire lesson was built to give you: Buenas noches, mi amor — good night, my love. There is buenas noches, doing its second job, the good-night job. And there is mi amor, riding on the end of it like it was always there. Buenas noches, mi amor. Say it with me, slowly, at bedroom-door volume: Buenas noches… mi amor. Again: Buenas noches, mi amor. Bill. Listen to me. You can say that tonight. Not next month, not after the wedding — tonight, with lesson one. That is not homework. That is a gift with today's date on it.

Before we put the whole day together, sit with me in the workshop for a few minutes, because there are three sounds I want to tune in your mouth while the words are still new. First, the sound hiding inside llamo. The double l in Spanish — ll — is not an l at all. It sounds like the y in "yes," softened a little toward the j in "jump." So me llamo sounds like "meh YAH-moh." You have been saying it almost right all lesson; now you know why it felt slippery. Say it again with the y: Me llamo Bill. ¿Cómo te llamas? — hear the same y inside llamas. Me llamo. Te llamas. Good — that one sound just improved every sentence you own.

Second sound: the written accent, that little leaning line you see in días and corazón. It is not decoration and it is not stress for your eyes — it is an instruction for your voice. It tells you: the beat falls here. Spanish words naturally lean on one beat, and the accent mark shows you where to lean when the word does something special. Días: DEE-ahs, the beat on the í. Corazón: koh-rah-SOHN, the beat lands at the end, exactly where the accent sits. When you see that little line, lean there. Mi corazón — lean on the end: mi corazón. You will never have to guess where a word's weight goes in this language; Spanish prints the answer right on the word.

Third, a small one with a big future: the letter r. A single r in Spanish is a quick tap of the tongue — por favor has two of them, light as a drumstick bounce: por favor. Later in this course you will meet its famous brother, the rolled rr, and we will tame him together when he arrives, with amor and corazón as our practice ground. For today, the tap is enough. Por favor. Gracias — hear the tap hiding in gracias too. Your mouth is learning the map of this language faster than your mind is, and that is exactly how it is supposed to work.

One more thing before the day begins, and it is not grammar — it is manners, and in Spanish the two are cousins. In the Spanish-speaking world, you greet the room. You walk into a small shop, a café, an elevator with people in it, and you say buenos días or buenas tardes to the room in general — to everyone and no one, the way you would nod in a small town back home, except here it has words. Not greeting is what reads as rude; the greeting is just the sound of a person raised right. And when Jillian's coworkers or your new neighbors in Key West greet you first — and they will — you now own everything you need to answer like a gentleman: Hola. Buenos días. Mucho gusto. That is the whole ceremony, and you are already dressed for it.

Now let me change the scenery on you, because a phrase is only yours when it survives a new room. Picture a café in Key West, the wedding week, morning light. You walk in first — greet the room: Buenos días. The woman behind the counter smiles and asks your name for the order. You do not point at yourself and grunt like a tourist; you say: Me llamo Bill. She says hers — Carmen, let us say, a woman of excellent taste in names — and you answer with the warm ceremony: Mucho gusto. She asks where you are from — they always ask, your face is friendly — and you give her the two lines, easy as breathing: Soy de Michigan. Soy empresario. Por favor and gracias carry your coffee to the table. And when the beautiful woman walks in and joins you, and the counter woman asks, you get to say the sentence in front of a witness this time: Mi prometida se llama Jillian. Same words as the kitchen. New room. Still yours. That, Bill, is what it means for a lesson to be learned: it travels.

Let us now walk your first full conversation from the top, start to finish, the way it will really happen, and I will narrate while you speak your parts out loud. Morning: she comes into the kitchen, and you are ready. Buenos días, mi amor. She smiles — she will smile — and later, meeting someone new together, you introduce yourself the way you learned: Hola, me llamo Bill. Mucho gusto. You are polite all day without thinking: Por favor. Gracias. De nada. Someone asks where the man beside her is from: Soy de Michigan. Soy empresario. And when they ask about the beautiful woman next to you, you do not hesitate: Mi prometida se llama Jillian. And at the end of the day, at the door of the bedroom, the two sentences this course is really about: Te quiero, Jillian. Buenas noches, mi amor. That is a day, Bill. A whole day, lived partly in Spanish, on your first lesson. Most people study a language for a year and cannot do what you just did in thirty minutes — because they studied it. You spoke it.

Now, because I am your profesora and not your parrot, let me give you hooks to hang these words on, so they are still yours next week and not just tonight. Spanish and English are cousins — more of this language is already in your head than you think. Corazón and the English word core are family: both come from the old word for heart, which is why the core of an apple is its heart and the core of a man is what he loves. When you say mi corazón, think core — my core, my heart, same word wearing two coats. Amor lives inside the French amour and inside a dozen English words about love that your ear already knows. Gracias is a cousin of grace and gratitude — when you say gracias, you are saying grace, the same word your grandmother said before dinner. Por favor carries favor inside it, plain as day: a favor, asked politely. Días is day with a Spanish ending — you can hear the day inside it. And hola — well, hola sounds like a wave hello across a fence, which is exactly what it is. Hang every new word on a hook you already own, and the words stop being homework and start being furniture in a house you live in.

And now, the real examination of a lesson — not the questions waiting below, those are easy — the real one: can you do it with a stranger, at full speed, without me narrating? Let us find out. I am going to be a neighbor you have never met, on a porch, in the evening. I will speak my lines, and leave you silence to answer in yours. Ready? Here I am, coming up the walk: Buenas noches. … And you greet me back — yes, with buenas noches, the evening one, you have it. Now I ask the question: ¿Cómo te llamas? … Answer me — me llamo, and your name, full sentence, no shrinking. … I smile and say: Mucho gusto. … And you return the ceremony — mucho gusto, warm, like you mean it. Now I am curious about you, the way neighbors are: ¿De dónde eres? — that is a new question and I am teaching it to you right now, because you already own its answer: de dónde means from where, and your answer begins soy de. ¿De dónde eres? … Soy de Michigan. There it is. And because neighbors are neighbors, I nod at the house and ask about the woman on the porch, and you — you know what you say. … Mi prometida se llama Jillian. Bill. Stop. Look at what just happened. A stranger walked up a porch in the evening, and you greeted her, named yourself, traded courtesies, placed yourself on the map, and named your prometida with pride — in Spanish, at conversation speed, on your first day. That is not a student. That is a man who speaks some Spanish now. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.

So let us gather what you own now, in order, one last time. Hola — hello, for any hour, any person. Buenos días, buenas tardes, buenas noches — the day in three greetings, with buenas noches working twice, hello in the evening and good night at the end. Me llamo Bill — your name, offered like a hand. ¿Cómo te llamas? — the question that turns it around. Mucho gusto — pleased to meet you. Por favor and gracias — the two keys, please and thank you, and de nada for you are welcome. Soy de Michigan, soy empresario — where you are from and what you are. Mi prometida se llama Jillian — her, named with pride. And the sweet words, the heart of the whole lesson: mi amor, mi corazón, te quiero, and buenas noches, mi amor for tonight. And underneath all of it, the two sounds-rules that will carry you all year: five vowels that never change their story, and the silent h that you will never pronounce again.

That is lesson one, and you did beautifully — ay, do not argue with your profesora, I heard you, you did. Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions: more than a few, all of them about what I taught you in this lesson and nothing else — nobody is going to ask you one single thing I did not teach. Most of them give you choices to pick from, and you can also answer out loud or type your answer, whichever door you like. The lesson text stays open beside the questions, and if you want to read a part of it again before you answer, do it with my blessing — that is what it is there for. And your speaking practice is here in the lesson too: hear me say each phrase, then say it back until it lands and you see the green check. Tonight, your only assignment in the world is one line, said once, out loud, to her: Buenas noches, mi amor. Lesson two will teach you the words for your people — Jillian, your kids, your dad — your family, in Spanish. Until then: gracias for my first class, Bill. Say it back to me one last time — gracias — and go say good night to your prometida. Buenas noches, mi amor. She is waiting to hear it.
