# Lesson 5 — Food and drink: ordering like you live there

Hola, Bill — hello, Bill. Soy la profesora Carmen Vega — I am Professor Carmen Vega, and welcome back to your Spanish room. Sit down, get comfortable, and bring your appetite, because today we eat. This is lesson five: food and drink, and ordering like you live there. Not ordering like a tourist who points at a picture and hopes. Ordering like a man who belongs at that table, who knows what he wants, who asks for it clearly, who enjoys it out loud, and who asks for the check like he has done it a hundred times — because after today, in your head, you will have.

Here is why this lesson matters, and it matters more than it looks. A man can greet people beautifully and still go hungry in a foreign language. Food is where a language stops being a classroom and starts being a life. You will sit in cafés in Key West during your wedding week. You will sit across from Jillian at breakfast the morning after you arrive, tired from traveling, happy, starving. You will want coffee. She will want coffee. And you, Bill, are going to be the man who orders it for both of you, in Spanish, calmly, without reaching for your phone. That is what today buys you. Every phrase in this lesson will be used by you, out loud, in a real place, within the year. I do not teach museum Spanish. I teach table Spanish.

Our method is the same as always, because it works. I say it, you say it back to me out loud, the way you mean it, and only then do we move on. When I say say it with me, I want to hear you — in the kitchen, in the truck, wherever you are sitting. Your mouth learns this language before your mind does, and today your mouth has delicious work to do. And remember how this room works: when the lesson ends, the questions wait below, all of them about what I teach you right here and nothing else, and the full text of this lesson stays open beside you. Reading it again is not cheating. Reading it again is learning.

We begin before the restaurant. We begin in your body, because that is where every meal begins. In English, you say I am hungry — as if hunger were something you are. Spanish is more honest and more practical: Spanish says you have hunger, the way you have a coat or a key, something you carry and can set down when the food arrives. So: Tengo hambre — I am hungry. Word for word, I have hunger. Tengo means I have. Hambre means hunger. Tengo hambre. Say it with me, and put a hand on your stomach like you mean it: Tengo hambre. Again, like a man who skipped lunch: Tengo hambre.

Hambre has a small sound lesson hiding in it, and you already own the key. Remember lesson one: the letter h in Spanish is silent — written, never heard. Hambre starts with that silent h, so hambre sounds like AHM-breh, no h sound at all. And tengo — listen to the ending, that warm -go sound. You will hear tengo again before this lesson is over, in another costume, so keep it in your pocket. Say the whole sentence once more, clean: Tengo hambre. Good. That sentence, said at the right moment, has started more good evenings than almost any sentence I know.

Now its brother. If hunger is what you have for food, thirst is what you have for drink, and Spanish builds it the exact same way — that is the kindness of this language, it reuses its own bricks. Tengo sed — I am thirsty. I have thirst. Sed means thirst. Tengo sed. Notice sed is a short word, one clean beat: sed. Say it with me: Tengo sed. And now put the brothers side by side, because in real life they travel together: Tengo hambre. Tengo sed. I am hungry. I am thirsty. One more time, both, like a man arriving at a table after a long hot drive: Tengo hambre. Tengo sed.

Hold onto the pattern, because this is a moment where Spanish shows you its machinery, and the machinery is friendly. Tengo plus a thing you feel in your body equals how you say you feel it. Tengo hambre, tengo sed. Later in your Spanish life this same frame will carry other feelings, but today two is plenty — hunger and thirst are the two that get you fed. So if tengo means I have, then hambre and sed are simply the names of what you have. You are not describing your soul. You are reporting your cargo. I have hunger. I have thirst. Honest, plain, and every waiter on earth understands it instantly.

Now, you are hungry, you are thirsty — what does a man do next? He wants. And wanting, in a restaurant, is not rude; wanting clearly is a courtesy, because it lets the other person help you fast. Here is the verb that runs every restaurant on earth: Quiero — I want. Quiero. It comes from querer — to want, and also to love; yes, Bill, it is the same family as te quiero — I love you — from lesson one. In Spanish, wanting and loving live in the same house, and at a table, quiero is simply the wanting branch of the family, doing honest work. Say it with me, firm and friendly: Quiero. Quiero.

Watch how quiero works, because it is a handle you can attach almost anything to. Quiero plus the thing. That is the whole machine. Quiero café — I want coffee — and we will get to café properly in a moment. The point right now is the shape: quiero, and then the thing you want, riding behind it like a cart behind a horse. You do not need a new sentence for every dish. You need one strong handle and the names of the things. Today I am going to give you the names of the things a man actually needs first: water, coffee, breakfast, chicken, rice — and the small phrases that turn a list into a meal ordered with style.

One softening note before we build, because I am teaching you to be a gentleman in two languages, not just a customer. Quiero is correct and it is normal — Spanish speakers say quiero at tables all day long and nobody blinks. But you already own por favor — please — from lesson one, and por favor rides beautifully at the end of a quiero sentence and turns a plain want into a polished order. Quiero café, por favor — I want coffee, please. Hear how the temperature changes. Same want, better manners. In this room we order like gentlemen: quiero, the thing, por favor. Say that whole shape once now, slowly: Quiero café, por favor.

First thing on the table, always, before anything else, in any country where the sun is hot: water. El agua — the water. Agua means water. And I know, your eye just caught something: el is the word for the that goes with words like café and pollo, the masculine team, and agua looks like it should belong to the other team — and mostly it does, but Spanish puts el in front of agua for the sound of it, because el agua rolls off the tongue and la agua trips on it. Do not fight it, do not analyze it tonight; just take the pair the way Spanish hands it to you: el agua. Say it with me: El agua. Again: El agua. Cold water on a hot day — you can almost hear the glass sweat.

So here is your first complete order, and I want you to feel how much of it you already owned before today: Quiero agua, por favor — I want water, please. Quiero, the handle. Agua, the thing. Por favor, the polish. Three pieces, one sentence, and a glass of water is on its way to you anywhere on earth where Spanish is spoken. Say it with me: Quiero agua, por favor. Once more, like it is ninety degrees and the drive was long: Quiero agua, por favor. Beautiful. You just ordered in Spanish. We are five minutes in and you are already not going thirsty in two languages.

But water has a detail, and details are where ordering like you live there is won or lost. Ice. In a lot of places, water arrives with ice unless you speak up, and some men — and some brides, after a long day of travel — want it without. Sin hielo — without ice. Sin means without. Hielo means ice. And there is our silent h again, working its invisible shift: hielo sounds like YEH-loh, the h nowhere to be heard. Sin hielo. Say it with me: Sin hielo. Now attach it where it lives, at the end of your order: Quiero agua sin hielo, por favor — I want water without ice, please. Say the whole long beautiful sentence: Quiero agua sin hielo, por favor. Again: Quiero agua sin hielo, por favor. Bill, listen to yourself. That is not a beginner's sentence. That is a man who knows exactly how he takes his water.

Second drink, and for many of us the most important one: El café — the coffee. Café means coffee, and it also means a café, the place — one word, the drink and the house it lives in, which tells you everything about how seriously the Spanish-speaking world takes coffee. El café. The accent mark sits on the e — café — and remember lesson one: the accent tells your voice where to lean. Kah-FEH, lean on the end. Say it with me: El café. Again, with the lean: El café.

Now order it, with your machine: Quiero café, por favor — I want coffee, please. Say it: Quiero café, por favor. And let me set the scene where you will say it first, because it is a good one. Key West, the morning of the wedding week. You and Jillian find a little café near the water. The air is warm already. She is looking at the boats. You are looking at her, and then you are looking at the waiter, and you say, like you have said it every morning of your life: Quiero café, por favor. And because you are who you are, you do not stop there — but ordering for her is its own skill, and I am saving it for its own moment later in this lesson. Patience, mi estudiante. First, your own cup.

Let me pause the drinks and put the first meal of the day on the table, because breakfast is where a traveling man lives or dies. El desayuno — breakfast. Desayuno means breakfast. It is a longer word, so let us walk it in pieces: des-ah-YOO-noh. El desayuno. The word has a story folded inside it, and stories make words stick: desayuno is built on the idea of breaking the fast — the night was a fast, you slept through it, and breakfast is the meal that breaks it. English breakfast says the exact same thing in its own bones: break, fast. Cousins again, Bill. These two languages are always shaking hands under the table. Say it with me: El desayuno. Again: El desayuno.

In real life, your machine handles desayuno the same way as everything else today: Quiero desayuno — I want breakfast — said at a small café where breakfast is simply what the kitchen is making. Hold the word itself today: el desayuno, breakfast, the fast-breaker, first meal, best meal when you are marrying the love of your life that week.

Now the heart of the menu, the plate that feeds half the world: El pollo — the chicken. Pollo means chicken. It sounds like POH-yoh — and hear that double l, the ll, doing its lesson-one trick, sounding like the y in yes. Pollo. Say it with me: El pollo. Again: El pollo. Pollo is the safest delicious word in the Spanish-speaking world: every kitchen has it, every grandmother has an opinion about it, and a man who can order pollo will never go hungry from Madrid to Key West to Mexico City.

And pollo does not travel alone. Its partner, on the same plate, in the same breath, all over the Caribbean and Latin America and Spain: El arroz — the rice. Arroz means rice. Arroz sounds like ah-ROHS, and it carries the double r's famous brother in its family — you hear a stronger roll of the tongue in the middle, ah-RROHS, that rolled rr we promised to tame together. Do not wrestle it today; let it roll a little and smile. Say it with me: El arroz. Again: El arroz. Pollo and arroz. Chicken and rice. Arroz con pollo — rice with chicken — con means with, and I am giving you that tiny word now because arroz con pollo is not just a phrase, it is a dish, a famous one, the plate that shows up at weddings and Sunday tables across the Spanish-speaking world. Arroz con pollo. Say it like a man who has eaten it and wants it again: Arroz con pollo.

Now build the order, because this is where your machine earns its keep. Quiero pollo, por favor — I want chicken, please. Quiero arroz, por favor — I want rice, please. And together, the full plate in one breath: Quiero arroz con pollo, por favor — I want rice with chicken, please. Say it with me, slowly first: Quiero… arroz… con pollo… por favor. And now at table speed, like the waiter is standing there with his pencil up: Quiero arroz con pollo, por favor. Again: Quiero arroz con pollo, por favor. You feel that? That is a dinner order, complete, polite, and clear, from a man five lessons into a language. Most tourists never get there in five years.

Now — the food arrives. It is hot, it smells like somebody's grandmother is in that kitchen, and you take the first bite. Spanish has a sentence for this moment, and using it is the difference between a customer and a guest, because it pays the cook in the only currency a cook truly wants. Está delicioso — it is delicious. Está means it is — the is for how something is right now, its condition in this moment. Delicioso means delicious. Está delicioso. Say it with me, and mean it at the plate: Está delicioso. Again, looking toward the kitchen the way a grateful man does: Está delicioso.

Delicioso is a word with music in it — deh-lee-see-OH-soh, five happy beats — and it deserves a little practice, because you are going to say it at real tables, to real cooks, and Jillian is going to hear you say it and watch what it does to people's faces. When a man compliments the food in the cook's own language, something changes at the table. You stop being a transaction. You become a guest they want back. Está delicioso. Say it once more, slower, savoring it the way you savor the food: Está delicioso. Good. And when she asks you how yours is, across the table in Key West, you now have the only review that matters: Está delicioso, mi amor — it is delicious, my love — and there is mi amor, my love, from lesson one, riding along for free because you already own it.

Now the skill I promised you, the one that turns a man ordering lunch into a man taking care of his woman in public: ordering for two. It starts with two small phrases that are really addresses — they tell the waiter where each plate and each glass belongs. Para mí — for me. Para means for. Mí means me. Para mí. Say it: Para mí. And its partner: Para ella — for her. Ella means she, her — the word for her. Para ella. Say it: Para ella. Para mí, para ella. For me, for her. Two little signposts, and now a waiter can build your whole table without a single point or gesture from you.

Watch the machine take a full table now. You are ordering drinks. Para mí, café — for me, coffee. Para ella, agua — for her, water. Hear how para mí and para ella stand at the front of each item like a name tag on a glass. And you can dress them with your other tools: Para ella, agua sin hielo, por favor — for her, water without ice, please. Because you know how she takes it, Bill. That is the whole point. Ordering for her, correctly, in detail, in another language, in front of her — that is not showing off. That is a man demonstrating, in public, that he pays attention to his woman. Say that sentence with me: Para ella, agua sin hielo, por favor. Again: Para ella, agua sin hielo, por favor.

And the same machine builds the food. Para mí, arroz con pollo — for me, rice with chicken. Para ella, pollo — for her, chicken. Or keep it simple and let one order cover the table. The shapes are yours now: para mí for your side of the table, para ella for hers, quiero when you are naming what you want, por favor keeping the whole thing warm. A table ordered this way runs smooth, and the woman across from you gets to just sit, and smile, and be taken care of. That is a gift you can give her in Spanish that costs you nothing but attention.

Every good meal has a last scene, and in a restaurant the last scene has its own line — the line that separates the man who is done from the man who is stuck at the table waving at the air. La cuenta, por favor — the check, please. Cuenta means the check, the bill, the account of the meal. La cuenta. Say it with me: La cuenta, por favor. Again, with the small friendly nod that goes with it everywhere on earth: La cuenta, por favor. You do not need to mime writing in the air. You have the words. The words are better.

And notice, Bill, la cuenta closes the circle of this lesson's manners. You arrived with tengo hambre. You ordered with quiero and por favor. You enjoyed with está delicioso. And you leave with la cuenta, por favor — please riding in its usual seat, keeping even the bill polite. A meal in Spanish, start to finish, has a shape now, and you own every joint of it. Before we play the whole scene at full speed, let me check your understanding the way I always do, with the machinery said out loud: tengo tells the table what you have — hunger, thirst. Quiero tells them what you want. Para mí and para ella tell them where it goes. Está delicioso tells them how it was. La cuenta tells them you are ready to settle up like a gentleman. Five moves. That is the whole game. Everything else is menu.

Now we play it. The full restaurant scene, both sides, and I will be the waiter first while you shadow your lines out loud — say them every time, do not just read them in your head, your mouth is the student here. The scene: a small restaurant in Key West, evening, wedding week. You and Jillian have walked in hungry. I, the waiter, greet you — buenas noches — good evening. You answer like a gentleman greeting the room: Buenas noches — good evening. I ask what you would like, and you begin the way a man begins, with the truth: Tengo hambre — I am hungry. Say it now, in the scene: Tengo hambre.

I bring the menus, I come back, pencil ready. Now you order, and you order for the table, because that is who you are. Drinks first. Say your line: Quiero agua sin hielo, por favor — I want water without ice, please. And for her — say it: Para ella, agua, por favor — for her, water, please. Then your coffee, because the day was long: Para mí, café — for me, coffee. Hear how para mí stepped in front that time — the signpost works at the front of any item, drink or dish. Good. The waiter nods. He has understood every word. So has she.

Food now. You have looked at the menu and the kitchen smells wonderful, so the decision is easy. Say it: Quiero arroz con pollo, por favor — I want rice with chicken, please. And for her, the same plate, because she trusts your nose: Para ella, arroz con pollo — for her, rice with chicken. The waiter repeats it back, you nod, and off he goes. And now the part tourists never rehearse: the waiting, the talking, the being on a date in another language — you already own te quiero — I love you — for exactly this weather, and a table in Key West at night is exactly its natural home. Use it if the moment rises. Spanish will hold it.

The plates arrive. Steam, color, that smell. You take the first bite, and you pay the cook. Say it toward the kitchen, and to her: Está delicioso — it is delicious. She tries hers, she nods, and you say it again because it is true twice: Está delicioso. The waiter passes, hears it, smiles — that smile is real, Bill, compliments to the kitchen travel fast in every language, and in the cook's own language they travel fastest. You eat. You talk. The evening does what evenings in Key West do. And when it is time — not rushed, never rushed, but time — you catch the waiter's eye and you close the scene like you opened it, with the words: La cuenta, por favor — the check, please. Say it now, final line of the scene: La cuenta, por favor.

That was the whole play, and you spoke every one of your lines. Now we run it once more, faster, with you leading and me only prompting, because a scene is not yours until you can run it without your profesora holding the flashlight. Ready? Evening, Key West, you walk in. Your greeting: Buenas noches. Your report: Tengo hambre. Your water: Quiero agua sin hielo, por favor. Hers: Para ella, agua, por favor. Your coffee: Para mí, café. The main plate, yours: Quiero arroz con pollo, por favor. Hers: Para ella, arroz con pollo. The verdict: Está delicioso. The close: La cuenta, por favor. Run it again in your own voice, all the way through, top to bottom, without stopping — I will be quiet and just listen. … Ay, Bill. That was a man ordering dinner in Spanish. Not reciting. Ordering.

One more room, the most important one: your own kitchen, at home, on an ordinary Tuesday — because ordering like you live there also means living there, and Jillian comes home from the hospital tired, and dinner is your department that night. You hear her car, and when she walks in you already know the report she would give: tengo hambre, tengo sed — she is hungry, she is thirsty — work like hers earns both. The arroz is on. The pollo is on. You put agua in front of her — sin hielo if that is how she takes it that day — and when she asks what smells so good, the menu is one line: Arroz con pollo. And when she tastes it and her eyes close a little, you do not need a compliment in English. You have watched a man feed his prometida — his fiancée — in two languages now, and the Spanish one is cooking in your own kitchen. That is not a lesson anymore. That is a life with Spanish in it.

A quick tune of the mouth before your solo, three small things. First, quiero: it sounds like KEE-eh-roh, and the middle is quick, a slide, not three heavy steps — quiero. Second, hambre and hielo both hide the silent h, so if you hear yourself breathing an h at the front of either word, drop it — Spanish h is a ghost, written and never heard. Third, delicioso: lean gently on the OH, deh-lee-see-OH-soh, the same lean the accent mark teaches in café. Small tunings. Your mouth is a good student; it keeps what we give it.

Now your solo — the guided try-it, and this time I am only the waiter, and a slightly distracted one, so you have to carry the scene. I greet you: Buenas noches, señor — good evening, sir. Answer me and tell me the truth about your stomach. … Tengo hambre — yes. I ask what you will drink, and I want the water the way you take it, the whole sentence. … Quiero agua sin hielo, por favor — there it is, complete, ice handled, manners on. And for the señora — for the lady? Order hers. … Para ella, agua, por favor — good, and add your coffee for yourself, with the signpost. … Para mí, café — perfect. Now the food, one plate, the famous one. … Quiero arroz con pollo, por favor. The kitchen sends it out, you taste it, and I am hovering — tell me how it is. … Está delicioso — and my whole kitchen thanks you. The night winds down, and you want to settle up like a gentleman. … La cuenta, por favor. Bill. Scene complete, waiter dismissed, profesora delighted. You just carried a full restaurant scene in Spanish with a distracted waiter and no phrasebook. Keep that in your chest for the days when this feels hard.

Let us gather the lesson in order, one last time, the way we will want it in your pocket. Tengo hambre — I am hungry; I have hunger. Tengo sed — I am thirsty. Quiero — I want, the handle that carries any order, with por favor — please — riding at the end to keep it gentlemanly. El agua — the water. Sin hielo — without ice. El café — the coffee. El desayuno — breakfast, the fast-breaker. El pollo — the chicken. El arroz — the rice. Arroz con pollo — rice with chicken, the famous plate. Está delicioso — it is delicious, the cook's pay. Para mí — for me. Para ella — for her. And la cuenta, por favor — the check, please, the gentlemanly close. Hunger to check, want to verdict — a whole meal, in Spanish, in order, yours.

That is lesson five, and I am proud of this one, Bill, because this is the lesson where your Spanish leaves the classroom and sits down at a table in the real world, across from the woman you love, in the town where you will marry her. Here is what happens next. Below this lesson are your questions — all of them about what I taught you here today: the hunger and the thirst, the quiero machine, the water without ice, the chicken and the rice, ordering for her, and the check. Nothing I did not teach, I promise you. Most give you choices; you can answer out loud or type, whichever door you like. The lesson text stays open right beside the questions, and going back to read is learning, not cheating — read with my blessing. And your speaking practice is here too: hear each phrase, say it back, until it lands and you see the green check. Your assignment in the real world is small and delicious: the next time you sit down anywhere — home counts — say tengo hambre out loud before you eat, and está delicioso after the first bite if it is true. Lesson six will teach you time itself — the days, the hours, and the sweet word mañana — tomorrow. Until then: buen provecho — enjoy your meal — that is the blessing Spanish says over food, and I say it over yours. Come hungry next time, Bill.
