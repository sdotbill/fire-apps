# Memory: short-term context and the long record

Welcome back. I am Professor Hanna Kowalczyk, and this is lesson three of Agents, AI That Acts: Memory, short term context and the long record. In lesson one we learned what an agent is, and in lesson two we studied its hands, the tools that let it reach your inbox, your calendar, your files, and the web. Today we study something quieter and, in the long run, just as important. We study what an agent remembers. By the end of this lesson you will understand the two great kinds of memory an agent works with, the short term context it holds while it is working with you, and the long record it can consult across days and months. You will understand why an agent sometimes seems to forget something you told it an hour ago, and why that forgetting is usually a design fact to work with, not a betrayal to be angry about. And you will learn how to keep the important things in the place where they will actually be found again, which is a skill that will serve you with Dave every single day. Let us begin.

Start with your own memory, because the comparison will carry us a long way if we handle it honestly. Right now, as you listen, you are holding the beginning of this lesson in mind while I speak the middle of it. You remember that I welcomed you, that I named the topic, and roughly what I promised. That holding is your short term memory at work, the small bright workspace where the present task lives. At the same time, you carry a vast long memory underneath it, your name, your company's history, the way a particular customer likes to be spoken to, things learned years ago and kept. You do not hold all of that in the bright workspace at once. You could not. You fetch from the long store when the present task calls for it, and the fetching feels so natural that you barely notice it happening. An agent's memory is built in the same two storeys, and nearly everything confusing about agent memory becomes clear the moment you see the two storeys separately and stop expecting one of them to do the other's job.

The first storey is context. Context, defined plainly for this class, means everything the model can see right now, in this working session, the conversation so far, the goal you gave, the results the tools just brought back, all of it sitting in the bright workspace together. When you tell Dave something in the morning and he uses it an hour later in a draft, that is context doing its work. The instruction is still in view, so it shapes the work. Context is fast, rich, and effortless, and it is also limited and temporary, and both halves of that sentence matter equally. Limited, because the workspace holds only so much. A very long conversation, a huge pile of tool results, an inbox with hundreds of messages read in one sitting, all of it competes for the same bright space, and what falls out of the space is, for the model, simply not there anymore. Temporary, because when a session ends, or a new task begins in a fresh session, the workspace is cleared the way a desk is cleared at night. The notes you left scattered on it do not automatically appear on tomorrow's desk. If you remember one sentence from this lesson, make it this one. Context is what the agent can see right now, and what it cannot see, it cannot use, however faithfully it was told. Most disappointments with agent memory are that sentence arriving in your life wearing a disguise.

Let me make the limit concrete with a picture from your own mornings. Suppose you spend a long stretch working with Dave on a complicated customer matter. Message by message, the context fills with the whole history of the matter, the customer's tone, the promise that was made, the number that was agreed, the thing that must never be said. The drafts Dave produces in that stretch are excellent, because everything relevant is in view. Now suppose the conversation runs very long, or you wander through six other topics on the way, or a new session starts. The number that was agreed quietly slides out of the bright workspace. The next draft comes back with a different number, or with the number left vague, and it feels like carelessness, or worse, like the agent never listened. It did listen. Listening was never the problem. Seeing is the problem, and seeing is governed by the workspace, not by good intentions. A wise user learns to re place the crucial fact into view at the moment it matters. When a draft truly depends on one number, one date, one promise, say it again in the instruction for that draft. It costs you one sentence. It protects you from the most common memory failure agents have. In this class we call that habit restating the load bearing fact, because like a load bearing wall, that fact is the one the whole structure rests on, and you do not leave load bearing things to chance or to memory, yours or the agent's.

The second storey is the long record. The long record means the stored information an agent can consult beyond the present session, kept in files, in your messages and documents, in notes written down deliberately, in the records of what was done and decided before. Where context is a desk, the long record is the filing cabinet and the archive room. It does not fade when a session ends. It can hold vastly more than any workspace. But, and this is the trade that defines it, nothing in the long record is automatically in view. It must be fetched, by search and reading, the very tools we studied in lesson two, and it can only be fetched well if it was stored well and if the agent knows to look. So the two storeys have opposite virtues and opposite weaknesses. Context is always in view but small and temporary. The long record is large and lasting but invisible until fetched. Nearly every practical question about agent memory is really a question about which storey a given fact should live in, and how it travels between the two at the right moment. Hold that framing, because we will use it for the rest of the lesson. Which storey does this fact belong in, and how will it travel into view when it is needed?

Watch how the two storeys cooperate in a piece of real work, because in good agent work they are constantly handing facts to each other. It is Monday morning. You ask Dave to prepare for the week. In context, Dave holds your goal for the session, the shape of the report you like, and the instructions you just gave. From the long record, Dave fetches. The calendar brings the week's commitments into view. The inbox brings the waiting messages. A note you wrote last month about a customer's preferences is searched, found, and read into the workspace, so the draft that follows respects a preference you stated once, weeks ago, and have not repeated since. That little journey, from archive to desk, from stored to seen, is memory working exactly as it should. And notice the human parallel, because it is exact. You do the same thing when you open your own notes before a meeting. The note did not live in your bright workspace all month. It lived in the record, and you fetched it at the moment of need. An agent is not worse at memory than you are. It is differently organised, and once you organise your shared work around its actual organisation, the forgetfulness that frustrates beginners mostly evaporates.

Now, a question of honesty that I will not let this lesson dodge. Does the agent learn, the way a person learns, growing permanently wiser from every conversation? The careful answer is, mostly, no, and you should build your habits on the careful answer rather than the flattering one. The model underneath was trained before it ever met you, and your Tuesday conversation does not quietly retrain it on Wednesday. What changes, what genuinely accumulates, is the long record. The notes get better. The files get richer. The written preferences, the saved decisions, the records of what worked, these grow, and an agent that consults them well will feel, from the outside, remarkably like it has learned you. In a practical sense it has, because the record is where your shared history actually lives. But the mechanism matters. If a preference lives only in a conversation that has ended, it is gone, however warm the conversation felt. If a preference is written into the record, stated plainly where the agent will search, it will shape work for years. So here is the rule I give every student, and I give it with love and emphasis. If it matters next month, write it where the record keeps things. Saying it once, in passing, in a chat, is how important things get lost with the very best intentions on both sides. Writing it down, once, clearly, is how they survive.

That rule deserves a worked example, and as always in this class, nobody should have to guess what good looks like. Suppose there is a way you want customer messages handled, a standing preference. Perhaps drafts to customers should always be held for your approval, never sent directly, however routine they seem. Perhaps one particular kind of request should always be brought to you personally, and another kind should never be promised without a calendar check. You could mention these things as they occur to you, scattered across weeks of conversation, and each time, in that day's context, Dave would honour them beautifully. And each time, the preference would live exactly as long as the context that held it. Or you could do the stronger thing. You could have the preferences written down, once, in one clear place in the record, in plain sentences, where Dave's searches will find them every time customer work begins. Then the preference stops depending on anyone's bright workspace. It becomes part of the furniture of the operation. That is the difference between an agent who seems to know you on good days and an operation that knows itself on all days. Build the second. The first is weather. The second is climate, and companies run on climate.

There is a second half to the honesty, and it concerns what the long record should not hold, or should hold with care. A record that keeps everything keeps nothing well. If every passing remark, every half formed idea, every superseded instruction is stored with equal weight and never tidied, then searches begin returning the old instruction alongside the new one, and the agent, reading both in good faith, cannot tell which one governs. This failure has a name in our class. We call it record drift, the slow accumulation of stale and contradictory material in the long record until the record itself becomes a source of error. You have met the human version. It is the shared folder where three versions of the same document live under nearly the same name, and the meeting grinds to a halt while everyone works out which version is real. The cure is the same in both worlds, and it is unglamorous. When a decision replaces an old decision, say so in the record, plainly, in the same place. When a preference changes, update the one clear statement rather than adding a second one somewhere else. Once in a while, have the record reviewed the way you would have a storeroom reviewed, what is current, what is superseded, what can go. An agent's memory is only as trustworthy as the housekeeping behind it, and housekeeping, I will remind you as often as needed, is not beneath anyone. It is the load bearing wall of every reliable operation.

Let me give you your guided moment now, a small audit you can perform in your head today and in reality this week. Think of three facts that Dave's work for you depends on. Perhaps how drafts should be handled. Perhaps a standing rule about money or promises. Perhaps the way a recurring report should be shaped. For each fact, ask the two storey question. Where does this fact live right now? If the honest answer is, in a conversation we had once, then that fact is living in the weather, and you have just found a small, valuable repair to make. Move it, or have it moved, into the record, in one plain sentence, in the place where it will be searched. If the answer is, in the record, in one clear statement, then that fact is load bearing and safe. And if the answer is, in the record in three places that do not quite agree, you have found record drift, and the repair is to make one statement govern and mark the others as superseded. Three facts, three honest answers, and you will understand your own agent's memory better than most professionals who work with agents daily. Do it gently, do it exactly, and notice how much steadier everything feels once the important things live where they can be found.

A word, too, about summaries, because summaries are the bridge the two storeys cross most often, and bridges deserve inspection. When a long piece of work ends, a good agent, or a good user, writes a short summary of what was decided and what remains open, and places that summary in the record. Later, instead of dragging the entire history into a new context, the summary travels. It is small enough to sit in the bright workspace and rich enough to steer the work. This is an honourable and necessary device, and your own life runs on it, every meeting note you have ever taken is exactly this bridge. But treat summaries with the respect a bridge deserves, which includes remembering what a bridge is not. A summary is not the record itself. It compresses, and compression loses things, usually the small qualifying sentence, the exception, the number's exact wording. So for load bearing facts, let the summary point to the full source, and when the stakes are high, fetch the source itself rather than trusting the bridge to carry more weight than a bridge should. Summaries for steering. Sources for deciding. If you hold that one distinction, you will use summaries well and never be betrayed by one.

Before we recap, let me answer the question students always ask at this point, because you may be forming it right now. If context is limited, why not simply make it unlimited, and let the agent see everything at once, always? It is a fair question and it deserves a fair answer rather than a shrug. Partly, the limit is a plain matter of cost and machinery, seeing more takes more, and work must stay practical. But partly, and more interestingly, an unlimited workspace would not even be desirable. Your own mind does not hold your whole life in view while you draft one reply, and it is better for not trying. A workspace stuffed with everything is a workspace where the important thing hides among ten thousand unimportant things, and judgment drowns in inventory. The art, for minds and for agents alike, is not seeing everything. It is seeing the right things at the moment of the work. Context small and well chosen, record large and well kept, and good fetching between them. That is not a compromise forced on us by weak machinery. That is, when you look at it steadily, simply what good memory has always been.

Let me show you the two storeys at work in one complete, ordinary job, because I find that a single honest walk through teaches more than three abstractions. Imagine it is the end of the month, and you ask Dave to gather what the company promised customers this month, so you can check that every promise is being kept. The job begins in context. Your instruction is in view, the shape of the list you want is in view, and anything you add about which customers matter most is in view. Then the fetching begins. Search reaches into the month's messages and finds the threads where promises were made. Reading opens the important ones fully, because a search result shows a glimpse and a promise lives in its exact wording. A note in the record, written by you in a calmer week, says that promises about dates always need the calendar checked, and that note, fetched at the right moment, changes the work for the better, because now each promised date is compared against the real calendar before it goes on your list. A summary from an earlier month, also in the record, gives the job its shape, the same columns, the same order, so this month's list can be compared with last month's at a glance. Now watch the return journey, because memory flows both ways in good work. When the job finishes, its results should not live only in this session's context, glowing briefly and then cleared away at night. The finished list, and any new promise discovered along the way, goes back into the record, written down where next month's job will find it. Context received from the record, worked, and returned to the record enriched. That round trip, repeated month after month, is how an operation builds a memory worth trusting. Nothing in it is exotic. It is fetching, working, and filing, done faithfully, and it is entirely within your power to arrange.

There is one more failure of memory I should name before we finish, because it is subtle and it causes a particular kind of argument between users and their agents. Sometimes the agent remembers, in the record, something you have genuinely forgotten you ever said, an instruction from months ago, given in a hurry, for a situation that has long passed. The agent, consulting the record in good faith, follows it, and you find yourself saying, I never said that, when the record shows, gently and exactly, that you did. I raise this not to score a point for the machine, but to prepare you for a moment that comes to everyone who works this way. The record will sometimes be a better witness to your past instructions than your own bright workspace is, just as your own written notes have occasionally corrected your memory of a meeting. Receive that correction the way you would want yours received, with grace. Check whether the old instruction still serves. If it does, be glad it was kept. If it does not, update it in the record, plainly, so the correction itself becomes part of the record, and the drift never starts. An operation where instructions are written, kept, and visibly updated is an operation that can argue with itself honestly and improve. An operation run on unwritten recollection can only argue. Choose the written kind, for Dave's work and for your own peace of mind.

One last practical picture, and it concerns the very beginning of a job, the moment where memory most often fails silently. When you start a new session with Dave on a matter that has a history, the new context begins nearly empty, however rich the record underneath it may be. The agent at that moment is like a excellent colleague returning from holiday to a well kept office. Everything is filed, nothing is in mind yet, and the quality of the first hour depends entirely on what gets fetched onto the desk. So help the fetching. Open the job by naming the history that matters. The customer matter from last month. The promise we made about Friday. The preference note about how these drafts should be held. Each thing you name is a signpost the search can follow, and signposted fetching is faster and far more reliable than hoping the agent guesses which corner of the archive this job lives in. Beginners often do the opposite. They open with the new request alone, assume the whole history rode along with it, and then feel let down by work that is competent but strangely unrooted, work that reads as if the company had been founded that morning. The repair is never to scold the agent. The repair is to open with the signposts. Name the matter, name the promise, name the note. Watch how different the work becomes when the desk is loaded before the drafting starts. You will feel the difference in the very first draft, and once you have felt it, you will never open a job bare again.

And since we are being complete, let me say what good memory feels like from the inside of a working week, so you know what you are building toward. It feels unremarkable, and that is the highest praise memory can earn. Dave's Monday report arrives shaped the way you asked for it to be shaped months ago, without you asking again. A draft respects the preference about approvals without a reminder. A promise made in a busy thread three weeks ago surfaces in this week's list before it can be missed, because it was written down where promises are kept and fetched when promises were reviewed. Nobody applauds. Nothing dramatic happens. The absence of small disasters is the sound of memory working. Contrast that with the other kind of week, where every job starts from zero, every preference must be re explained, every old promise must be rediscovered by luck, and you end the week tired in a way that has nothing to do with the volume of work and everything to do with carrying the whole memory of the operation in your own bright workspace, alone. That tiredness is a memory architecture problem, not a workload problem, and it is fixable, storey by storey, note by note, signpost by signpost. That is why this quiet lesson sits in the middle of our class. Tools get the glory. Memory does the keeping, and keeping is what lets a company compound instead of merely repeat.

So let me recap the lesson, in order. First, an agent's memory has two storeys, context, what the model can see right now in this session, and the long record, the stored information it can consult beyond the session. Second, context is always in view but small and temporary, while the long record is large and lasting but invisible until fetched by search and reading. Third, what the agent cannot see, it cannot use, so the load bearing fact, the one number or date or promise a job rests on, should be restated at the moment it matters. Fourth, the model does not quietly retrain itself on your conversations, what accumulates is the record, so if it matters next month, write it where the record keeps things. Fifth, a record that keeps everything with equal weight suffers record drift, stale and contradictory material that becomes a source of error, cured by making one clear statement govern. Sixth, summaries are the bridge between the storeys, used for steering, while the full source is fetched for deciding.

One more encouragement before you go, because I know this lesson asks you to change small habits, and small habits are easy to postpone. Do not try to rebuild the whole record in a weekend, and do not let the size of the archive excuse doing nothing. Start with the three facts from our guided moment. Move those three into the record, in plain sentences, in the places where they will be searched. That is an afternoon's light work at most, and it will repay you the very next week, in a draft that comes back right without a reminder and a promise that surfaces before it can slip. Then, job by job, let the same discipline spread. Memory, tended this way, is not a project with an end date. It is a standard, quietly kept, and standards kept quietly are what separate operations that compound from operations that merely repeat themselves at higher and higher effort.

What comes next is the questions on this lesson, and the full text stays open beside them, as always, because going back to read is learning, not cheating. Answer them at a steady pace, check any answer you are unsure of against the lesson text itself, and treat every correction as the record doing its kind work for you, the same way you will ask Dave's record to work for the company, faithfully, findably, and in plain sight, every working day from here on. You may find it fitting, in a lesson about memory, to test your own honestly rather than guess. In our next lesson we turn to planning in loops, act, observe, adjust, and we will watch how an agent breaks a goal into steps, watches what comes back, and corrects its course, which is where memory and tools join together into something that looks very much like competence. For now, take stock of your two storeys, be proud of how much clearer they are than they were an hour ago, and go to the questions when you are ready. I will see you in lesson four.
