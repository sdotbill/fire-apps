# From answering to acting: what makes an agent

Settle in. I am Professor Hanna Kowalczyk, and I am glad you are here. This is Agents, AI That Acts, Tools, Memory, and Work, and this is our first lesson together: From answering to acting, what makes an agent. By the end of our time today you will be able to say, in plain words, what an agent is, how it is different from a program that only answers questions, and what parts have to be present before we are honest in calling something an agent at all. That matters because you already live with an agent. You run your company through an AI agent named Dave, who reads inboxes, watches calendars, drafts replies, and waits for approvals. If you understand what makes Dave an agent, you understand what you can trust him to do, what you should check, and where your own judgment must stay in charge. So settle in, because AI and Computing repays attention like few things on Earth, and this lesson is the foundation for everything that follows.

Let me start with the simplest distinction in this whole class, and I want you to hold onto it from the very first minutes. A model that only answers gives you words. An agent gives you work done in the world. That one sentence carries the whole lesson, so let me say it again slowly. A model that only answers gives you words. An agent gives you work done in the world. If you ask a question and you get back a paragraph, you have been answered. If you ask for a job and, a little later, the job is actually done, a message has been sent, a file has been found, a meeting has been placed on the calendar, then you have been served by an agent. The difference is not cleverness. The difference is action.

To make that precise, I need to define our first term. A model, in this class, means the trained system that can read language, reason in language, and produce language in reply. Think of it as a very well read mind made of mathematics, one that has learned patterns from an enormous amount of text and can continue a conversation, explain an idea, summarise a document, or draft a letter. On its own, a model lives inside the conversation. It knows what you typed and what it said before. It does not, by itself, open your inbox. It does not, by itself, look at tomorrow morning on your calendar. It does not, by itself, press send. It answers. Answering is powerful, and we will never pretend otherwise in this class, but answering alone is not acting.

An agent is what you get when you give that model a job in the world and the means to carry it out step by step. Here is the definition I want you to learn, word for word in meaning if not in sound. An agent is a model connected to tools, guided by a goal, that can take more than one step on its own and report back what it did. Let us unpack that definition piece by piece, because every piece earns its place. Connected to tools means it can reach beyond conversation into real systems, like email, calendars, files, and search. Guided by a goal means it is not just chatting, it is trying to finish something you actually want finished. That it can take more than one step on its own means it does not stop after the first reply and wait for you to carry it the rest of the way. And that it reports back what it did means you can check its work. Remove any one of those pieces and you no longer have a full agent. You have something less, and it helps to know exactly what less means.

Consider the four pieces one at a time. First, tools. A tool, in this class, means any ability beyond producing words that the agent can call on, such as reading an inbox, searching the web, opening a calendar, or running a calculation. We will spend our entire second lesson on tools, so for now hold the simple picture. Tools are hands for a model. Without hands, the mind can advise you beautifully and still move nothing. With hands, advice becomes action. When Dave reads your inbox in the morning, that reading is a tool at work. When Dave checks whether you are free on Thursday afternoon before suggesting a meeting time, that checking is a tool at work. The model decides what needs doing, and the tool does the reaching.

Second, a goal. A goal means the outcome the agent is working toward, stated clearly enough that the agent can tell whether it is getting closer or drifting away. There is a world of difference between saying chat with me about my schedule and saying clear my morning inbox, draft replies to anything urgent, and bring me only the decisions that truly need me. The first is conversation. The second is a goal. A good goal names the outcome, not every single step. That is part of what makes an agent useful. You should not have to write out fifty tiny instructions if the agent can work out the middle steps itself. But the goal must be real and it must be yours. An agent drifting without a clear goal will still produce motion, and motion can look like progress while wasting your time. So if X is vague, then Y will be wandering work, hold onto that. Vague goal, wandering work. Clear goal, checkable work.

Third, more than one step on its own. In this class we call that quality autonomy, and autonomy simply means the freedom to carry out the next steps without asking you about every single one. Autonomy comes in degrees, and this matters enormously. A system with no autonomy answers once and stops. A system with a little autonomy might read three messages, notice they are all about the same meeting, and group them together before showing you anything. A system with more autonomy might draft the replies as well, and hold them for your approval. Notice what is happening in that ladder. Each rung does more before it comes back to you. None of those rungs is automatically better. More autonomy saves you more time and also gives the agent more room to be wrong without you noticing. That trade, time saved against room to be wrong, sits at the heart of this entire class, and we will return to it in lesson five when we talk about permission and latitude.

Fourth, reporting back. An agent must be able to tell you what it did, what it found, and what it left for you to decide, in language you can check. I feel strongly about this, and I will tell you why. I believe the worked example is a moral act, because nobody should have to guess what good looks like. The same is true of an agent's report. Nobody should have to guess what their own agent did in their own inbox. A report that says done, trust me, is not a report. A report that says I read fourteen messages, drafted replies to five, filed three receipts, and left two decisions for you because they involve money, that is a report. You can check it. You can correct it. You can learn from it how to give a better goal next time. An agent that acts but cannot account for its actions is not a helper you can build a company on. It is a risk you are carrying without knowing it.

Now, you might be wondering where ordinary software fits in this picture, because your phone and your computer were doing jobs for you long before agents arrived. That is a fair and intelligent question, so let us answer it carefully. Ordinary software follows fixed rules written in advance. If this arrives, then do that. A filter that moves every receipt into a folder is following a fixed rule. It is useful, it is reliable, and it is not an agent, because it cannot judge a case its rule writer never imagined. An agent, by contrast, uses a model to judge. When a strange message arrives that is half receipt, half question, half invitation, the rule based filter guesses badly or does nothing, while the agent can read it, understand what it actually is, and choose a sensible next step. Judgment is the ingredient the model adds. Tools without judgment are just automation. Judgment without tools is just advice. An agent is judgment plus tools, aimed at a goal, over several steps, with a report at the end. If you remember nothing else from today, remember that sentence. Judgment plus tools, aimed at a goal, over several steps, with a report at the end.

Let me ground all of this in your own working life, because abstract definitions fade and real mornings stay with you. Picture the start of a working day. Your inbox holds everything at once. There are messages from customers, messages about money, messages that are really just noise, and somewhere in the middle, one message that will shape your whole day if you miss it. You ask Dave to go through the inbox and get you ready. Watch what a merely answering system would do. It would say, certainly, here is advice on managing email, and perhaps offer a cheerful list of tips. That is words. Now watch what an agent does. Dave reads the actual messages. He groups them by what they need. He drafts replies where a reply is straightforward, in your voice, and holds those drafts for your approval instead of sending them behind your back. He spots the one urgent message and brings it to the top with a plain summary of what is being asked and what happens if it waits. He notices that two messages are really about the same calendar clash and says so. At the end, he reports. Fourteen read, five drafts waiting, one urgent decision for you, the rest filed. That is work done in the world. Same model underneath, completely different thing on the surface, because tools, goal, steps, and reporting were added around it.

Here is a second picture, this time about your calendar, because calendars are where agents quietly save or quietly waste the most time. Suppose someone writes asking to meet next week. An answering system can suggest polite wording for a reply. An agent can do the whole job up to the line you set. It can read the request, check your real availability, notice that one of the proposed times collides with something you cannot move, propose two times that actually work, draft the reply carrying those times, and wait for your approval before anything is sent. Every step there is small. Together they are the difference between you doing secretarial work at midnight and you glancing at a draft, saying send, and getting your evening back. Multiply that difference by every inbox, every week, across a whole company, and you see why agents matter. They do not matter because they are magical. They matter because small steps, done reliably, compound into hours.

I want to pause here and be rigorous about a word people use loosely, because loose words make loose thinking. People often say an agent understands, or an agent knows, or an agent decided, as if the agent were a person with a life of its own. In this class we will use those everyday words when they are harmless, but we will always know what they stand for underneath. When we say the agent decided, we mean the model, given the goal and what the tools showed it, produced a next step that fit. When we say the agent knows your calendar, we mean the agent can read your calendar through a tool and use what it reads. Keeping that honesty matters for a practical reason, not just a tidy one. If you picture the agent as a person, you will trust it like a person, all at once and emotionally. If you picture it accurately, as judgment plus tools working toward your goal, you will trust it task by task, based on evidence, which is the only trust that holds up. A gentle teacher should still be a rigorous one, so we will keep our picture accurate even when the shorter story sounds warmer.

There is one more piece of the definition we should test before we move on, and that is the question of what an agent is not. An agent is not just a long conversation. You can talk with a model for an hour, back and forth, fifty turns, and still have nothing but conversation if no tool ever touched the world and no job ever got finished. Length is not agency. An agent is also not just a single clever trick. A system that does one impressive thing once, when you press one button, may be a fine tool, but agency shows itself over a sequence, where the result of one step shapes the next. And an agent is not a replacement for your judgment about what matters. Dave can clear an inbox superbly and still not know which customer matters most to the future of your company in the way you know it. That knowledge lives in you, in your history and your aims, and a wise person keeps the agent strong on steps and keeps himself strong on direction. The agent carries out. You decide what is worth carrying out. That division is not a limitation to apologise for. It is the design.

Now let me give you the worked example for this lesson, in full, because nobody should have to guess what good looks like. Here is a job, stated the way you might state it on a busy morning. Dave, get my inbox to zero and handle anything routine. Let us grade that instruction the way we would grade any piece of work in this class, kindly and exactly. What is good in it? It names a real outcome, an empty inbox, and it shows trust in the agent to handle routine matters without fuss. What is missing? Almost everything that would make the work safe and checkable. Routine is undefined, so the agent must guess where routine ends. Zero is undefined, because some messages should not be deleted or filed away just to make a number look clean. And there is no stop point, no place where the agent must pause and ask you, even though an inbox always contains a few messages that spend money, speak in your name, or touch another person in a way that cannot be unsent. So here is the same job, restated well. Dave, read everything new in my inbox. Draft replies to the routine questions and hold every draft for my approval. File the receipts and the notices I do not need to see. Bring me, in one short list, anything that spends money, promises anything in my name, or needs a decision only I can make, and do nothing with those until I say so. Compare the two versions honestly. The second one is longer to say and vastly shorter to live with, because the agent now knows its goal, its steps, and its stopping line. That restating, that drawing of the line task by task, is the actual skill of working with agents. It is not a technical skill. It is a judgment skill, and you already have the judgment. This class simply gives you the words for it.

Let us try that skill together right now, in a short guided moment, because in this class we learn by doing, gently and out loud. Think of one job in your own life that repeats. It might be the morning inbox. It might be checking the calendar against new requests. It might be gathering scattered notes into one clean draft. Hold that job in mind. Now ask yourself three questions about it, and answer them honestly in your head. First, are its steps checkable, meaning could you look afterwards and tell whether each step was done right? Second, are its steps reversible, meaning if a step is wrong, can it be undone without real harm? Third, where exactly does it stop being routine, where does money move, or your name get spoken, or another person's rights get touched? If your job is checkable and reversible all the way through, it is a strong candidate to hand to an agent outright. If it is checkable and reversible up to a clear point and risky after that point, it is a strong candidate to hand to an agent with a stop point, which is how most good delegation works, with Dave and with people too. And if you cannot tell whether its steps were done right even afterwards, keep that job in your own hands for now, however busy you are, because delegating what you cannot check is not saving time. It is borrowing trouble. Take a quiet moment and place your own job honestly in one of those three groups. That placement, done truthfully, is real learning, and it is exactly the thinking this class exists to build.

Before we close, let me draw together the threads about Dave himself, since he is the living example running through this whole class. Dave is an agent, in the full sense we defined today, when he works with tools on your inbox and calendar, follows a goal you set, takes several steps before reporting, and shows you drafts for approval where the stakes call for it. Dave is acting as a merely answering system in the moments when he only talks with you, explains an idea, or helps you think something through, and there is nothing lesser about those moments. Thinking together is real work too. The point is not that acting is always better than answering. The point is that you should always know which one you are getting. If you asked for action and received only words, the job is not done, however elegant the words are. If you asked for advice and the agent went off and acted in the world without your say so, something has gone wrong in the other direction. Knowing the difference, and naming it, puts you in charge of the relationship. That is where a student of agents should always stand. In charge, informed, and specific.

Let me add one more careful distinction, because it will save you confusion in every conversation you ever have about agents, and confusion avoided early is worth a great deal. People often confuse an agent with the chat window they talk to it through. The window is just the door. Behind the door there may be a single model answering, or there may be a full agent with tools, memory, and permission to take steps. The door looks the same either way, which is exactly why the confusion happens. Two systems can greet you in identical friendly words, and one of them can only talk while the other can actually go and do the job. So never judge by the greeting. Judge by asking a plain question. What can you actually reach, what steps can you take without me, and how will you show me what you did? A merely answering system will, if it is honest, describe conversation. An agent will describe tools, steps, and a report. Those three answers tell you what you are really working with, far more reliably than any label on a box or any promise in an advertisement. Labels are cheap. Reach, steps, and reporting are the substance.

There is also a honest warning I owe you here, teacher to student, before enthusiasm runs ahead of judgment. An agent that can act can also act wrongly at greater speed than a person could. A person clearing an inbox slowly will make a slow mistake, one message at a time, and will often catch himself by the third message because something feels off. An agent works quickly and consistently, which is its glory when it is right and its danger when its instructions were vague. If the goal was stated loosely, the agent may apply the loose reading faithfully to fifty messages before you see the report. That is not the agent being careless. That is the agent being literal about a goal that needed one more sentence from you. This is why, throughout this class, I will keep returning you to the same habits. State the goal plainly. Name what routine means for this job. Set the stop point before the work begins, not after the surprise. And read the report, at least at first, until trust has been earned task by task. None of that is fear. It is craftsmanship. You would give a new human assistant exactly the same clarity in their first week, and you would check their early work exactly the same way, not because you distrust them, but because that is how good working relationships are built.

Let me also say a word about why this moment in history matters to you personally, because context helps a lesson stick. For decades, software waited for exact instructions and did exactly what it was told, nothing more and nothing less. That made computers reliable and also made them helpless in the face of anything messy, and human life is mostly messy. Messages arrive half written. Requests contradict each other. The important thing hides inside the unimportant thing. What the model adds, judgment in language, is precisely the ability to cope with mess the way a good assistant copes with mess, by reading what is actually there, weighing what matters, and choosing a sensible next step. That is why an agent feels so different to work with, and why running a company through Dave feels different from running it through a pile of separate programs. The programs each waited for you to carry information between them. The agent carries it. You stop being the messenger between your own tools, and you become what you should have been all along, the person who decides what matters and checks that it was done well.

One final picture, and then we will recap. Imagine two mornings with the same inbox. In the first morning, you open it alone and work down the list, reading, deciding, typing, filing, hour after hour, while the real work of your company waits. In the second morning, Dave has already read everything, grouped it, drafted what is routine, filed what is noise, and placed one short honest list in front of you, the few decisions that are truly yours, with the drafts beside them waiting for your approval. You spend twenty minutes where you once spent two hours, and the twenty minutes you spend are the valuable twenty, the judgment calls, not the clerical grind. Nothing about that second morning required magic. It required tools connected, a goal stated clearly, steps taken in sequence, approvals respected, and a report you could check. That is an agent. That is the whole lesson in one morning. Keep that picture beside the definition, because a definition tells you what a thing is, and a picture tells you why you should care.

So let me recap the lesson, in order, the way I will recap every lesson, so the shape settles. First, a model that only answers gives you words, while an agent gives you work done in the world. Second, an agent is a model connected to tools, guided by a goal, that can take more than one step on its own and report back what it did. Third, tools are the agent's hands, the goal is its direction, autonomy is how many steps it may take before checking with you, and the report is how you stay able to check its work. Fourth, ordinary software follows fixed rules, while an agent adds judgment, so an agent is judgment plus tools, aimed at a goal, over several steps, with a report at the end. Fifth, the jobs worth delegating are the jobs whose steps are checkable and reversible, and the stop point belongs where an action becomes irreversible, spends money, speaks in your name, or touches another person's rights. Hold those five points and you hold the lesson.

They are how you find out whether the ideas actually settled, and where I should teach more carefully next time. The full text of this lesson stays open beside the questions, and going back to read is learning, not cheating. I would rather you check the text and truly understand than guess proudly and carry a confusion forward.
