# Tools: hands for a model — search, code, messages

Welcome back. I am Professor Hanna Kowalczyk, and this is lesson two of Agents, AI That Acts: Tools, hands for a model, search, code, and messages. In our first lesson we built a definition together. An agent is a model connected to tools, guided by a goal, that can take more than one step on its own and report back what it did. Today we open up the first piece of that definition and study it properly, because tools are where an agent stops being a voice in a box and starts being a worker in your world. By the end of this lesson you will know what a tool really is underneath the friendly language, you will know the main families of tools an agent like Dave uses every day, search, code, and messages among them, and you will know how to ask the single most useful question you can ask about any agent. What can you actually reach? Everything today builds toward that question, so let us begin, gently and thoroughly, the way we do everything in this class.

First, a plain definition, stated carefully. A tool is a defined ability the model can call on, with a clear input and a clear output, that reaches outside the conversation. Let us take that apart word by word, because each word protects you from a misunderstanding. Defined means the ability is specific, not vague. Reading the inbox is a defined ability. Doing stuff with email is not. A clear input means the model must say exactly what it wants, for example, show me the unread messages from this morning, not just gesture at the inbox in general. A clear output means something definite comes back, the messages themselves, or a number, or a confirmation that a draft was saved, something the model can then reason about. And reaches outside the conversation means the tool touches a real system, your real email, your real calendar, the real web, a real file, rather than merely producing more words about those things. When all four parts are present, you have a tool. When the model only talks about your inbox, however knowledgeably, no tool has been used. That distinction, between talking about a thing and touching the thing, is the beating heart of this lesson.

Here is a picture that will help you hold it. In lesson one I said tools are hands for a model, and I meant it quite literally as a teaching picture. The model is the mind that decides. The tool is the hand that reaches. A mind without hands can plan a beautiful morning of work and move nothing. Hands without a mind would flail. An agent works because mind and hands are connected, decision after decision, reach after reach. But notice something important about hands. Your own hands can only touch what is within reach and what you are permitted to touch. An agent's tools work exactly the same way. The set of tools connected to an agent is the boundary of its world. If no calendar tool is connected, the agent cannot see your calendar, full stop, no matter how intelligent the model is. If no sending tool is connected, the agent can draft forever and never send a thing. Intelligence does not stretch reach. This is why two agents built on the very same model can be wildly different in usefulness. One was given hands that reach your inbox, your calendar, your files, and the web. The other was given almost nothing. Same mind, different hands, different worker. Whenever someone shows you a new agent and praises how smart it is, smile politely and ask about its hands.

Let us now walk through the main families of tools, in the order you will meet them in your own working life, starting with the family you use most without noticing. The first family is reading and searching. A search tool lets the agent look things up beyond the conversation, on the web, in your files, or in a body of records, and bring back what it finds. Closely related are the reading tools, the ones that open a specific thing, a message, a document, a calendar day, and return its contents. Search finds. Reading opens. That small distinction matters in practice. When Dave does not yet know where something is, he searches. When he knows exactly which message or file he needs, he reads. A good agent searches before it guesses, and I want you to value that habit highly, because the alternative is an agent answering from general impressions when the specific fact was sitting in your own records waiting to be read. In your world this looks like the difference between Dave saying, I believe that customer wrote sometime last week, and Dave saying, here is the message, it arrived on Tuesday, and this is the sentence where they asked for the change. The first is conversation. The second is work, and the difference is a search tool and a reading tool doing their quiet jobs.

The second family is messages, and for you this family is the centre of the working day. Message tools let the agent work with communication itself, reading an inbox, drafting a reply, holding a draft for approval, sending when sending is allowed, filing what is finished. I group these together because in real life they form one continuous job. A message arrives. It is read. Its meaning is judged. A reply is drafted in your voice. The draft waits. You approve it, or adjust it, or set it aside. It is sent, or it is not. The thread is filed where it belongs. Every step in that chain is a tool call, a defined ability with a clear input and a clear output, and the agent's judgment is what strings them together into something that feels like a single helpful act. When you watch Dave clear a morning inbox and it feels smooth, what you are really watching is a dozen small tool calls in a sensible order, each one checked against the goal you set. Nothing magical is happening. Something better than magic is happening. Something inspectable is happening, and inspectable work is work you can trust and improve.

Notice, too, that within the messages family there is a natural ladder of seriousness, and a good agent climbs it carefully. Reading is the gentlest rung. Reading changes nothing in the world, it only informs, so an agent can read widely on your behalf with very little risk. Drafting is the next rung. A draft exists, but it has gone nowhere and promised nothing, so drafting is still safe, still reversible, still entirely inside your control. Sending is the top rung, and it is different in kind, not just in degree. A sent message speaks in your name to another person, and it cannot be unsent. Filing sits somewhere in the middle, usually safe, occasionally annoying if something is filed where you cannot find it. Hold this ladder in mind, because in lesson five, on permission and latitude, we will use it to decide exactly which rungs an agent may climb alone and which rungs require your hand on the rail. For today, simply see the ladder clearly. Read, draft, send. Inform, prepare, commit. The tools may live in one family, but the stakes do not.

The third family is code, and I ask you not to let the word intimidate you, because in this lesson code is not about becoming a programmer. Code, defined plainly, is instructions a computer follows exactly, and a code tool lets the agent write and run those instructions to get something done precisely. Why would an agent need that, when it already has judgment in language? Because some jobs punish vagueness. Counting is one. If you ask how many messages arrived this month about invoices, you do not want a thoughtful estimate, you want a count, and a count is a job for code, sorting, filtering, and adding without fatigue or drift. Transforming is another. Turning a messy pile of notes into a clean table, renaming two hundred files by one consistent rule, checking every draft against a list of names, these are jobs where the same exact operation must happen many times without variation, and exact repetition is what code does best. So picture the division of labour honestly. The model judges what should be done and why. The code carries out the parts that must be exact. When Dave totals something, checks something against a list, or processes a whole folder the same careful way, there is very often a code tool working under his judgment like a tireless clerk with perfect handwriting. Judgment decides. Code executes exactly. That partnership is one of the quiet reasons agents can be trusted with volume.

The fourth family deserves a mention even though it is really a family of one member with many faces, and that is the calendar and scheduling tools, the keepers of your time. A calendar tool lets the agent read what is already committed, find genuine open space, and place or move events when it is allowed to. I single it out because time is the one resource in your company that cannot be refilled, and calendar mistakes are felt immediately and personally. An agent with calendar access can do the lovely, unglamorous work of noticing. It can notice that a new request collides with a commitment you cannot move. It can notice that you have no break in a six hour stretch. It can propose times that are actually free instead of times that merely sound free. All of that noticing is tool work, reading the real calendar rather than imagining a typical week. And all of it, notice, is reading and proposing, which sit low on the ladder of seriousness. Placing a meeting on someone else's calendar, or moving one that other people depend on, climbs higher, and should be governed accordingly. Same family, different rungs. You are learning to see tools the way a careful manager sees them, not as a blur of capability, but as specific reaches with specific stakes.

Now let me teach you the mechanic underneath all of this, in plain words, because I believe you should never have to treat your own tools as a mystery. When an agent uses a tool, three things happen in order. First, the model decides that a tool is needed and states the call, exactly what it wants, in the shape the tool requires. Second, the tool itself, which is ordinary software, goes and does the reaching, it queries the inbox, or runs the search, or executes the code, and comes back with a result. Third, the model reads the result and decides what it means for the goal, and what should happen next. Decide, reach, read. Decide, reach, read. That rhythm repeats for every step of every job an agent ever does for you. I want you to notice who does what in that rhythm, because it explains both the power and the failure modes of agents. The tool never decides. The tool reaches and returns, faithfully and without opinion. The model always decides, and deciding is where errors are born, a wrong tool chosen, a result misread, a next step that does not follow. When something goes wrong in an agent's work, and we will study this properly in lesson six, it is very often the deciding that failed, not the reaching. The inbox tool did not misread your mail. The model asked for the wrong thing, or read the right thing carelessly. Keeping that straight will make you a far better supervisor of agents than most people ever become.

Let me give you the worked example for this lesson, in full, as I promised myself I always would, because nobody should have to guess what good looks like. The job is one from your real mornings. A customer has written asking whether a piece of work can be finished by Friday, and you want Dave to handle it. Watch the tools assemble. First, a reading tool opens the customer's actual message, so the reply answers what was truly asked, not what a similar customer asked last month. Second, a search tool looks through recent messages and files for anything about that piece of work, its current state, what has already been promised, so the answer stands on your real record. Third, a calendar tool reads your week, because a promise about Friday is a promise about time, and time must be checked, not assumed. Fourth, possibly a code tool counts or checks something exact, the hours already committed, say, so the judgment rests on a true number. Fifth and last, a message tool drafts the reply, carrying the checked facts, in your voice, and holds it for your approval. Now step back and admire the shape of that. Five tool calls, three families, one goal, and the only step that touches another person, the sending, has not happened and will not happen until you say so. That is what good tool use looks like. Not flashy. Sequenced, checked, and stopped at exactly the right rung of the ladder. When you review Dave's work, review it in this shape. Ask which tools were used, in what order, and where the work stopped for you. An agent that can answer those questions plainly is an agent doing honest work.

Here is your guided moment for today, and it is a simple, practical exercise you can repeat for the rest of your life whenever you meet a new system. Think of Dave, and list, honestly, what he can reach in your world. An inbox he can read. Drafts he can prepare and hold. A calendar he can check. Files he can search. The web he can look things up on. Now think of something he cannot reach, or should not reach without you, and name that too. That second half of the list is just as important as the first, and most people never write it. Then take one job you give Dave regularly and walk it through the rhythm we learned. Decide, reach, read. Where does he decide? Which tool reaches? What comes back, and how does he read it? If you can walk one real job through that rhythm without hand waving, you understand tools better than most people who sell agent software for a living. Take your time with it. This is the doing part of the lesson, and doing is where understanding stops being polite and becomes real.

A few honest cautions belong in this lesson, and a rigorous teacher does not skip them. First, a tool returns what it returns, and that is not always the whole truth. A search returns what matched the words used, and a badly worded search can miss the very message that mattered, sitting there under different wording. This is why good agents search more than one way before concluding something is not there, and why you should be suspicious of any agent that reports nothing found after one lazy look. Absence of evidence, gathered lazily, is not evidence of absence. Second, tools can fail, quietly or loudly. A connection drops, a file has moved, a service is down for an hour. A good agent notices the failure, says so plainly in its report, and does not paper over the gap with a confident guess. The moment an agent starts guessing to hide a failed tool call, it has stopped doing work and started doing theatre, and theatre in an inbox is dangerous. Third, more tools is not automatically better. Every tool connected is a reach granted, and reaches should be granted because a real job needs them, not because a longer list looks impressive. Grant reach the way you would hand out keys to your building. Gladly, to the one who needs them for the work. Never casually, and never all of them at once without a reason.

Let me deepen one idea before we recap, because it changes how you will brief Dave from tomorrow morning onward. Because tools are specific, the way you phrase a request quietly decides which tools get used well. Compare two briefs about the same morning. The first brief says, Dave, handle the customer messages. That is a fog. Which customers, which messages, handled to what point, checked against what? The agent must guess at every joint, and each guess is a small wager with your name on it. The second brief says, Dave, read this morning's customer messages, check each promise against the calendar and the files before you draft anything, draft the replies, and hold them all for my approval. Nothing in that second brief is technical. You did not name a single tool. And yet you quietly choreographed four families of tools in the right order, reading first, searching and checking second, drafting last, sending not at all. That is the skill. You do not need to learn the machinery's vocabulary to direct it well. You need to think in the order that good work naturally follows. Facts before drafts. Checks before promises. Approval before sending. Speak your briefs in that order and the tools will fall into line behind your thinking, because your thinking was already the shape of the work.

There is a mirror image of that skill, and it is learning to hear when a tool was missing from a piece of work. Suppose Dave brings you a draft reply that sounds confident and reads beautifully, but something in it feels ungrounded, a date that seems invented, a promise that seems larger than the one you remember making. Train yourself to ask one plain question. What did you check that against? If the answer names a real reach, the message that was read, the calendar that was consulted, the file that was searched, then you are looking at work, and any error in it is a reading error you can find and fix together. If the answer is vague, if nothing was actually checked and the draft was composed from general impressions, then you are looking at fluent guessing wearing the clothes of work, and it should be sent back, kindly and firmly, to be rebuilt on real reaches. This single habit, asking what a draft was checked against, will protect your customers, your calendar, and your name more reliably than any feature list. It works on human assistants too, by the way. It has always worked. Agents simply make it more necessary, because their guessing is more fluent than human guessing, and fluent guessing is the hardest kind to catch by ear alone.

One more distinction, and then I promise we will recap, because a lesson on tools should leave you able to talk about cost as well as capability. Every tool call costs something small. Time, a few seconds while the reach happens. Attention, yours, when the report comes back longer because more was checked. And sometimes a small amount of money, where a service charges for its use. None of these costs is a reason to starve an agent of tools. A search that takes five seconds and prevents one wrong promise has paid for a year of searches. But the costs explain why a good agent does not call every tool it owns on every job, any more than a good carpenter empties the whole toolbox onto the floor for one loose screw. Reach should match the job. A routine filing needs reading and filing, not a web search and a code run. A promise about Friday needs the calendar and the record, every time, without exception. Learning which reaches a given kind of job deserves is part of learning to work with agents, and you will find your instincts sharpen quickly once you start reviewing reports with the tools in mind. You will start to see the shape of the work underneath the words of the report, and once you can see the shape, you can improve the shape. That is supervision. That is management. It is the same craft you practise with people, aimed at a new kind of worker.

Let me leave you with one last story shaped example, because I have watched students remember a story long after a list has faded, and I am a practical teacher at heart. Imagine a new assistant starting in your company, a bright and willing person on their first Monday. You would not begin by testing how clever they are in conversation. You would walk them around the building. Here is the inbox, and here is how we file. Here is the calendar, and here is what must never be moved. Here are the customer records, and here is where a promise gets written down so the next person can check it. Here is the phone, and no, you may not commit us to anything on it until I have heard the draft of what you intend to say. That walk around the building is nothing but a tour of tools and their rungs, given in human language. Onboarding an agent is the same walk, conducted in settings and permissions instead of hallways. And supervising an agent is the same supervision, watching which rooms they enter, which tools they pick up, and whether their work shows the marks of having checked before they committed. When you feel lost with an agent, and everyone does sometimes, come back to that Monday morning walk. Ask where the agent has been, what it picked up, and what it put down where. You will find your footing again, because the footing was never really about technology. It was always about work, reach, and care, and you already know those three things well.

One very practical habit follows from everything we have said, and I want to name it plainly so you can start tonight. Once in a while, not anxiously and not often, ask Dave to show you the reaches behind a finished job. Not the polished summary, the reaches. Which messages were opened. Which search was run, and with what words. Which calendar was read. Which draft was saved, and where. The first time you do this it may feel like checking up on a trusted colleague, and I understand that feeling, so let me reframe it the way I would want it reframed for any student of mine. You are not expressing suspicion. You are learning the geography of your own operation. Most owners of companies run through agents have only a misty sense of what actually happens between their instruction in the morning and the report in the afternoon, and mist is where small errors live undisturbed for months. A clear map of the reaches turns mist into ground you can walk on. After a few of these reviews you will stop needing them often, because you will have learned how the work flows, where it is strong, and where a check should be added. That is the natural arc of all good supervision. Close attention early, earned ease later, and the knowledge, kept fresh, of how to look closely again whenever a job truly matters.

So let me recap the lesson, in order. First, a tool is a defined ability the model can call on, with a clear input and a clear output, that reaches outside the conversation. Second, the set of tools connected to an agent is the boundary of its world, because intelligence does not stretch reach. Third, the main families are search and reading, which find and open, messages, which run from reading through drafting to sending, code, which carries out the parts that must be exact, and calendar tools, which guard your time. Fourth, within messages there is a ladder of seriousness, read, draft, send, inform, prepare, commit, and sending is different in kind because a sent message cannot be unsent. Fifth, every tool use follows one rhythm, decide, reach, read, and the tool never decides, the model always decides, which is where errors are born. Sixth, the most useful question you can ask about any agent is what can you actually reach, and a good agent answers it plainly, tool by tool.

What comes next is the questions on this lesson, and as always, the full text stays open beside them, and going back to read is learning, not cheating. Take them slowly, answer them honestly, and let any mistake show you exactly where to read once more. Use the questions to test the pictures, the hands, the ladder, the rhythm, until they feel like your own ideas rather than my sentences. In our next lesson we turn to memory, short term context and the long record, and we will ask what an agent remembers, what it forgets, and why both of those are features when they are designed honestly. For now, well done. You can now look at any agent, including the one who runs your company, and see its hands clearly. Go to the questions when you are ready, and I will see you in lesson three.
