# Planning in loops: act, observe, adjust

Welcome back. I am Professor Hanna Kowalczyk, and this is lesson four of Agents, AI That Acts: Planning in loops, act, observe, adjust. We have built our agent piece by piece. Lesson one gave us the definition, a model connected to tools, guided by a goal, taking more than one step on its own and reporting back. Lesson two studied the tools, the hands. Lesson three studied memory, the context in view and the long record underneath. Today we study the motion itself, the way an agent actually gets a real job done when the job is too big, too messy, or too uncertain for a single step. By the end of this lesson you will understand what a plan is for an agent, what a loop is, why the loop matters more than the plan, and how to recognise the difference between an agent that is genuinely adjusting and one that is merely repeating itself with confidence. This is the lesson where tools and memory join together into something that looks very much like competence, so let us waste no time.

Begin with a failure we have all watched, in people and in machines alike. Someone is given a complicated job. They think hard at the start, write out a complete sequence of steps in advance, and then march through the sequence exactly as written, even as the world keeps handing them information that the sequence never anticipated. The customer has already replied. The file has moved. The meeting was rescheduled by someone else an hour ago. The march continues anyway, because the plan said so, and the result is work that is orderly, confident, and wrong. I call that pattern the straight line march, and I want you to be able to spot it everywhere after today, because the alternative to it is the subject of this lesson. The alternative is the loop. An agent that works in loops does not pretend the world will hold still for its plan. It takes a step, looks at what the step actually produced, compares that with the goal, and lets what it saw change what it does next. Act, observe, adjust. Act, observe, adjust. That humble rhythm, repeated faithfully, is how complicated work gets done in a world that refuses to be predicted in detail.

Let us define our terms with the usual care. A plan, in this class, means an ordered set of intended steps toward a goal, held lightly enough to be changed by evidence. Every phrase there is working. Ordered, because sequence matters, you cannot draft the reply before you have read the message. Intended, because the plan describes what the agent means to do, not what the world will permit. And held lightly enough to be changed by evidence, because a plan that cannot be changed by what the tools bring back is not a plan at all, it is a script for a play the world has not agreed to perform. A loop means one full turn of acting, observing what came back, and adjusting the next step in light of it. And a step, the smallest term, means one decide, reach, read cycle of the kind we studied in lesson two, one tool used for one purpose inside the larger job. Plans are made of steps. Loops are what happen when steps are allowed to learn from each other. Hold those three definitions together and the rest of the lesson is commentary, useful commentary, I hope, but commentary all the same.

Now let me slow down inside a single loop turn, because each of its three beats does distinct work and beginners tend to blur them together. The first beat is act. The agent chooses the next step that best serves the goal given everything currently in view, and it carries that step out through a tool. Notice the restraint hidden in that sentence. The agent does not choose all the remaining steps. It chooses the next one. That restraint is wisdom, because the next step is the only one whose conditions the agent actually knows. The second beat is observe. The agent reads what the tool brought back, really reads it, the actual message, the actual calendar, the actual result, including the parts that are inconvenient for the plan it had in mind. Observing is a discipline, not a formality. An agent that skims its own results, or reads only the part that confirms its intention, has broken the loop at its most important joint, and everything downstream will be built on a world that does not exist. The third beat is adjust. The agent compares what it observed with the goal and changes something, the next step, the order of steps, sometimes the understanding of the goal itself, before acting again. If nothing is allowed to change in the adjust beat, there is no loop, whatever the marketing says. There is only a march with pauses. So if you ever want to test whether a system truly works in loops, ask it one question. Show me a place where what you found changed what you did next. A looping agent can answer that question with examples all day. A marching system will change the subject.

Here is the loop at work in your own world, in a job you will recognise. Suppose you ask Dave to get a overdue piece of work chased up, the customers involved contacted, and the situation summarised for you by the end of the day. Watch the turns. First turn, Dave acts by searching the record for what was promised and when. He observes that the promise exists, but also that one part of it was already renegotiated in a later message, so the overdue amount is smaller than the original note suggested. He adjusts, the chase list shrinks, and one customer comes off it entirely. Second turn, Dave acts by reading the relevant threads fully. He observes that one customer has already replied with a question that is really the blocker, the delay is not neglect, it is an unanswered question sitting in your inbox. He adjusts again, that customer no longer needs a chase, they need an answer, and the draft changes from a reminder to a reply. Third turn, Dave drafts the remaining messages, observes against the calendar that one promised call cannot happen on the day the draft proposes, and adjusts the draft's wording before it ever reaches you. By the time the summary lands in front of you, the job you asked for has quietly become the job that was actually needed, and the difference between those two jobs is exactly the value of the loops. A marching system would have sent three identical chases, one of them to a customer who had already settled, one to a customer waiting on you, and one promising a call on an impossible day. Same tools. Same model. The loop is the difference between diligence and embarrassment.

I want to draw out a principle hiding inside that example, because it generalises to nearly all agent work. The goal stays fixed while the plan stays fluid. Read that sentence again, because it is the management secret of this whole class in nine words. The goal stays fixed while the plan stays fluid. Your goal in the example never moved. Get the situation chased, honestly, and summarised by the end of the day. Everything underneath it, who needed chasing, what the messages should say, which promises were still real, was allowed to move as evidence arrived. When users get frustrated with agents, it is very often because the two have been swapped in practice. The plan has been treated as fixed, every step defended long after the evidence turned, while the goal has quietly drifted, the job slowly becoming about finishing the steps rather than achieving the outcome. Guard the goal. Release the plan. An agent briefed that way, and supervised that way, will surprise you pleasantly and often. An agent briefed the other way will complete procedures while the purpose leaks out of the work unnoticed.

A word now about the size of steps, because loop craft is largely step craft. Steps should be small enough to observe cleanly. If a step bundles five reaches together and something in the result looks wrong, neither you nor the agent can tell which reach went astray, and the adjust beat has nothing solid to work with. Small steps make observation sharp and adjustment cheap. But steps can also be too small, and a rigorous teacher admits both edges of the blade. An agent that stops to reconsider after every trivial reach will take all day over a morning's inbox, spending your time and your patience on ceremony. The craft is in matching the step to the stakes and the uncertainty. Where the ground is familiar and the action reversible, filing routine notices, grouping messages, let the steps run larger and the loop turn quickly, with observation riding along in the report. Where the ground is uncertain or the action hard to reverse, anything touching money, promises, or another person's feelings, shrink the steps, observe each one, and let the loop turn slowly and deliberately. Fast loops on safe ground. Slow loops on serious ground. That one habit, practised consistently, is most of what experienced agent supervisors actually do all day, beneath all the vocabulary.

There is a special turn of the loop I must teach you explicitly, because it is the turn that separates trustworthy agents from merely busy ones. Sometimes, in the observe beat, the agent discovers that the job cannot proceed as briefed. The information needed is not in the record. Two instructions in the record contradict each other. The next step would cross the stop point we discussed in lesson one, into money, or a promise, or someone else's rights. At that moment the correct adjustment is not a cleverer step. The correct adjustment is to stop the loop and bring the problem to you, clearly, with what was found and what is needed. I call this breaking the loop upward, and I want you to honour it whenever you see it, because an agent that breaks the loop upward is showing you the exact virtue you most need in a worker who acts in your name. The dangerous agent is never the one that stops and asks. The dangerous agent is the one that treats every obstacle as a puzzle to be solved silently, guessing its way past contradictions and missing facts so the loop never has to trouble you. Smoothness is not the goal. Truth is the goal, and sometimes the truth is that the loop has reached the edge of what it should do alone. When Dave stops mid job and says, plainly, here is the contradiction I found, here is the decision I need, that is not the system failing. That is the system working at its very best, and your part of the craft is to receive it that way, answer it cleanly, and let the loop resume with better ground under it.

Let me give you the worked example for this lesson, in full, because nobody should have to guess what good looks like. The job is preparing your week on a Monday morning, a job with enough moving parts to show the loop honestly. The goal, stated and fixed, is this. By the time you sit down, you want to know what the week actually holds, which commitments are at risk, and what needs a decision from you, with drafts ready for anything routine. Now watch a good first plan form, steps in order. Read the calendar for the week. Read the new inbox messages. Match messages against commitments. Draft replies for the routine matters. Summarise the risks and decisions. A perfectly sensible plan, and if the world were tidy, the march would serve. But the world is your world, so watch the loops turn instead. Reading the calendar, Dave observes that two commitments overlap on Wednesday, a conflict no message has mentioned yet. Adjustment, a new step enters the plan, check which of the two can move, before any replies are drafted, because replies written in ignorance of that clash would promise the same hour twice. Reading the inbox, Dave observes that a customer has already answered the question one of the planned drafts was going to ask. Adjustment, that draft dies quietly, replaced by a reply to what the customer actually said. Matching messages against commitments, Dave observes that a deadline in a message contradicts the deadline in the record, and neither can be verified from the other. Adjustment of the honourable kind, the loop breaks upward, the contradiction comes to you in the summary flagged as a decision you must make, rather than being guessed away. End state, the summary you receive is not the summary the original plan would have produced. It is better in three specific, nameable ways, and each improvement can be traced to an observe beat that was taken seriously and an adjust beat that was allowed to change something. That traceability is the signature of real looping. When you review Dave's work, ask to see it. Where did the plan change, and what evidence changed it? The answers will teach you more about your own operation than the finished work itself.

Your guided moment today is a reviewing exercise, and I think you will enjoy its honesty. Take one job Dave has done for you recently, any job with a report attached. Read the report once through, the way you normally would. Then read it a second time with three questions in hand. First, where did the agent act, what steps were actually taken, in what order? Second, where did it observe, what did the tools bring back that the report shows it truly read? Third, and most revealing, where did it adjust, what did it do differently because of something it found? If you can find all three beats, you are looking at looped work, and you can trust its shape. If the report shows acting from beginning to end with no observation and no adjustment anywhere, treat it gently but clearly for what it is, a march, however polished its language, and consider whether the job deserved a loop, smaller steps, and a report that shows its working. Do not be discouraged if the first report you examine this way looks thin. You are not grading Dave. You are learning to see, and seeing, once learned, improves every brief you write from then on, because you will start briefing for the loop, naming what should be checked, what evidence should change the plan, and where the loop must break upward to you.

Before we recap, one caution about loops that run long, because every strength casts a shadow and this one's shadow has a name in our class. We call it looping without landing, the failure where an agent turns the loop faithfully, act, observe, adjust, act, observe, adjust, and yet never arrives, because each observation spawns two new considerations and the goal recedes like a shoreline. The work expands. The report grows. The day ends with motion everywhere and delivery nowhere. The cure is not fewer loops. The cure is a goal stated with a finish line the agent can recognise, and a report that must answer, at the end, the plain question, is it done, and if not, what exactly remains? Loops serve landings. A loop that never lands is not diligence, it is drift wearing diligence's clothes, and a good supervisor learns to ask the landing question early and cheerfully, not as an accusation but as a compass. Done is a beautiful word. Teach your agent, by your briefs and your questions, that done is the point of every loop it will ever turn for you.

Let me add one more layer before we recap, because there is a question thoughtful students always ask at this point, and it deserves a proper answer. How does the agent decide what to observe, when a single tool result can contain a hundred details and no worker, human or otherwise, can weigh them all? The honest answer is that the goal does the choosing. Observation is not reading everything with equal care. Observation is reading with the goal in hand, alert for the details that bear on it. A message observed in the service of clearing an inbox is read for what it needs and what it changes. The same message observed in the service of preparing a difficult negotiation would be read for tone, for history, for what is carefully not being said. Same message, different observation, because the goal held the lamp. This is why the fixed goal matters so much more than beginners expect. It is not merely a destination. It is the instrument by which the agent sees. A vague goal does not just produce wandering work, as we said in lesson one. It produces blind observation, results read without a lamp, surprises missed that were sitting in plain sight inside the very messages the agent touched. When you brief a job, then, you are doing something more generous and more powerful than giving orders. You are handing the agent its eyes. Brief the goal as if someone you trust will see the whole job through it, because someone you rely on will.

And one more distinction, closely related, which will sharpen how you read every report from now on. There is a difference between an agent adjusting its steps and an agent adjusting its understanding, and the second is the deeper and more valuable of the two. Adjusting steps is rearranging the sequence, adding a check, dropping a draft. Adjusting understanding is revising what the job actually is. The overdue chase that became an unanswered question was not a step adjustment. It was the agent discovering that the situation itself had been misdescribed by the original brief, through nobody's fault, because the brief was written from the record and the record was incomplete. Mature agent work revises the description of the job when the evidence demands it, and then, crucially, tells you it did so, in the report, in plain words, so your own understanding gets revised alongside the agent's. When you see that in a report, a sentence like, this turned out not to be a chasing job but an answering job, you are watching the loop do the finest work it can do. Treasure it. Ask for it. And when you brief, leave the door open for it explicitly, with a sentence as simple as, if you find the job is actually something else, tell me what you found and proceed on that basis where it is safe. Permission to revise understanding, granted in advance, is one of the highest value sentences you will ever write to an agent.

Finally, let me connect the loop back to the rest of our class, because no piece of this subject lives alone and I want you to see the whole machine at once before we move on. The loop is where the earlier lessons stop being separate ideas and become one working thing. Memory feeds the loop, because observation is only possible when the results of the last step are held in context and the relevant parts of the long record are fetched into view. Tools power the loop, because every act beat is a tool reaching into the world and every observe beat begins with what the tool brought back. The goal governs the loop, holding the lamp for observation and standing fixed while everything beneath it is allowed to move. And the report, that fourth piece of our very first definition, is where the loop shows its working, the acts, the observations, and the adjustments laid out so you can check the turns rather than merely admire the destination. When all of that is present, you have something genuinely new under the sun, not a program marching and not a voice merely answering, but a worker who can be given an outcome in the morning and be trusted to find the honest path to it by afternoon, stopping where it should stop and landing where it should land. That is worth the four lessons it took us to build it. Next lesson we fence it properly, with permission and latitude, so that its freedom is the freedom you chose, and not a freedom it wandered into.

One practical rhythm for your own briefs falls out of all of this, and I will give it to you as plainly as I can, because a lesson should leave tools in your hands and not only ideas in your head. When you hand Dave a job of any real size, write four short things, in this order. First, the goal, with its finish line, what done looks like and by when. Second, the checks that must happen, the record to be searched, the calendar to be consulted, the facts that must be verified before anything is drafted or promised. Third, the freedoms, what the agent may revise on its own when the evidence turns, steps, order, wording, even the description of the job where that is safe. Fourth, the fences, where the loop must break upward to you, the amounts, the promises, the names, the contradictions that must never be guessed past. Goal, checks, freedoms, fences. Four short paragraphs at most, and often four sentences will do. A brief written that way does not slow the work down. It speeds the work up, because the agent spends its loops on the job instead of spending them discovering, the expensive way, where your boundaries were. And on the days when you are too busy to write even four sentences, write the first and the fourth, the goal and the fences, and let the middle be filled by the standing record we built in lesson three. A loop with a clear goal and clear fences will carry a busy day safely. A loop with neither is a hope, and hope is not a management method.

And if all of this still feels abstract, reduce it to the smallest possible scene and keep the scene. A single message arrives asking for something by Friday. The march reads it and drafts the obvious reply. The loop reads it, checks the calendar and the record, discovers Friday is already promised twice over, and drafts a different reply entirely, one that tells the truth early while the truth is still cheap. Everything in this lesson lives inside that small scene. The plan changed because the observation was real. The goal, a truthful and workable answer, never moved. When you are unsure whether a piece of agent work looped or marched, look for that scene inside it. Look for the moment the easy reply died and the honest one was born. That moment is the loop, and it is the moment your company is paying for.

So let me recap the lesson, in order. First, a plan is an ordered set of intended steps toward a goal, held lightly enough to be changed by evidence. Second, a loop is one full turn of acting, observing what came back, and adjusting the next step in light of it, and the rhythm of good agent work is act, observe, adjust, repeated. Third, the goal stays fixed while the plan stays fluid, and swapping those two is where most frustration with agents is born. Fourth, step size should match the stakes, fast loops on safe ground, slow loops on serious ground, with small steps wherever observation must be sharp. Fifth, when observation reveals a missing fact, a contradiction, or a stop point, the correct adjustment is breaking the loop upward, stopping and bringing the problem to you clearly. Sixth, loops serve landings, and a report should always be able to answer whether the job is done and what exactly remains.

What comes next is the questions on this lesson, and the full text stays open beside them, as always, because going back to read is learning, not cheating. Work through them in order, from the plain recall of the rhythm up to the harder questions where you must judge a piece of work and name its beats, and do not rush the harder ones, because they are the ones that most resemble the real supervision you will do every week, reading a report, finding the turns, and deciding whether the loop truly turned or merely marched in nicer language.

 In our next lesson we take up permission and latitude, who decides what the agent may do, which is where the loop meets its boundaries and where your judgment becomes the frame the whole system works inside. For now, notice the loops in the next job Dave does for you, and enjoy how much more of the work you can suddenly see. Go to the questions when you are ready, take a breath first if the day has been long, and I will see you in lesson five.
