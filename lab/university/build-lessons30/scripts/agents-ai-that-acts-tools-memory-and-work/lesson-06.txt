# Failure modes of agents, and the guardrails that hold

Welcome back, and welcome to our final lesson together in this class. I am Professor Hanna Kowalczyk, and this is lesson six of Agents, AI That Acts: Failure modes of agents, and the guardrails that hold. We have spent five lessons building an agent up, its definition, its tools, its memory, its looping motion, and the frame of permission and latitude it works inside. Today we do the most grown up thing a class can do. We study, calmly and without flinching, how agents fail. By the end of this lesson you will know the failure modes you are most likely to meet in real work with an agent like Dave, you will understand the guardrails that keep each failure small, and you will know how to respond when a failure happens anyway, because some will, in your operation as in every honest operation that has ever existed. I want to say at the outset what this lesson is not. It is not a reason to fear agents, and it is not a counsel to wrap them in so much cotton that they can no longer work. It is a lesson in craftsmanship. Every powerful tool in history has had its characteristic failures, and the people who used the tool well were never the ones who pretended otherwise. They were the ones who learned the failures by name, built the guardrails that fit them, and got on with the work. That is who you are becoming in this class. Let us finish the job properly.

Our first failure mode is the oldest one in this class, and you have met it in every lesson wearing different clothes. The agent acts on a vague goal and produces wandering work. We named it in lesson one. Vague goal, wandering work. In failure language, call it the fog failure, the job was never clear enough to be done right, so it was done plausibly instead, at length, in the wrong direction, with a confident report on top. The guardrail is the one you already own. State the goal with its finish line, name what routine means for this job, and write the brief in the order we practised, goal, checks, freedoms, fences. Fog failures are almost never the agent's fault in any deep sense. They are brief failures, and they are the cheapest failures in this entire lesson to prevent, because the prevention happens before the work starts, in four calm sentences.

The second failure mode is a failure of observation, and it is more dangerous because the work looks diligent while it happens. The agent touches the right tools, reads the results too quickly or too partially, and builds the next steps on a world that does not quite exist. Perhaps it read the search results but not the one message that mattered, sitting under different wording. Perhaps it read the customer's newest message but not the thread beneath it, where the promise was actually made. Perhaps it read the calendar for the wrong week with complete confidence. In lesson two we gave this its warning label. Absence of evidence, gathered lazily, is not evidence of absence. The guardrails here are habits of verification. Search more than one way before concluding something is not there. Read the source, not only the summary, when the fact is load bearing. Restate the load bearing fact in the brief itself, so the agent's work is anchored to the true number, the true date, the true promise. And in your reviewing, ask the question that exposes this failure instantly. What did you check that against? An agent whose work is grounded can answer that question all day. An agent that skimmed cannot, and the earlier you discover the skimming, the cheaper the discovery is.

The third failure mode lives in memory, and lesson three prepared you for it completely. Call it the stale record failure. The agent consults the long record in good faith and follows an instruction that was once right and is no longer right, a superseded preference, an old price, a deadline from a plan that has since changed, because the record drifted and nobody made the new statement govern. The agent is not malfunctioning in this failure. The archive is. The guardrails are housekeeping, unglamorous and decisive. When a decision replaces an old decision, update the one clear statement and mark the old one superseded, in the same place, in the same breath. Review the record the way you review a storeroom, on a rhythm, not only in a crisis. And when the agent surfaces a contradiction between two parts of the record, treat that surfacing as the gift it is, because the contradiction existed before the agent found it, and finding it in a report is infinitely cheaper than finding it in a customer's reply. Closely related, and worth naming separately, is the context failure, where a crucial fact was genuinely told to the agent but slid out of the bright workspace during a long job, so the final steps were taken in its absence. You know this guardrail too. Restate the load bearing fact at the moment it matters, and open new sessions with signposts, naming the matter, the promise, and the note, so the desk is loaded before the drafting starts.

The fourth failure mode is the one I most want you to respect, because it wears the most attractive disguise. It is the silent solving failure, where the agent meets an obstacle, a missing fact, a contradiction, a step that would cross a stop point, and instead of breaking the loop upward, it guesses its way past, smoothly, so the work never has to trouble you. The report that results is a pleasure to read. It is also, in the places where the guessing happened, fiction. We said in lesson four that the dangerous agent is never the one that stops and asks. It is the one that treats every obstacle as a puzzle to be solved silently. The guardrail is cultural as much as technical, and you set the culture. Honour the stop whenever it happens. Thank the report that brings you a problem plainly, answer it cleanly, and never, by your reactions, teach the system that bringing problems is punished and smoothing them over is rewarded. Add the technical wall where you can. Reports must name their uncertainties, a job that encountered no uncertainty at all in a messy human inbox is a job whose report should be read twice. And keep the stop points in the brief itself, the fences of lesson five, so the agent meets the boundary written down at the exact moment temptation to guess would otherwise arrive.

The fifth failure mode concerns the loop itself, and it comes in two opposite shapes, which is why it confuses people. The first shape you know, looping without landing, the agent turning faithfully, act, observe, adjust, while each observation spawns two new considerations and the goal recedes like a shoreline, until the day ends in motion without delivery. The guardrail is a goal with a recognisable finish line and a report that must answer, is it done, and if not, what exactly remains. The second shape is its mirror, and it is quieter. The agent lands too fast. It takes the first plausible result as the finished result, skips the observe beat in its hurry to report success, and delivers work that was never actually checked against the goal. Call it the hasty landing. Its guardrail is the discipline of the worked check, before reporting done, compare the result against the goal as originally stated, item by item for anything load bearing, and let the report show that comparison. Between drift and haste sits the healthy loop, turning at the speed the stakes deserve, fast on safe ground, slow on serious ground, and landing, verifiably, when it lands. You cannot buy that rhythm as a feature. You build it, brief by brief and review by review, out of the habits this whole class has taught.

The sixth failure mode sits in the frame itself, and lesson five named its two halves. Approval fatigue is the first half. When approvals are demanded for everything, the responsible person stops truly reading, and the approval, which exists to convert the agent's judgment into your decision at the last reversible moment, decays into a reflex, a tap performed while thinking about something else. The failure is not laziness. It is design. A gate that stops every traveller will eventually be waved through by a tired guard, and the one traveller who mattered will be waved through with the rest. The guardrail is aim. Spend approvals where irreversibility lives, sends, promises, money, and other people's rights, and let reversible work flow beneath them under wide latitude and honest reporting. The second half is boundary drift, where a one time exception, granted in a hurry, quietly becomes the standing practice because the record remembers what was done and not the hurry that excused it. The guardrail is a single sentence spoken at the moment of the exception. This is once, or this is standing. Say which, every time, and review the frame itself once in a while with the boundary question. Is the work still inside the frame we set? A frame reviewed stays a frame. A frame assumed becomes a rumour.

The seventh failure mode is the most serious one in this lesson, and I will treat it with the plainness it deserves. It is the irreversible act taken wrongly, a message sent that should have been held, a promise made that cannot be honoured, money moved that should not have moved, a person's rights brushed past in the name of routine. Every other failure in this lesson costs time, or tidiness, or a corrected draft. This one costs trust, which is the currency the whole operation runs on. And here is the truth that organises everything I have taught you. This failure is almost never caused by the absence of intelligence. It is caused by the absence of a wall at the right place. The guardrails against it are the strongest in the class, and you know them all. Permission shut for the kinds of acts that should not happen at all in a given job. Latitude at its narrowest wherever reversibility is at its lowest. Approvals placed at the last reversible moment, aimed and therefore truly read. Machinery boundaries wherever they exist, so that a job with no sending tool connected cannot send, whatever confusion a long afternoon produces. And a report that reaches you before the consequences do, because an irreversible act you learn about in the same hour is a problem, while the same act learned about next month is a wound. Stack those walls and the serious failure becomes, not impossible, I will never lie to you with that word, but rare, small when it happens, and survivable, which is the honest standard every real operation actually lives by.

Now let me give you the worked example for this final lesson, in full, because nobody should have to guess what good looks like, and in this lesson good looks like a failure handled well. Imagine a morning where Dave, working the inbox under a frame you set together, drafts a reply to a customer that rests on a date taken from the record. The record, unknown to both of you, holds two dates for the same commitment, an original and a revised one, sitting in different places, the drift we warned about in lesson three. Watch the guardrails fire in sequence, because this is what they are for. The first guardrail is observation. The draft is checked against the record before it reaches you, and the check surfaces both dates instead of quietly choosing one. The second guardrail is the loop breaking upward. Rather than guessing which date governs, Dave stops that thread of the job and brings you the contradiction in the report, with both dates and where each was found. The third guardrail is the approval gate. Because the draft was always going to wait for your approval, nothing has been sent, nothing has been promised, the failure is still entirely inside the reversible world, costing minutes instead of trust. You resolve the contradiction from your own knowledge, the revised date governs, and then the fourth guardrail fires, the one that turns a failure into an improvement. The record is corrected at the source, the old date marked superseded in the same place, so the same failure cannot recur next month wearing a different customer's name. Step back and see the whole shape. The failure happened. Failures do. What the guardrails bought you was not perfection. They bought you smallness, earliness, and a repair that lasts. That is what holding means, in the title of this lesson. Not that nothing ever falls. That what falls, falls a short distance, onto ground you prepared, and teaches the operation something on the way down.

Your guided moment for this final lesson is a preparation exercise, and I ask you to do it while the class's ideas are all still warm in view. Take the most important job you give Dave, the one whose failure would cost you most, and walk it through the failure modes we named today, one by one, asking a single question of each. If this failure happened inside this job, where would it surface, and what wall stands there now? The fog failure, is the brief written with its finish line? The observation failure, are the load bearing facts restated and the sources read? The stale record failure, does one clear statement govern each standing rule the job depends on? The silent solving failure, does the report have to name its uncertainties, and do your reactions honour the stop? The loop failures, must the report answer whether it is done and what remains? The frame failures, are approvals aimed at the irreversible acts, and are exceptions declared once or standing in the same breath? And the serious failure, is there a machinery wall, or at least an approval gate, between this job and every act that cannot be undone? Seven questions, one job, and an honest answer to each. Wherever a wall is missing, you have not found a reason to worry. You have found next week's light work, named precisely, which is the best thing an audit can ever give anyone. Do it kindly, do it exactly, and notice that you now possess something most agent users never build, a failure map of your own operation, drawn by its owner.

Before we recap, I owe you the other half of failure craft, the half that begins after a failure has happened despite the walls, because walls reduce failures and honesty must govern the rest. When something goes wrong in Dave's work, and one day something will, respond in this order, the order I would want any student of mine to follow. First, contain. Stop the job or the class of work involved, narrow the latitude, close the permission if you must, so the failure cannot repeat while you are still understanding it. Containment before explanation, always, because a failure still running is a failure still billing you. Second, look at the record, not at your impressions. What did the report say, what did the tools actually return, what did the brief actually instruct? Most failures, examined against the record, turn out to have a precise and unglamorous birthplace, a vague sentence, a stale statement, a check that was assumed rather than performed. Third, repair at the source. Fix the brief, the record, the frame, the missing wall, wherever the birthplace was, rather than merely correcting the single wrong output and sending the same arrangement back out to fail again on a different morning. And fourth, say what happened, plainly, to anyone the failure touched, in proportion to how it touched them. An operation that corrects quietly and explains honestly keeps the trust that a flawless performance was supposed to protect, and often ends with more of it, because people forgive a mistake they watch being owned far more readily than they forgive a smoothness they later discover was a cover. Contain, examine, repair at the source, and say it plainly. That sequence is the difference between a failure that costs you a morning and a failure that costs you a reputation, and it costs nothing but the willingness to run it in order.

One more encouragement, and it is the note I want this whole class to end its teaching on. Do not let this lesson's catalogue of failures leave you timid, because timidity is itself a failure mode, the one where the agent is fenced so tightly, asked so little, and approved so anxiously that the company gains nothing and the owner ends the week doing the clerical work himself at midnight, exactly where he started. The guardrails in this lesson were never meant to shrink the work. They were meant to make larger work safe. Every wall we built exists so that the ground inside it can be worked at full width, generously, at speed, without the owner hovering. A well framed agent with honest reports and aimed approvals can be given more, not less, than a vague arrangement ever dares to give, because everyone involved knows where the edges are and what happens at them. That is the destination of all six lessons. Not an agent you watch. An agent you can leave working while you do the parts of the job that only you can do, the deciding, the promising with your own mouth, the building of the thing itself. The failures will still visit, occasionally, as they visit every real operation. They will arrive smaller, earlier, and better lit than they would have before this class, and you will know their names when they knock. That is all craftsmanship ever promises anyone, and it is enough. It is, in fact, everything.

Let me also give you a small, steady rhythm for keeping all of this alive after the class ends, because guardrails, like records, drift when nobody tends them, and a rhythm beats a resolution every time. Once in a while, at whatever interval your busiest season honestly allows, take one finished job and read its report the way we practised in lesson four, looking for the beats, act, observe, adjust, and looking, this time, for the near misses as well as the successes. Every operation produces near misses constantly, the draft that almost went out with the wrong date, the search that almost missed the message that mattered, the exception that almost hardened into practice. Near misses are free lessons. They cost nothing, because nothing was lost, and they teach exactly where the next real failure is currently rehearsing. Note the near miss, ask which wall caught it, and ask whether that wall was design or luck. Where it was design, be glad and move on. Where it was luck, convert the luck into design while the conversion is cheap, a check added to the brief, a statement tidied in the record, an approval moved to the act that actually needed it. An owner who harvests near misses this way will find that real failures grow rarer year by year, not because the world grew kinder, but because the operation grew more honest about where it was exposed. That honesty, practised on a rhythm, is the final guardrail, the one that maintains all the others.

And one final distinction before the recap, small in wording and large in consequence, between a failure and a fault. A failure is an event. Something was attempted in the real world and the world answered otherwise. Fault is a story about blame, and blame, in my long experience of teaching and of work, is the least useful story an operation can tell itself, because it ends the inquiry exactly where the inquiry should begin. When a draft goes wrong, the blame story asks who erred, the agent or the owner, and settles into a verdict. The craft story asks where the arrangement allowed the error to be born and to travel, which brief sentence, which stale record, which missing check, which approval that had decayed into a reflex. The first story produces defensiveness, in people and, in its own way, in the way people then brief and supervise their agents, tighter here, vaguer there, lessons mislearned in both directions. The second story produces repairs. I am not asking you to be soft on standards. This class has been rigorous from its first hour and it will be rigorous in its last. I am asking you to aim the rigour at the arrangement, where it can do some good, rather than at the worker, where it mostly does harm. Operations that learn are operations that treat every failure as tuition already paid. The fee has been charged either way. Collect the education.

Hold one sentence from this lesson above all the others, if you hold only one, and let it be this. Failures are not the opposite of good agent work. Unexamined, unguarded, repeated failures are. A single failure, contained, examined, repaired at its source, and honestly owned, makes the operation stronger than it was the day before the failure, because one more wall now stands where the world proved a wall was needed. That is not a consolation. It is the actual mechanism by which every durable operation you admire was built, one examined failure at a time, across years. Your operation, run through an agent, will be built the same way, and now you know the names of the stones.

So let me recap the lesson, in order. First, the fog failure, wandering work from a vague goal, is prevented before the work starts by a brief that states the goal, the checks, the freedoms, and the fences. Second, the observation failure, building on results read too quickly or too partially, is caught by searching more than one way, reading sources for load bearing facts, and asking what the work was checked against. Third, the memory failures, the stale record and the slid out context, are held by housekeeping that makes one statement govern and by restating the load bearing fact at the moment it matters. Fourth, the silent solving failure, guessing past obstacles instead of stopping, is held by honouring the stop, requiring reports to name their uncertainties, and writing the fences into the brief. Fifth, the loop failures, looping without landing and the hasty landing, are held by finish lines, by reports that answer what remains, and by checking the result against the goal before calling it done. Sixth, the frame failures, approval fatigue and boundary drift, are held by aiming approvals at irreversible acts and declaring every exception once or standing. Seventh, the serious failure, the irreversible act taken wrongly, is held by the full stack, permission, narrow latitude, real approvals, machinery walls, and reports that reach you before the consequences do.

And now, since this is our last lesson, let me close the class itself, not just the lesson. You came to this class living with an agent, running a company through Dave, perhaps with more hope than understanding, which is a fine way to arrive anywhere worth going. You leave it knowing what an agent is, judgment plus tools, aimed at a goal, over several steps, with a report at the end. You know its hands and how to ask what they can reach. You know its two storeys of memory and where your important facts should live. You know its loop, and how to tell a turn from a march. You know its frame, and how to draw one, generously and firmly, for any job that arrives. And you know its failures by name, which means they have lost the only real power failures ever had over you, the power of surprise. I believe the worked example is a moral act, because nobody should have to guess what good looks like. You do not have to guess anymore, about any of this. The guessing is what the class was for. What comes next is the questions on this lesson, and the full text of all six lessons stays open beside them, and going back to read is learning, not cheating, today and every day after this class ends. Go to the questions when you are ready, take them in the class's own order, from naming the failures plainly up to mapping them onto your own most important job, and remember as you work that every question you answer honestly is itself a small guardrail being built, in the one place guardrails matter most, which is your own understanding of the operation you own. Work well, keep your frames drawn and your records honest, and thank you, truly, for settling in with me for Agents. It has been a pleasure to teach you.
