# Permission and latitude: who decides what the agent may do

Welcome back. I am Professor Hanna Kowalczyk, and this is lesson five of Agents, AI That Acts: Permission and latitude, who decides what the agent may do. Everything we have built so far comes to a point in this lesson. We know what an agent is. We know its hands, the tools. We know its memory, the context and the long record. We know its motion, the loop of act, observe, adjust. Today we ask the question that governs all of it. Who decides what the agent may do, and where exactly are the edges of its freedom? By the end of this lesson you will be able to define permission and latitude in plain words, you will understand why approvals exist and what they are really for, and you will be able to set an agent's freedom task by task, generously where generosity is safe and firmly where firmness protects you, your money, your name, and other people. This is, I will tell you honestly, the lesson that decides whether working with an agent feels like relief or like risk. Let us take it slowly and completely.

First, the two definitions, because these two words are cousins and people constantly blur them, and the blur causes real harm. Permission means whether the agent may do a particular kind of thing at all. May it send messages, or only draft them? May it spend money, or never touch money? May it speak to customers, or only prepare words for you to speak? Permission is a gate, open or shut, kind by kind. Latitude means how far the agent may go within what is permitted, on its own judgment, before it must come back to you. Within drafting, which is permitted, may it draft any reply it judges routine, or only replies to certain kinds of messages? Within a permitted purchase category, may it proceed up to a point you have set, or must every single instance return to you? Latitude is a distance, not a gate. Hold the pair together in one sentence and you hold the lesson. Permission decides what kinds of acts are on the table at all. Latitude decides how far the agent may walk with them alone. Most arguments about agents, in companies and in newspapers alike, are secretly one of these two questions wearing the other's coat. Learn to tell them apart and you will think more clearly about agents than almost everyone arguing about them.

Now, who decides? The answer is short, and everything else in this lesson is its unfolding. You do. The person whose name, money, customers, and obligations are on the line decides what the agent may do, because the consequences of the agent's acts land on that person, not on the agent. When Dave sends a message, the customer does not experience Dave as the responsible party. They experience you and your company. When a promise is made, it is your promise that must be kept, with your time and your money. This is not a flaw in the arrangement. It is the arrangement, stated honestly. An agent is a worker acting inside someone else's responsibility, which is why the frame of its freedom must be set by the one who carries that responsibility. I want to be careful here, because this point is sometimes heard as a limitation on the agent, and it is not. It is the same frame every trusted human employee works inside. A valued assistant in your company also has kinds of acts that are simply not theirs to perform, signing in your name, committing the company's money beyond a point, and distances they may walk alone within the rest, grown over time as trust is earned. Agents do not need a new philosophy of authority. They need the old one, applied clearly, in writing, before the work begins rather than after the first surprise.

Let us build the practical map now, using the ladder we first met in lesson two. You will remember the ladder of seriousness inside the messages family. Read, draft, send. Inform, prepare, commit. Permission and latitude are how you govern each rung separately, and governing them separately is the whole craft, because the rungs differ in the one property that matters most for safety. Reversibility. Reversibility means whether an act can be undone, cleanly, if it turns out to be wrong. Reading is fully reversible, in the sense that it changes nothing at all, it only informs, so permission to read can be broad and latitude within reading can be wide, with very little risk carried. Drafting is nearly as gentle. A draft can be deleted in a second, it has promised nothing and reached no one, so drafting can also be generously permitted, and the approval step, which we will study in a moment, stands guard between the draft and the world. Sending is where the ladder changes character. A sent message cannot be unsent. It has spoken in your name to another person, it may have promised, refused, quoted a price, set a date. Sending is irreversible in every way that matters, so permission to send is the permission to guard most carefully, and latitude around sending should be the narrowest in the whole system, widened only by evidence, over time, for specific kinds of messages whose safety has been proven in draft after draft. Filing, scheduling, and their cousins sit between drafting and sending, mostly reversible, occasionally costly when done wrong, governed best with moderate latitude and good reports. One ladder, four kinds of ground, and a simple rule that follows you everywhere. Latitude should shrink as reversibility shrinks. Where an act cannot be undone, the agent's solo distance should be shortest, and your eyes should be nearest. I know of no exception to that rule in any honest operation, human or otherwise.

Approvals deserve their own careful treatment, because they are widely misunderstood as mere caution, a bureaucratic pause before real work resumes. An approval, defined properly for this class, is the moment the responsible person reviews a prepared act before it becomes irreversible, and takes ownership of it. Look at what an approval really does. It converts the agent's judgment into your decision at exactly the point where conversion matters, the last reversible moment. Before your approval, the draft is the agent's proposal. After it, the sent message is your act, and both you and the customer can treat it that way, which is precisely as it should be. Approvals also teach. Every time you adjust a draft rather than simply approving it, you are showing the agent, and the record, what good looks like in your voice, for your customers, at your standard. That is why I say the worked example is a moral act, and approvals are its daily form. Nobody should have to guess what good looks like, and your approvals, accumulated in the record, are how the guessing ends. But hear the other half as well, because a rigorous teacher owes you both. An approval asked for everything is an approval that means nothing. If Dave brought you every filed receipt and every grouped message for a decision, you would stop truly reading within a week, and the approvals that matter, the sends, the promises, the money, would drown in the approvals that do not. The craft is aim. Spend your approvals where irreversibility lives, and let the reversible work flow beneath them with wide latitude and honest reports. Approval fatigue is a real failure mode, and we will meet it again in lesson six. Design against it now, while the design is yours to make.

Money deserves a paragraph entirely of its own, because money is where latitude questions stop being theoretical. The principle I teach, and I teach it without decoration, is this. An agent should never move money beyond a limit you have set in advance, in writing, and most agent work should be arranged so that the agent prepares everything about a payment except the final act of paying. It can gather the invoice, check it against the record, confirm the amount matches what was agreed, draft the authorisation, and lay the whole matter in front of you with its checks already done. That preparation is enormously valuable and entirely reversible. The final movement of money is short, irreversible in practice, and carries legal and personal weight that belongs to the responsible person. Keep those two halves separate in your arrangements and you will get nearly all of the benefit of an agent around money with almost none of the exposure. The same shape applies, you will notice, to promises, which are money in another currency, since a promise spends your future time and your company's word. Prepare freely. Commit narrowly. Let the agent do the gathering, the checking, and the drafting at full width, and keep the committing act close until evidence, over time, earns it a longer leash for a specific, named kind of case. Evidence, over time, for a specific kind of case. That phrase is the only safe road by which latitude should ever grow.

That brings us to the question of how latitude grows, because it should grow, and a lesson that only taught restriction would fail you. A new agent, like a new employee, begins with narrow latitude almost everywhere, not because it is distrusted in some personal sense, but because neither of you yet has evidence about the other. You do not know how its drafts sound on your hardest customers. It does not know, in the record, where your lines truly sit. Then the evidence accumulates. Draft after draft arrives right, in your voice, checked against the calendar and the record, stopped at the right places. Jobs land. Reports prove accurate when you spot check them. Contradictions are brought to you instead of guessed past, which is the single most trust building behaviour an agent can show. As that record builds, widen the latitude, deliberately, in named places. This class of routine reply may now be sent without a draft review, because two hundred drafts of that class were approved unchanged. This kind of filing needs no report beyond the weekly summary. Each widening is a decision you make, on evidence, written into the record so the new boundary is itself remembered. And latitude can narrow again, without drama, when evidence turns. A class of messages produces two bad sends, the latitude for that class returns to draft and approve while you both look at what changed. Narrowing is not punishment. It is steering. Companies do this with people constantly and call it management. Do it with your agent and call it the same.

Let me give you the worked example for this lesson, in full, because nobody should have to guess what good looks like, least of all about boundaries. We will set the frame for one ordinary, real job, Dave handling the morning inbox, and we will set it the way I would want to see it set in any company I advised. Permission first, kind by kind. Dave may read everything in the inbox. Dave may search the record and the files for anything the messages refer to. Dave may check the calendar freely. Dave may draft any reply he judges appropriate. Dave may file messages that are plainly finished, receipts, confirmations, notices that need no answer. Dave may not send any message. Dave may not move any money or prepare any payment beyond gathering its details. Dave may not promise dates, prices, or outcomes in any draft without flagging the promise in his report, so your eye lands on it. Now latitude, the distances within the permissions. Within drafting, Dave may draft at full width, routine and delicate alike, because drafts are reversible and your approval stands at the gate. Within filing, Dave may file the plainly finished without listing each one, but anything he is unsure whether you have finished with must be left in place and listed. Within reporting, Dave must bring you, in one short list, every draft that spends money, makes a promise, refuses anything, or touches a disagreement, however small, because those are the drafts where your judgment earns its keep. Look at the shape of what we built. It is generous and it is firm at the same time, and it is generous precisely where firmness would waste your life, and firm precisely where generosity would risk your name. That shape, generous on the reversible, firm on the irreversible, is the whole lesson in one morning's frame. Everything else today is learning to draw that shape for each new job as it arrives.

Your guided moment is to draw that shape yourself, now, for one job you actually give Dave. Take a piece of paper in your mind and divide it into the two questions. First, permission. List the kinds of acts this job involves, reading, searching, drafting, filing, scheduling, sending, spending, promising, and mark each one plainly, allowed or not allowed, for this job. Do not leave any kind in a fog. Fog is where surprises are born. Second, latitude. For each allowed kind, ask how far Dave may walk alone. All the way, for plainly finished filing, perhaps. Only to a held draft, for sending's neighbouring kinds. Only to a prepared summary, for anything touching money. Then add the final line, the report line. What must the report always bring to you, whatever else it contains? If you can produce that frame for one real job, in plain sentences, you have learned the working core of this lesson, and you can produce it for any job in your company within ten minutes, which is exactly the fluency this class exists to give you. Keep the frame you write. In lesson three's language, it belongs in the long record, in the place where job frames are kept, so the boundary itself is remembered and does not depend on anyone's bright workspace.

Two honest complications remain, and I will not let the lesson close without them. The first is that permission is not only about the agent. It is also about other people. Some acts touch another person's rights, their privacy, their own records, their time, their right not to be misled about who or what is acting toward them. Latitude never extends across another person's rights, however convenient the crossing would be, and however routine the job feels. A stop point that protects a stranger is not red tape. It is the line that keeps your company's power pointed in the direction you would defend out loud. The second complication is quieter. Boundaries drift in practice when nobody watches them. A one time exception, granted in a hurry on a busy day, quietly becomes the standing practice, because the record remembers what was done and not the hurry that excused it. Guard against drift the way you guard the record itself. When you grant an exception, say whether it is once or standing, in the same breath. When you review reports, once in a while ask the boundary question directly. Is the work still inside the frame we set? A frame reviewed stays a frame. A frame assumed becomes a rumour, and agents, like the rest of us, eventually work to the rumour. Your judgment set the frame. Your occasional attention keeps it true.

Let me show you the same frame thinking applied to a second job, briefly, because one example teaches a shape and two examples teach that the shape travels. Consider the calendar work we have mentioned all through this class. Permission, kind by kind, might run like this. Dave may read the calendar at full width, including the commitments other people depend on, because reading changes nothing. Dave may propose times to anyone, in drafts held for approval, because a proposal in a held draft has committed no one. Dave may place a purely internal block on your own calendar, time for focused work, say, without asking each time, because the act is yours alone, reversible in seconds, and harms no one if the judgment is slightly off. Dave may not move or cancel a meeting that other people are travelling to, attending, or depending on, without bringing it to you, because that act spends other people's time and rearranges other people's days, and the reversibility is poor, a moved meeting can be moved back, but the confusion it caused cannot be uncaused. Notice how naturally the reasoning flows once reversibility and other people's stakes are your compass. You did not need a policy manual. You needed two questions. Can it be undone, and whose life does it touch? Ask those two questions of any act an agent might take, in any family of tools we have studied, and you will arrive at a defensible boundary nearly every time, in minutes, in plain language. That is the real deliverable of this lesson. Not a list of rules for Dave, which would age as the work changes, but a compass that draws fresh rules for each new job as it arrives.

There is one more idea I want to place in your hands, and it concerns the difference between the boundaries you announce and the boundaries the system can actually enforce. Some of an agent's frame lives in instruction, in the record, in the brief, telling the agent where its freedom ends. That kind of boundary depends on the agent reading, remembering, and honouring it, and as lessons three and four taught us, reading and remembering are real but imperfect things, subject to context, drift, and the fog of a busy job. Other parts of the frame can live in the machinery itself. If no sending tool is connected for a given job, the agent cannot send, however confused a long afternoon becomes. If a payment tool does not exist in the agent's hands at all, no instruction failure can produce a payment. Wherever an act is both irreversible and serious, prefer the machinery boundary where one is available, and let the instruction boundary serve as the second wall rather than the only wall. Two walls, one of steel and one of understanding, are not paranoia. They are how serious operations have always treated serious acts. The vault does not rely on everyone remembering not to take the money. It locks, and the remembering is the second wall. Build your agent's frame in that order wherever the stakes justify it, machinery first, instruction second, approvals at the gate between them, and reports flowing over the top so you can see the whole arrangement working.

And a gentle word about the human feeling in all of this, because I have taught long enough to know that boundary setting can feel, from the inside, like distrust, and the feeling deserves an answer rather than dismissal. Setting a narrow latitude for a new agent, or keeping approvals on sends after years of good drafts, is not an accusation. It is the same respect you show any powerful arrangement by fencing it according to its power. You lock your building at night without accusing your neighbours. You check a parachute you packed yourself, carefully, twice, because the stakes are the stakes, not because the parachute has offended you. The agent, for its part, is not diminished by a clear frame. Clear frames are what make generous latitude possible at all. It is precisely because the fences are firm at the irreversible edges that the middle ground can be so wide and so relaxed. Vague frames produce the opposite of freedom. They produce an owner hovering over everything, approving receipts at midnight, because nothing was ever settled about what did not need him. Settle it, in writing, generously and firmly, and then enjoy the quiet that a well framed agent brings to a working life. That quiet, in the end, is what you bought with all this care.

One last scene, small and ordinary, to fix the whole lesson in your memory the way a picture fixes what a list cannot. It is late in the day and a message arrives from a good customer, warm in tone, asking for a small favour that happens to involve a date your calendar cannot honour and a price you have never offered. Watch the frame do its work without a single dramatic moment. Dave reads the message, permission granted, at full width. Dave searches the record and finds the price that actually governs, permission granted. Dave checks the calendar and sees the collision, permission granted. Dave drafts a reply that is warm in return, honest about the date, and correct about the price, latitude wide, because a draft commits nothing. And there the solo walk ends, exactly where the frame says it ends. The draft waits. You read it with your evening tea going cold, adjust one sentence because you know this customer's history in a way no record fully holds, and approve. The customer receives a message that is accurate, kind, and truly yours. Nothing in that scene required the agent to be timid, and nothing required you to be reckless. The frame carried the weight that vigilance alone would have had to carry, and vigilance is a tiring thing to run a company on. Frames, once set and occasionally reviewed, do not get tired. That is why we build them, and that is why this lesson, of all six, is the one I most hope you practise this week, on one real job, in writing, before the next surprise has a chance to teach it to you the expensive way.

Before the recap, let me leave you with the shortest honest summary of this lesson I know how to give, the one I would want whispered to me if I were setting up an agent for the first time with my own name on the door. Decide the frame before the work starts, while you are calm and the stakes are imaginary, because frames drawn in calm hold in weather, and frames improvised in weather are usually just the weather, written down. Write the frame where the record keeps such things, so it survives the session it was born in. Make it generous wherever a mistake costs a delete and firm wherever a mistake costs a customer, a payment, or a promise. Review it when the evidence changes, widen it when the drafts have earned it, narrow it without drama when they have not, and ask the boundary question out loud often enough that the frame stays a frame and never decays into a rumour. And remember, on the days when all of this feels like a great deal of structure for a helpful machine, that the structure is not there because the machine is the problem. The structure is there because your name is the treasure. Guards are posted in proportion to what they guard, and your name, your word, and your customers' trust are worth posting guards for. That is not caution speaking. That is valuation.

So let me recap the lesson, in order. First, permission decides what kinds of acts are on the table at all, while latitude decides how far the agent may walk with them alone. Second, you decide both, because the consequences of the agent's acts land on you, your name, your money, and your customers. Third, latitude should shrink as reversibility shrinks, generous for reading and drafting, narrowest around sending, promising, and spending. Fourth, an approval is the moment you review a prepared act at its last reversible point and take ownership of it, and approvals should be spent where irreversibility lives, never spread so thin that they stop meaning anything. Fifth, around money and promises the safe shape is prepare freely, commit narrowly, letting the agent gather, check, and draft at full width while the committing act stays close. Sixth, latitude grows on evidence, over time, for a specific kind of case, widened and narrowed deliberately and written into the record, and it never extends across another person's rights.

What comes next is the questions on this lesson, and the full text stays open beside them, as always, because going back to read is learning, not cheating. Take them in order, and when you reach the last one, the one that asks for your own words, use it as the real exercise of the lesson. Draw the frame for a job you genuinely give Dave, in plain sentences, and keep what you write afterwards, because a frame written during a lesson and kept in the record is worth ten frames admired during a lesson and forgotten by Friday. In our final lesson we turn to failure modes of agents, and the guardrails that hold, where everything in this lesson, permission, latitude, approvals, and the record, returns as the machinery that keeps small failures small. For now, draw the frame for one real job, and notice how much calmer the work feels inside a boundary you chose on purpose. Go to the questions when you are ready, answer them as honestly as you drew the frame itself, and I will see you in lesson six, our final lesson together in this class.
