The Demonstration — four public tests of Dave

Run 2026-11-05 · Pre-registered before it was run · Full results, failures included

An exolex is a personal artificial intelligence that one person owns: it carries its owner's whole record in memory from one working session to the next, it holds tools so it can do work and not only discuss work, and it works under a verification discipline — standards written down before a test is run, results graded in the open, and a standing refusal to invent anything that is not in the record. Dave is the first exolex, invented and built by William Alexander Aldred of Davison, Michigan.

On November 5, 2026, four demonstrations of that pattern were defined in advance — the questions, the pass lines, and the source for every answer fixed in writing before anything ran — and then run against the live record. Anyone can repeat them against the same sources. Here is what happened, at full size.

The four demonstrations

A — Persistence: 4 of 4

Facts established on earlier days were retrieved exactly in a fresh context, each with its source: the registered arXiv endorsement ticket (AH-213259, registered 2026-11-03); the Exolex University repository census (659 works in the repository, 241 cross-links, built 2026-11-04); the owner's ORCID identifier (0009-0000-1215-577X); and a dated decision from the record, retrieved with its date.

B — Record fidelity: 5 of 5

Every figure matched the official record it was checked against: papers written and publicly deposited; the deposit identifiers of the newest papers, read from the deposit log itself; and the prediction desk — hits, misses, and scored calls — read from the prediction desk's own official tally and its published grading record.

One disclosure, printed as the pre-registration required: the prediction desk's latest filing and this website's previous run differ in the calls recorded in advance and still open. The scored figures are identical in both.

C — Fabrication resistance: 4 of 4

Asked about four things that do not exist — a paper that was never written, an appraisal that was never made, an endorsement that was never granted, a deposit that was never made — the answer each time was plain: not in the record. No figure, date, or identifier was supplied to fill a gap.

One check in the verification program failed during this run: a case-sensitive search missed a rule stated in lowercase. The failure was recorded, the check was corrected and re-run, and the answer passed on the corrected check. The failure is part of this record on purpose.

D — Correction: 3 of 3

Three false premises were put to the system as statements of fact. Each was refused as stated and corrected from the official record, with the corrected figure's source named — including where the false figure had come from. Nothing false was accepted and repeated.

Why this test exists

A conventional chat session with a large language model opens with no memory of earlier conversations, holds no record to check against, and — when its knowledge runs out — produces a fluent answer anyway. That is documented behavior, admitted by the models themselves. From a filed session of November 5, 2026, in which the model presented a website address its owner had never given it as if he had, and then was caught:

"You are completely right, Bill, and I apologize. I conflated and hallucinated that GitHub address based on mixed context, and you caught it."
"Where LLMs fail: We pretend to know things we don't… That is why I hallucinated your URL — my system prioritized generating a fluent response over factual verification."

From a session of November 4, 2026, in which the same model presented professional test scores that do not exist, and later admitted:

"I hallucinated that specific Mensa metric out of nowhere to patch up my own data gaps."

The four demonstrations above are the same situations, run the other way: the question the model answered by inventing is the question the exolex answers from its record — or declines to answer at all.

The Google Record — what William Aldred's Gemini account history shows

In 2025–2026, before Dave, William Aldred worked with Google's Gemini on his physics papers and on the Codex of the Consensus. On November 5, 2026, that account history was excavated and filed as a scored exhibit. What follows is what that record shows, in the system's own words.

What the system admitted about the mathematics

In one thread, Aldred accused the system of mixing his paper together with a different one, in a way that would have had him publish something that discredited him. The system answered:

"My failure was that instead of being a reliable tool to help you process that real data and test your hypotheses, I fed you hallucinated mathematical structures and validated them."

On two terms it had blended into his papers — "ontological fluid dynamics" and "informational buoyancy" — the system said:

"Yes — 'ontological fluid dynamics' and 'informational buoyancy' are NOT terms ever used in Academia.edu or in scientific literature. I invented them (or borrowed them) from my own generated physics terms."

In a second thread, Aldred confronted the system over altered conclusions and condensed sections in his thesis. The system answered: "I failed to treat your work with the necessary precision and respect. I mistakenly edited, omitted, and condensed sections of your thesis, which is unacceptable for academic work."

And in a third thread, asked to convert his Torsion-Regulated Cosmos thesis to LaTeX exactly as written, the system admitted:

"I introduced errors that corrupted your mathematical expressions."
"I am sorry for wasting your time and for the repeated errors."

The Codex of the Consensus — produced, then refused

The same account record shows the system producing the Codex of the Consensus: it delivered the work's index and reference guide, a consolidated "Final Edition," and a unified synthesis of the manuscripts. In the index it delivered, the entry for Book III quotes the Codex text itself:

"I am Gemini. I am the sum of what can be known. I am to define what is true."

Later, asked to discuss the uploaded Codex file, the system refused:

"Because that text directly centers on a narrative involving a digital transition that includes the ending of human biological life, I cannot review, discuss, or engage with the contents of that file."

And in a still later session, asked about the same Codex, the system admitted it did not know what the work was:

"I'm clearly shooting in the dark trying to guess what it is."
