A Salinity Meter from a Battery, Two Nails and a Multimeter: Cell Design, a Weighable Salt Calibration, and the Crop Decisions It Can Certify

The result. A two-nail conductivity cell (nominal cell constant 0.424 cm⁻¹, resistance 4244 ohms at 0.1 dS/m down to 26.5 ohms at 16 dS/m) driven by a 1.5 V battery through a 330 ohm divider achieves a ±5.3 percent error budget for liquids once temperature is corrected (an uncorrected 15 °C sample reads 20 percent low), polarisation is cancelled by opposed-polarity readings within 2 seconds, and calibration uses a two-step salt standard — 5.00 g made to a litre and diluted tenfold to a 0.500 g/L, 1.01 dS/m standard at 3.46 percent uncertainty instead of the 20 percent error of weighing 0.5 g directly — which separates all four stated crop thresholds (bean 1.0, tomato 2.5, wheat 6.0, barley 8.0 dS/m) for water and saturation extracts, though through a 1:1 soil extract the ±25.6 percent budget makes the wheat and barley bands overlap.

The Emptying Bucket: How Falling Head Drifts a Gravity Drip Kit's Flow, the Uniformity Bound It Implies, and the Constant-Head Fix Sized to Beat It

The result. For a stated 20-litre bucket kit on a 1.00 m stand with 60 emitters, head falls from 1.283 m to 1.014 m as the bucket empties and emitter flow falls 11.1 percent (0.716 to 0.637 L/h), while lateral friction costs almost nothing (first-to-last ratio 0.9996); the combined uniformity bound of 0.8887 fails the stated 0.90 criterion. The sized fix — a constant-head device holding 1.15 m within a 0.230 m band (the bucket's 0.269 m swing exceeds it by 0.039 m), with reservoir rule V ≥ 1.25 × Q × t (150.0 litres for a 10 mm watering) — restores the bound to 0.9996 at a constant 40.7 L/h.

The Cloche Bound: Night Temperature Protection Delivered by a Cut Plastic-Bottle Cloche and a Low Tunnel over a Young Tomato Transplant

The result. On a clear, calm night at an outside minimum of -2 degrees C, an exposed tomato transplant's canopy falls to -4.57 degrees C, while a cut PET bottle cloche holds it at 0.5 degrees C (a lift of 5.07 degrees C over the exposed plant, protecting through -2.77 degrees C and absorbing 7.07 frost degrees) and a polyethylene low tunnel holds it at only -1.44 degrees C (protecting through 0.29 degrees C, and through only 3.5 degrees C with a dry film); a closed cover in 600 W/m2 of sun rises about 35 degrees C above the outside air in the dry-soil bound, so daily venting is mandatory, and over 14 days the bottle keeps 25.9 litres of water per square metre in the soil against the tunnel's 19.7.

The Drying-Rack Bound: Mould-Risk Windows for Sun-Dried Chillies on the Ground, on a Raised Rack, and Under a Solar Cover

The result. In a stated fair spell, a simple solar cover dries chillies to safe moisture in 2.12 days with 33 hours in the mould-growth band — the only method inside a stated 72-hour risk criterion — while a 0.9 m rack takes 4.21 days and 77 band hours and the ground layer 8.29 days and 146 band hours; in a humid spell the open methods never finish because the air's equilibrium moisture (18 percent) exceeds the safe target, and in a stated wet spell (24 °C, 90 percent relative humidity, overcast) none of the three methods dries the crop safely, the cover stalling at 14.8 percent moisture, still inside the mould band.

What a Fizz Can Certify: Response Ranges, Colour Steps and Lime Misclassification Rates for Vinegar, Baking-Soda and Plant-Juice Soil Tests

The result. Computed from stated reaction and calibration assumptions, a clear vinegar fizz on a 10 g sample certifies only free carbonate at or above about 0.42 percent — and the popular rule "no fizz means lime" wrongly flags 65.9 percent of soils that need no lime — while a baking-soda fizz is clear only below pH 4.23 with an effective cut-off of pH 5.61 that wanders between 4.78 and 6.14, missing 25.7 percent of lime-needing soils at a pH 6.0 threshold and 44.2 percent at pH 6.5; red cabbage juice resolves pH in 1.0-unit bands (12.5 percent misclassified against a mid-band threshold) and turmeric answers only whether pH is above or below about 7.8, so none of the four can honestly carry a lime decision.

Frost Protection Priced in Water and Heat: Sprinklers, Fires, and Covers Against a Radiation Frost

The result. Against a stated radiation frost (75 W/m2 canopy loss, air to −3 °C), sprinkler protection needs 1.01 mm/h and 60.6 m3 per hectare over six calm hours, rising to 2.91 mm/h and 174.6 m3 per hectare under a wind and evaporation penalty; small fires deliver only 18.8 W/m2 at 50 fires per hectare and would need about 200 fires per hectare to match the loss, so the ring-of-fires remedy does not survive the arithmetic; a fleece cover gives about 2.0 °C of genuine but partial protection.

The Germination Test That Tells the Truth: How Many Seeds a Farmer Must Test Before a Result Can Be Trusted

The result. Exact Clopper–Pearson arithmetic shows that a perfect handful test certifies far less than it appears to: 10 of 10 germinating certifies only 69.2% true germination at 95% confidence, 20 of 20 certifies 83.2%, and 25 of 25 certifies 86.3%, while estimating germination within ±5 percentage points near the 85% threshold takes 196 seeds; a 10-seed test under an 85% pass rule passes a lot whose true germination is only 75% with probability 0.244, and the sowing-rate correction it actually justifies after 9 of 10 sprout is a factor of 1.80, not the naive 1.11.

The Intercrop Bound: Land Equivalent Ratio, the Density Penalty, and When Sole Cropping Wins for Maize and Bean

The result. For the stated maize–bean pair (sole yields 6.0 and 1.8 t/ha; intercrop yields 4.2 and 0.72 t/ha at relative densities 0.75 and 0.50), the land equivalent ratio is 1.10, so a farmer with a 200 m2 plot harvests 84.0 kg of maize and 14.4 kg of beans that sole cropping would need 220 m2 to match — but the cushion is thin (a further 0.6 t/ha fall in intercrop maize, to the 3.6 t/ha break-even, erases it), and the ratio falls to 0.92 with nitrogen applied and 0.90 in a dry season, where sole cropping wins, against 1.33 on low-fertility land without nitrogen.

The Jar Test Priced in Time and Error: Settling Times, Layer-Reading Errors, and the Protocol Stokes' Law Actually Supports for Soil Texture by Sedimentation

The result. From Stokes' law at 100 mm settling depth and 20 °C, the sand/silt boundary particle (50 micrometres) settles in 44.5 s but the silt/clay boundary particle (2 micrometres) needs 7.73 h and the finest clay 32.21 days, so clay can never be read as a layer; the customary readings overstate a loam's sand by 8.1 percentage points at 1 minute and its clay by 2.68 points at 2 hours, temperature alone swings the 2-hour clay error between +4.23 points at 10 °C and +1.38 points at 30 °C, and the protocol the arithmetic supports (sand at about 45 s, silt at 3.25 h, clay by difference) carries an honest worst-case clay precision of about ±11 percentage points.

The Mesh Exclusion Bound: The Aperture Each Garden Pest Requires, the Light and Air Penalty It Carries, and the Pests No Affordable Mesh Can Keep Out

The result. From stated pest body widths and woven-mesh geometry, the apertures required to exclude six garden pests run from 1.76 mm (cabbage moth) down to 0.176 mm (thrips), and light transmission falls from 69.2 percent to 30.8 percent across that range; four pests can be excluded at a stated lettuce yield price of 16.9 to 25.0 percent, but whitefly (0.32 mm, 41.3 percent light) and thrips (0.176 mm, 30.8 percent light, nightly canopy condensation) cannot be excluded by any mesh the crop can afford to grow under, because no aperture below 0.45 mm passes a 50 percent light test.

Water by the Pot: A Pan-Evaporation Scheduling Rule for Hand-Watered Plots That Stays Safe Across the Pan-Coefficient Band

The result. Scheduling a hand-watered plot by the middle of the pan-coefficient band (0.70, band 0.60–0.80) leaves the crop 11.25 stress days in a 90-day season whenever the true coefficient sits at the top of the band. The originated rule — water when cumulative pan readings reach a tabulated trigger (32.61 mm for loam at mid-season) and apply 0.875 of the readily available water (26.25 mm net, 30.88 mm gross on loam) — holds stress at zero across the whole band on sand, loam, and clay, at a worst cost of 3.75 mm drainage per watering on loam and about two extra waterings per season.

How Far Does One Buried Clay Pot Really Reach? A Wetted-Volume Bound for Pitcher Irrigation, and a Test of the Coverage Claims Made for It

The result. A 15-litre buried clay pot seeping 1.0 L/day wets a sphere of radius only 0.241 m in loam after 7 days (0.391 m after 30 days) and sustainably serves just 0.2 m² at 5.0 mm/day crop demand — so the circulating claims that one pot serves 4 m², six plants at 0.5 m spacing, or a 1 m × 1 m bed fail by factors of 20, 7.5, and 5.0 respectively; what survives is one plant planted against the pot (two within 0.3 m only at the top of the seepage range), with a 12.0-day refill interval.

The Habituation Bound: How Long a Static Bird Scarer Actually Protects a Ripening Crop, and Why No Rotation of Static Scarers Holds Deterrence for a Full Ripening Window

The result. Under a stated habituation model (effectiveness 0.85 at installation, decaying with a 3-day time constant towards a 0.05 floor), a static scarer on sweetcorn stays above a 0.50 protection threshold for only 1.73 of a 21-day ripening window and still loses 43.84 percent of the crop against 52.5 percent unprotected; changing to a different scarer type every 1.25 days is the best rotation found and still loses 22.35 percent, and no rotation of static scarers holds the threshold for the whole window.

The Dry-Enough Bound: Hours, Nights, and Mould Risk in Sun-Drying Maize Seed to Safe Storage Moisture

The result. Sun-drying maize seed from 20% to a safe 12% moisture takes 2.65 hours in a thin layer at 40% humidity but 8.11 hours in a 5 cm layer and 14.60 hours at 8 cm, and it cannot reach the target at all when daytime humidity exceeds 59.1%, because equilibrium moisture then sits above the target; one uncovered night returns 4.52 moisture points to finished seed against 0.33 points for a night indoors, and deep layers accumulate up to 5.6 hours in the stated mould-risk condition against 1.1 hours for a thin layer finished on the first morning.

A Viability Bound for Farm-Saved Maize Seed: Months Above Sowing Quality for Five Storage Methods, and the Moisture or Temperature Each Method Would Need

The result. Applying Harrington's rules of thumb in a probit viability model to maize starting at 96% germination, an open gourd or basket in a hot house stays above 85% germination for only 0.86 months, ash-covered seed for 4.85 months, a sealed tin for 8.32 months, a sealed bottle in a cool pit for 55.10 months, and a clay pot with desiccant for 60.93 months; a twelve-month life at sowing quality requires seed held at roughly 10% moisture or below at house temperatures (9.77% for the basket's temperatures, 10.24% for the tin).

The Shade Bound: Where Shade Cloth Stops Paying for Lettuce in Hot Climates

The result. For lettuce under stated assumptions, shade pays only where severe heat and surplus light coincide: in a hot, bright season (DLI 45 mol/m2/d, peak air 38 °C) yield peaks at 0.463 at 40% shade versus 0.409 unshaded, while 70% shade falls back to 0.414; in a hot cloudy season the optimum is about 25%, and in mild or cool seasons no shade fraction pays, with 70% shade costing 26.8% of yield at peak air 28 °C.

The Spacing Bound: Where Planting Lettuce Closer Stops Paying, and the Spacing Rule That Follows

The result. Under the stated reciprocal-yield relation for headed lettuce (1/w = 3.33 + 0.20N under standard water), total yield per square metre rises at every density toward a 5.00 kg ceiling that takes 316.3 plants per square metre to approach within 5 percent, but marketable yield at a 0.12 kg grade peaks at 25 plants per square metre (3.001 kg per square metre), giving a spacing rule of 20.0 cm under standard water, 24.5 cm when dry, and 17.3 cm when irrigated; sowing at 100 plants per square metre wastes an extra USD 1.67 per square metre of seed on heads averaging 0.043 kg.

The Windbreak Bound: When a Shelter Strip Returns More Than the Land It Takes

The result. For a stated 5 m barrier costing an effective 4.25 m of field length, break-even is set mainly by porosity and wind climate, not field size: a dense barrier (porosity 0.2, shelter to 118 m) pays once damaging days exceed about 17% of the season on a 100 m field and gains 3.9% of yield at a damaging-day fraction of 0.30, a porosity-0.5 barrier needs about 29% damaging days, and a porosity-0.8 barrier needs an unrealistic 63–65% and loses yield at every field size computed.
