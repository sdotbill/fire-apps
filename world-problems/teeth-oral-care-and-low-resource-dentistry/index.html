When a Toothbrush Stops Cleaning: A Splay-Based Replacement Rule and Its Cost per Effective Brush

The result. Toothbrush replacement is usually governed by habit or by a calendar printed on packaging, not by how well the brush still cleans. This study models plaque-removal efficacy as falling linearly by 0.045 per week of twice-daily use from an initial index of 1.00, and computes end-of-life and mean efficacy, brushes per year, and annual cost at a bulk price of USD 0.60 for replacement intervals from 2 to 16 weeks. Efficacy first falls below 0.70 at 6.67 weeks. The longest interval whose mean efficacy over the brush's life stays at or above 0.85 is 6 weeks, using 8.67 brushes a year at USD 5.20. An 8-week interval lowers the cost to USD 3.90 with a mean efficacy of 0.82, while a 12-week interval gives a mean efficacy of only 0.73 at USD 2.60. The recommended rule is replacement at about six weeks on performance grounds, with eight weeks as a stated budget compromise. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

A Smear, a Pea, or a Ribbon: Fluoride Dose per Brushing for Young Children and the Dispensing Rule That Keeps It in Range

The result. Advice on how much toothpaste a young child should use is given in pictures, a smear or a pea, without stating the dose those pictures deliver. This study computes fluoride applied and swallowed for three dispensing amounts (smear 0.10 g, pea 0.25 g, full ribbon 0.60 g), two paste strengths (1000 and 1450 ppm), and two age bands, with stated swallowed fractions and a stated upper intake criterion of 0.10 mg per kg per day. For ages two to three at 1450 ppm, a ribbon applies 0.87 mg per brushing and brings total daily intake to 0.822 mg, 68.5 percent of the upper criterion, while a smear of 1000 ppm paste brings it to only 0.36 mg, 30.0 percent. For ages four to five a pea of 1450 ppm paste totals 0.409 mg per day, 22.7 percent of the criterion. The dispensing rule that follows is a smear for ages two to three and a pea for ages four to five. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

How Much Floss Is Enough: The Handling Length That Cleans Every Contact and the Waste in the Standard Instruction

The result. The standard floss instruction, to take about 45 cm, is tested against the geometry of the task it describes. Cleaning 26 interdental contacts with a fresh 2.5 cm segment for each, plus 8 cm for each of two hand wraps, requires 81.0 cm of floss. The standard 45 cm leaves 29.0 cm of fresh working length after the wraps, enough for 11 contacts with a fresh segment, leaving 15 contacts to be cleaned with a reused segment or not cleaned. Annual cost at the required length is USD 5.91 for daily use at a stated price of USD 0.02 per metre. A tied loop or a holder, which removes the wrap length, is identified as the practical route to complete cleaning. The standard instruction, as written, does not deliver the complete cleaning it promises. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

Brushing with a Cup Instead of a Running Tap: Water Saved per Person and the Rinse Volume That Still Clears Paste

The result. Toothbrushing advice assumes a running tap and never prices the water. This study compares a running-tap routine (6 litres per minute for 45 seconds, 4.5 litres per brushing) with a single 200 mL cup, and checks the cup against a stated dilution model of paste clearance in which the residual fraction after rinsing with volume V is exp(-V/60). The cup saves 4.3 litres per brushing, 3139.0 litres per person per year at two brushings daily, and 15695 litres per year for a household of five. The volume needed to reach a 5 percent residual is 179.7 mL, and a 200 mL cup leaves a residual of 3.57 percent. The cup method is a large saving with no modelled hygiene penalty. Sizing Interdental Brushes by Passage Diameter: A Selection Rule That Covers the Spaces a Single Size Misses

The result. Interdental brushes are commonly supplied in a single size, as if all interdental spaces were alike. This study models 26 spaces with stated passage diameters (eight at 0.6 mm, eight at 0.8 mm, six at 1.0 mm, four at 1.3 mm) and a stated fit rule under which a brush cleans a space when its diameter is at least the passage diameter and at most 0.4 mm larger. No single size cleans more than 61.5 percent of the spaces; the best single size, 0.8 mm, covers 16 of 26. The smallest set covering all 26 spaces contains two sizes, 0.8 and 1.3 mm. The sizing rule that follows is to gauge the tightest and widest spaces and stock the two sizes that span them. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

Miswak or Manufactured Brush: Cost per Year of Effective Cleaning Where Brushes and Paste Are Hard to Afford

The result. Miswak, the chewing stick of Salvadora persica, is widely described as the economical alternative to a manufactured brush and paste. This study tests that description at stated prices: a stick at USD 0.15 replaced weekly, against a brush at USD 0.60 replaced every eight weeks plus toothpaste at USD 0.80 per 100 g tube consumed in 100 days. A year of purchased miswak costs USD 7.80; a year of brush and paste costs USD 6.82. Dividing by stated relative efficacy (brush and paste 1.00, miswak five times daily 0.92, miswak twice daily 0.78) gives costs per unit of efficacy of USD 6.82, USD 8.48, and USD 10.00 respectively. Purchased miswak is therefore not the cheaper system; it is cheaper only where sticks are gathered at no cash cost. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

One Varnish Vial, How Many Children: The Dose per Child That Covers the Teeth Without Emptying the Vial Early

The result. Fluoride varnish in school programmes is usually dispensed as one unit-dose vial per child, so the package sets the dose. This study models a 0.40 mL vial of 5 percent sodium fluoride varnish (22.6 mg fluoride per mL), a stated coating need of 0.20 mL for a full primary dentition, and 15 percent applicator waste, and computes fluoride applied and children served per ten vials at doses of 0.20 to 0.40 mL. At the full-vial dose of 0.40 mL, ten vials serve 10 children and apply 7.68 mg of fluoride each. At a pooled dose of 0.25 mL, ten vials serve 16 children and apply 4.80 mg each, still above the modelled coating need after waste. Dose should be set by the dentition, not by the vial. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

A Level Teaspoon in a Cup of Water: The Actual Salt Concentration of the Standard Aftercare Rinse and a More Repeatable Recipe

The result. Patients recovering from a tooth extraction are told to rinse with a teaspoon of salt in a cup of warm water, an instruction whose terms are undefined. This study computes the concentration of six common versions using stated spoon masses (half teaspoon 3.0 g, level teaspoon 6.0 g, heaped teaspoon 9.0 g) and vessel volumes of 200, 250, and 350 mL, against the isotonic reference of 0.9 percent and a stated tolerable band of 0.7 to 1.2 percent. The six versions produce 1.2, 2.4, 3.0, 3.6, 1.71, and 1.5 percent; only one, the half teaspoon in 250 mL at 1.2 percent, falls inside the band. A proposed recipe, one level teaspoon in a standard 500 mL bottle, gives 1.2 percent with the volume fixed by the bottle. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

Steam Sterilisation for a Small Dental Clinic: Cycle Capacity, Daily Throughput, and the Instrument Sets That Remove the Bottleneck

The result. In a small dental clinic the steriliser, not the clinician, can set the pace of the day. This study models a benchtop steam steriliser holding six instrument sets per 45-minute cycle across an eight-hour day, and derives both daily capacity and the stock of sets a clinic must own to keep chairs working. Capacity is ten cycles and 60 sets per day, which meets demand at four, six, and eight patients per hour. The binding constraint is stock: the rotation rule of chamber load plus sets in use during one cycle plus two in preparation gives 13 complete sets at six patients per hour and 14 at eight. Energy use is 12.0 kWh per day at a cost of USD 2.40. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

Light for Looking in Mouths: Illuminance at Working Distance, Battery Life, and the Cheapest Headlamp Class That Meets an Examination Threshold

The result. Examiners working without a dental operating light rely on headlamps sold by lumen ratings that do not state the illuminance at the mouth. This study converts three lamp classes (100, 200, and 400 lumens, priced at USD 6, 12, and 25 as stated values) into illuminance at a 0.35 m working distance, assuming the output is focused into a 0.20 m spot with a stated optical efficiency of 0.70, and compares the result with a stated examination threshold of 10,000 lux. The classes deliver 2228, 4456, and 8913 lux respectively: none meets the threshold. Meeting it in the same spot requires about 449 lumens delivered to that spot. Lamp purchases for examination work should therefore be specified by spot illuminance at working distance, not by lumens. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

Hand-Mixed or Capsule: Glass Ionomer Waste and Restorations per Kit in Atraumatic Restorative Treatment

The result. Atraumatic restorative treatment depends on glass ionomer, bought either as powder and liquid for hand mixing or as pre-dosed capsules. This study compares the two routes at stated values: a restoration needs 0.20 g of mixed material; hand mixing prepares 0.35 g per attempt with 12 percent of mixes discarded for ratio failure; a capsule delivers 0.40 g with 2 percent failure. A 50 g hand-mix kit at USD 28 yields about 126 successful restorations at USD 0.22 each, while a box of 50 capsules at USD 42 yields 49 at USD 0.86 each. Hand mixing wastes 42.9 percent of its material as over-mix. Capsules buy consistency, not economy. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

Half a Tablet Cleans as the Label Fears It Might Not: Concentration, Soak Time, and Cost in Denture Cleansing

The result. Denture cleansing tablets are used one per soak by label instruction, and halving them is a common household economy of unknown effect. This study evaluates dose and soak time together under a stated concentration-time criterion of 3.0 normalised units, where a whole tablet in 150 mL defines a concentration of 1.00 and a half tablet 0.50. A whole tablet reaches the criterion at 30 minutes (3.0 units); a half tablet reaches it at 60 minutes (3.0 units) and exceeds it eightfold overnight at 480 minutes (24.0 units). Halving the tablet for overnight soaking saves USD 21.90 per wearer per year at a stated tablet price of USD 0.12. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

Drying Matters More Than Disinfectant: Spacing and Airflow Rules for Storing Forty Toothbrushes in a Classroom

The result. School toothbrushing programmes store dozens of brushes between sessions, and guidance often reaches for disinfectant soaking where the underlying problem is that brushes stay wet and touching. This study models drying under stated assumptions: a shaken brush head carries 2.0 mL of water, evaporation proceeds at 0.020 mL per minute with heads separated and at half that rate with heads touching, and the dry target is a residual of 0.2 mL. Separated brushes reach the target in 90.0 minutes; touching brushes need 180.0 minutes. A four-row rack at 25 mm spacing stores forty brushes in rows 250 mm long. Spacing, a one-time construction decision, replaces a recurring disinfectant cost. This study is proven in simulation. Proven in simulation.

Charcoal Toothpaste and the Whitening Claim: Abrasive Wear per Shade Gained and the Fluoride the Claim Leaves Out

The result. Charcoal toothpastes are sold on a whitening claim. This study tests the claim against a stated saturating model of stain removal, in which any paste above an abrasivity index of 60 reaches the same shade gain of 1.90 shades at four weeks, and a stated wear model in which annual abrasive wear is proportional to the abrasivity index. At stated indices of 120 for a charcoal paste, 70 for a standard fluoride paste, and 90 for a peroxide whitening paste, all three reach the same 1.90 shade gain, while the charcoal paste's annual wear is 87600 normalised units against 51100 for the standard paste, an excess of 71.0 percent. The charcoal formulation examined also carries 0 ppm fluoride against the standard paste's 1450 ppm. The distinctive element of the charcoal product is its wear, not its whitening.
