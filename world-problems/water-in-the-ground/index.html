An Aquifer-Safe Draw Bound for a Village Well Field: Why Recharge, Not Drawdown, Sets the Rate

The result. Drawdown allows 796.9 m3/day per well at 150 m spacing, but recharge at 100 mm/year sustains only 16.44 m3/day per well; at zero recharge every cubic metre is mined from storage.

Storing Water in the Valley Fill Itself: Storage and Seepage-Bypass Bounds for a Subsurface Dam Across a Buried Valley

The result. The stated valley fill stores 69,120 m3 of drainable water; seepage bypass over 180 days falls from 1.1% of storage with no key to about 0.1% at full key, with half key already at 0.5%.

A Car Battery Is Not a Resistivity Meter: The Contact-Resistance Bound on Wenner Sounding in Dry Soil

The result. A 12 V car battery injects only 1.19 mA at 5,000 ohm contact resistance in dry soil, giving 1.9 mV at 20 m Wenner spacing and a maximum usable spacing of 3.8 m — blind below about 2 m depth. Treating electrodes to 500 ohms restores a usable spacing of 36.4 m.

Deepening a Hand-Dug Well in the Dry Season, Priced as a Gamble: The Yield Bought per Metre and the Depth at Which It Stops Paying

The result. Deepening buys 2.5 m3/day per metre; two metres restores 3.42 m3/day at day 180, but each metre nets -7.5 dollars against one season's water value, and work is defensible only below the 12 m collapse-risk threshold.

What a Hammer and a Telephone Can Resolve: Timing Bounds for Seismic Refraction over a Weathered Aquifer

The result. With 1 ms first-break picking, hammer refraction resolves a weathered layer as thin as 0.41 m and a 5 m thickness to about 0.2 m, but a 10 m target needs about 35 m of spread. A low-velocity layer beneath a faster one produces no refraction and is invisible at any spread length.

A Step-Drawdown Test a Village Can Actually Run: Separating Well Loss from Aquifer Loss with a Fifty-Two-Dollar Kit, and the Error Budget That Decides When It Cannot

The result. Four 60-minute steps with a US$52 airline-gauge kit recover B = 1.8 m per (L/s) and C = 0.9 m per (L/s)^2, giving a sustainable rate of 1.33 L/s under a two-part rule; a 1.5 L/s demand fails on efficiency (57%) despite passing on drawdown. The Monte Carlo error budget (B sd 0.21, C sd 0.22, correlation -0.90, sustainable-rate sd 0.35 L/s) and the twin-well demonstration (opposite failures identical at one rate, separated by 37 standard deviations at the top step) state plainly when the cheap test cannot distinguish a failing well from a failing aquifer.

Hand-Pump Cup-Seal Wear Priced per Cubic Metre: The Sand Content at Which a Seal Design Change Is Forced

The result. At 761 strokes per cubic metre, seal cost rises from 0.019 dollars per m3 in clean water to 0.209 dollars per m3 at 500 mg/L sand, where replacement falls below a monthly interval and a seal design change is forced.

Ninety-Five Per Cent Is a Distance: Spare-Parts Travel, Maintenance Funds, and the Availability of a Village Hand Pump

The result. At a 180-day failure interval, 95 percent availability allows a 9.47-day repair time, crossed at about 80 km parts distance without local stock; a fund of about 203 USD per year covers repairs at 20 km. Village parts stocking raises availability to 0.997, while downtime at 80 km costs about 366 USD per year in collection time.

The Saline-Upconing Bound Beneath a Coastal Pumping Well: The Rate at Which a Village Well Salts Itself, and the Rate That Holds the Interface Down

The result. For the stated coastal well (K = 5 m/day, screen 12 m above the interface) the critical rate is 0.393 L/s and the safe operating bound is 0.196 L/s, which still meets the full demand of 5,000 L/day in 7.1 pumping hours. The installed 1.0 L/s pump runs at 2.55 times the critical rate and will salt the well; two wells at the safe rate deliver 11,310 L in 8 hours.

The Rope Pump Priced from First Principles: A Head-Flow Bound, a Worked Sizing, and the Depth at Which It Loses

The result. From 75 W of human power, a rope pump delivers 46.4 L/min at 5 m, 21.4 at 10 m, 9.0 at 20 m and 5.1 at 30 m; a piston pump delivers more at every head. The rope pump's practical crossover is about 27-30 m, where its efficiency falls to 0.35.

The Water Tank as the Battery: Hydraulic Storage Sizing for a Solar Borehole Pump Against the Electrical-Battery Alternative

The result. Three days of autonomy costs 2,700 dollars as a 30 m3 tank against 5,129 dollars as batteries; a mean-sized 0.65 kWp array delivers only 5.82 m3/day in the worst month, a shortfall of 4.18 m3/day.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24113407

Hand-Augering Priced as a Bound: Metres per Crew-Day and Cost per Metre Across Four Formations, and Why the Method Does Not Work in Laterite or Weathered Rock

The result. Sand (18.2 N m required torque) advances 6.0 m per crew-day and reaches water in 3.0 days at US$3.73/m; clay (45.6 N m) advances 3.6 m per crew-day at US$6.16/m. Laterite (109.3 N m) and weathered rock (273.3 N m) exceed the 60 N m sustained human torque limit by factors of 1.8 and 4.6, so the method does not work in those formations at any season length; rod-string losses also cap useful depth near 30 m.

The Suction Ceiling: Altitude, Temperature, and the Two-Stage Answer for the Treadle Pump

The result. Maximum suction lift is 9.39 m at sea level with cool water and 6.87 m at 2,000 m altitude with warm water; 100 W of leg power gives 152.9 L/min at 2 m total head falling to 30.6 at 10 m. Two-stage pumping buys back suction geometry but does not change the power bound.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24113419

Holding the Rate: A Clogging Model, a Scraping Cycle, and a Sized Infiltration Basin for Managed Aquifer Recharge

The result. With infiltration decaying from 0.50 toward 0.05 m/day, a 30-day scraping cycle nets 0.213 m/day, so a basin of about 141 m2 holds a 30 m3/day recharge target over a 90-day wet season, banking 2,700 m3. The scheme does not work on clay floors, over water tables within about two metres, or under heavy sediment loads.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24113421

A Protected Spring Capture Sized from Its Own Hydrograph: Night Storage for the Morning Peak, and the Dry-Season Flow at Which a Borehole Wins

The result. A spring recessing from 1.2 to 0.459 L/s over 120 days serves 300 people (6,000 L/day) with a worst-day supply margin of 6.62x; the one-hour morning peak requires 1,046 L of standing storage on the final day, met by a 2,000 L tank in a US$1,450 scheme delivering at US$0.056 per 1,000 L against a borehole's US$0.239. The binding crossover is a service limit at a dry-season minimum flow of about 0.076 L/s, below which overnight flow cannot fill the storage the morning peak requires.

The Depth Crossover Between Hand-Dug Wells and Drilled Boreholes: Cost per Reliable Litre Against Water-Table Depth, and the Pump-Class Step at the Suction Limit

The result. At US$6/day labour, cost per reliable litre over 15 years favours the hand-dug well down to a crossover at 27.2 m (US$0.087 vs US$0.313 per 1,000 L at 3 m; US$1.115 vs US$0.519 at 40 m), moving to 29.7 m at US$3/day and 23.7 m at US$12/day. Crossing the 7 m suction limit raises dug-well cost from US$0.109 to US$0.194 per 1,000 L across 0.2 m of depth, entirely from the pump-class change.
