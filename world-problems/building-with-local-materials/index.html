Building with local materials

Walls, beams, joints and roofs from bamboo, timber, stone and earth, each used only within its computed strength.

Lime and Ash: What a Wood-Ash Pozzolan Mortar Can and Cannot Do

The result. Per 100 kg, wood ash carries only 3.0 kg of reactive silica against rice husk ash's 77.44 kg. Wood ash with water alone forms at most 8.73 kg of gel per 100 kg (0.78 MPa dry, 0.08 MPa wet), and wood ash with clay at 50:50 gives 0.49 MPa dry and 0.02 MPa wet, so neither belief survives the arithmetic. The originated rule — lime and rice husk ash at 1:2, moist-cured — honestly claims M2.5 at 2.96 MPa after 28 days, and neither mortar is credited for below-ground or standing-water use.

How Far Can a Bamboo Culm Span? Bending Strength and Deflection Bounds for Single and Bundled Culms

The result. Deflection at span/240 governs every case computed; bending strength never does. A single 100 mm culm under 250 N/m spans 3.96 m by deflection against 7.89 m by strength; bundling two culms reaches 4.89 m and three reach 5.48 m at the same load, and a 1,000 N midspan point load bounds the single 100 mm culm to 3.23 m.

The Bamboo Column Bound: Safe Axial Load by Culm Diameter, Wall Thickness, and Node Spacing

The result. Safe axial load is the smaller of factored Euler buckling and crushing, with diameter and wall taken at mid-height: a 100 mm culm with a 10 mm wall carries 62.2 kN at 1.0 m unsupported length, 38.6 kN at 2.0 m, and 17.2 kN at 3.0 m. Local wall buckling never governs, and nodes do not shorten the column — an internode segment's Euler load is 2,896.1 kN against 51.5 kN for the whole 3.0 m culm — so only real bracing raises the load.

A Bolt Through a Bamboo Culm Does Not Use the Culm's Strength: Splitting and Bearing Bounds at Pinned Joints, and a Lashing Rule That Does

The result. A 12 mm through-bolt in a 100 mm culm with a 10 mm wall reaches an ultimate bearing load of 8.4 kN, which is 5.4% of the culm's crushing strength, and a safe transfer of only 2.8 kN. The selection rule that follows: a single lashing to 3.75 kN, a double lashing to 6.75 kN, a plugged bolt to 12.0 kN, and beyond that multiple joints rather than a stronger fastener.

The Dry-Stone Retaining Wall Bound: Batter, Base Thickness, and the Height a Vertical Face Cannot Reach

The result. A vertical dry-stone face needs a base of 0.58 times the retained height; a 1-in-6 batter needs 0.538 times the height with about a fifth less stone. At equal stone volume and 3.0 m retained height, the battered wall's overturning factor is 3.857 against the vertical wall's 3.010, a 28.1% gain; under a 1.50 m base cap the battered face reaches 2.75 m, a height the vertical face cannot reach.

Foundation Width from the Ground Up: A Bearing Rule for Stone Strip Footings

The result. Wall, roof, and floor loads are accumulated openly — 35.16 kN/m for a cottage, 71.82 kN/m for a two-storey stone house — and the footing width is the larger of the bearing width and wall thickness plus 0.30 m. On stiff clay and firmer, geometry alone sets the width; on soft clay at 50 kPa a two-storey stone house demands 1.783–2.003 m against stepped maxima of 1.35–1.40 m, so a stepped stone strip footing cannot spread that load there.

The Honest Load of a Green-Timber Lintel: Strength Loss and Creep in Unseasoned Wood

The result. Strength alone requires giving up 30.0–40.0% by species class when timber goes in green, but with green stiffness loss and drying-under-load creep the honest give-up for ordinary openings is 51.2–59.5%: a pine 100×200 mm lintel over 1.8 m carries 9.36 kN/m green against 21.95 kN/m seasoned, and green sag grows to 3.0 times its initial value over the years against 1.6 times seasoned.

Lashed, Pegged, or Notched: Computed Strengths for Timber Joints Without Metal, and a Selection Rule

The result. A 10-turn lashing on a 100 mm member resists 2.1 kN in slip and 4.2 kN in tension; a single 25 mm peg carries 4.42 kN in single shear and 8.84 kN in double shear, governed by peg shear rather than bearing; a quarter-depth notch retains 56.2% of bending capacity. The selection rule assigns notches, pegs, and lashings by load case, and names the case none of them serves — transferring a substantial bending moment — where the builder must change the geometry instead.

The Round-Log Beam Rule: Span from Diameter for Roof and Floor Loads

The result. Sag governs in all 54 combinations of diameter, species class, and load case computed; strength-only sizing overspans by roughly a factor of two. A 200 mm pine-class log has a 9.23 m strength span but governs at 4.64 m on a floor and 5.02 m on a roof, and the oak class at the same diameter governs at 4.95 m — only 1.18 times the light-softwood span, because stiffness, not strength, binds.

Mud Brick and Stabilised Earth: Strength by Mix, and the Least Cement or Lime That Does the Work

The result. Wet strength, not dry strength, sets almost every least stabiliser dose: unstabilised mixes reach 0.96–1.92 MPa dry but only 0.10–0.19 MPa wet. One storey needs 1.5–3.5% cement or lime in every soil; two storeys need 3.0% cement in loam against 5.5% lime, and expansive heavy clay fails the two-storey threshold at the 8.0% cap under either stabiliser, though it passes one storey at 3.5%.

Scaffolds and Formwork from Poles: Safe Loads, Bracing, and the Bay Size That Holds

The result. A braced 90 mm upright at a 2.0 m lift carries 23.84 kN, falling to 15.26 kN at 2.5 m and to 5.96 kN unbraced, so bracing on both faces is a structural necessity. Ledger bending governs the bay: 1.52 m, 1.35 m, and 1.13 m for light, medium, and heavy load classes with a 90 mm ledger. A 90 mm formwork prop at a 2.0 m lift may carry at most 3.51 m² of a 0.20 m wet slab.

Roof Trusses from Poles: Geometry and Member Sizes for Spans from Four to Eight Metres

The result. For a king-post pole truss with two struts, solved exactly by joint equilibrium and sized against buckling, combined compression and bending, and joint capacities, the rafters govern: 90 mm under thatch at 4 m span to 165 mm under tile at 8 m, while the tie needs 60–90 mm and the king post passes at the 60 mm floor throughout. The form stops working under tile between 9.0 m and 9.5 m span and under thatch between 11.5 m and 12.0 m.

Rammed Earth by Layers: Lift Thickness, Compaction Energy, and the Slenderness Bound

The result. At 350 kJ/m³ of compaction energy, strength falls from 2.12 MPa at 100 mm lifts to 1.58 MPa at 200 mm lifts, with a moisture optimum of 10.0%. The originated rule is lifts of 100–120 mm, a 0.60 m pour height, and an 8–12% moisture window. Slenderness, not crushing, governs height: maximum unsupported height is 3.6 m at 300 mm thickness, 5.4 m at 450 mm, and 7.2 m at 600 mm.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24115382

How Long Does Timber Take to Dry? A Diffusion Bound Tested Against the One-Month-Per-Inch Rule

The result. Drying time scales with the square of thickness, so both linear rules of thumb fail: one month per inch understates thick refractory stock (a 150 mm board to a 15% core target has a 27.77-month bound against the rule's 5.91 months) and overstates thin permeable stock, while one year per inch exceeds the bound everywhere. The corrected scheduling rule is minimum months = C × (thickness in inches)², with C tabulated by permeability class and moisture target.

How Thick Must a Rubble Stone Wall Be? A Stability Bound by Height and Mortar

The result. The middle-third resultant check, not overturning or sliding, sizes a rubble stone wall: a free-standing dry wall needs 0.40 m at 1.0 m height, 0.64 m at 3.0 m, and 0.905 m at 6.0 m, against 0.30 m, 0.41 m, and 0.58 m mortared. Unmortared rubble stops working at 5.95 m under a 0.90 m practical thickness cap, and at 2.65 m at the common 0.60 m thickness.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24115422

Termites and Damp: The Detailing Limits That Decide How Long a Timber Building Stands

The result. Modelled time to structural loss of a 150 mm post runs from 4.3 years for the least durable class in the ground in a wet climate to beyond the 50-year design life on a ventilated, shielded 450 mm plinth. The charcoal and ash belief does not survive: a 75 mm layer is crossed by a mud tube in 3.0 days at the stated build rate, and even a perfectly intact layer extends a ground post's life only from 12.5 to 14.5 years. A minimum detailing standard by climate band follows.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24115440

Thatch Against Rain and Rot: Pitch, Thickness, and Service Life by Material and Climate

The result. Most dry and temperate combinations are governed by a shedding floor — 45° for long straw, 40° for reed, 35° for palm — but rain penetration governs thin wet-tropical coats: 200 mm palm needs 57.2°, and 150 mm long straw and 150 mm palm in a wet tropical climate fail at any pitch up to 60°. Modelled service lives at standard thicknesses span 4.4 years for unsmoked palm in a wet tropical climate to 61.1 years for smoked reed in a temperate one.
