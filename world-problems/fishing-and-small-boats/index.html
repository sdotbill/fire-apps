A Salt-Penetration Rule for Whole and Split Fish: Salting Times by Thickness, the Spoilage Thickness Limit, and Dry Salting Compared with Brining

The result. Salt preserves fish only where the salt has actually arrived, and the last place it reaches is the centre of the thickest part. This paper models salt uptake as one-dimensional diffusion into a slab of fish flesh, with an effective diffusivity of 3.0e-10 m2/s as a stated assumption, and computes the time for the centre to reach 10 percent salt in the water phase, a stated keeping target. Dry salting, with a saturated surface film at 26 percent, and brining in a stated 15 percent brine are computed as separate cases throughout. Salting time grows with the square of the half-thickness, so splitting a fish open, which halves its thickness, cuts the curing time by a factor of 4.0 exactly in this model. Against a stated spoilage clock of 36 hours for an unsalted centre at 25 degrees C, a whole fish thicker than 2.30 cm dry salted, or 1.69 cm brined, has a centre that spoils before the salt arrives; splitting raises those limits to 4.60 cm and 3.38 cm of original thickness. A 4 cm whole fish needs 109.0 hours dry salted and 201.2 hours in brine, against 27.2 hours dry salted once split. Brining at 15 percent takes 1.846 times as long as dry salting to reach the same centre target, because its surface concentration is lower. The study is proven in simulation, and its limits are upper bounds on safe practice, for reasons set out in the Limitations.

A Waiting Rule for Newly Anchored Fish Aggregating Devices: How Long a New Device Must Stand Before Fishing It, with a Mooring Scope and Anchor Table

The result. A fish aggregating device, a moored raft or buoy under which fish gather, does not fish at full strength on the day it is anchored. This paper models the build-up of the aggregation as an approach to a settled level with a time constant of 7 days, a stated assumption informed qualitatively by published accounts of aggregation behaviour, and turns that curve into a waiting rule. A new device reaches 50 percent of its settled catch rate after 4.85 days, 80 percent after 11.27 days, 90 percent after 16.12 days, and 95 percent after 20.97 days. The recommended rule is therefore to let a new device stand for about 16 days before fishing it in earnest if 90 percent of the settled rate is wanted, or about 21 days for 95 percent. Fishing every day from day 1 at a settled rate of 40 kg per day, taken as a stated assumption, returns a mean of only 41.2 percent of the settled rate across the first week and a cumulative shortfall of 22.8 percent across the first 28 days, not because waiting creates fish, but because early effort is spent at low rates. Alongside the waiting rule, the paper originates a mooring scope rule in which the required scope ratio grows with surface current, from 1.312 at 0.5 knots to 1.95 at 2.0 knots, and tabulates rode length and concrete anchor mass by depth and current: at 200 m depth and 1.0 knots the rode is 300.0 m and the computed minimum concrete anchor is 187.3 kg in air, rising to 915.9 kg at 2.0 knots. The study is proven in simulation, and the anchor figures are steady-current minima that exclude wave and storm loading.

An Ice-Planning Rule for Small Fishing Trips: Ice Needed to Chill the Catch and to Offset Heat Leak, and the Trip Length at Which a 1:2 Ice-to-Fish Ratio Runs Out

The result. How much ice should a small fishing boat carry? Guidance is usually given as a single ratio of ice to fish, with no account taken of how warm the day is or how long the trip lasts. This paper separates the two jobs ice has to do — chilling the catch from its loading temperature to about 0 °C, and replacing the ice melted by heat leaking through the box walls during the trip — and combines them into a single planning rule for a reference insulated box (50 mm expanded polystyrene walls, 100 kg of fish). The rule is R = 0.01078 × T + 0.00706 × T × t, where R is kilograms of ice per kilogram of fish, T is ambient temperature in °C and t is trip length in days. Chilling alone consumes 0.216 kg of ice per kilogram of fish at 20 °C and 0.377 kg/kg at 35 °C. Heat leak melts a further 14.12 kg of ice per day per 100 kg of fish at 20 °C, rising to 24.72 kg per day at 35 °C. A commonly carried ratio of 1:2 ice to fish (0.50 kg/kg) is therefore exhausted after 48.3 hours at 20 °C, 20.0 hours at 30 °C and only 11.9 hours at 35 °C; indeed, at 35 °C a trip of just 12 hours already requires 0.501 kg/kg, slightly more than the whole 1:2 allowance. A two-day trip at 30 °C requires 0.747 kg/kg, and a five-day trip at 35 °C requires 1.613 kg/kg. A fixed ratio cannot be right at all temperatures and trip lengths; the rule and table given here replace it with arithmetic a skipper can do before loading. The study is proven in simulation.

Sizing the Walls of a Low-Cost Insulated Fish Box: Expanded Polystyrene versus a Sawdust-Filled Double Wooden Wall

The result. An insulated fish box is usually built with whatever sheet insulation is at hand, at whatever thickness fits, and its performance is discovered on the first hot trip. This paper instead sizes the walls from a stated duty: a box of fixed external size 1.00 m × 0.60 m × 0.60 m must hold 40 kg of fish, loaded at 5 °C, with a 25 kg ice charge, at or below 4 °C for 48 hours at an ambient temperature of 35 °C. Because the contents remain at about 0 °C while any ice remains, the duty reduces to one condition — the ice must not be exhausted — and the wall thickness follows from a conduction balance. Two low-cost constructions are sized. Expanded polystyrene sheet (thermal conductivity 0.035 W/(m·K)) requires a wall 69.5 mm thick, which in practice means building to 70 mm; it leaves an internal volume of 182.9 litres, 50.8 percent of the 360-litre gross volume. A double wooden box with dry sawdust fill (0.09 W/(m·K)) requires 136.2 mm, built to 140 mm, and leaves only 78.1 litres — 21.7 percent of gross — which is barely more than the 76.5 litres the fish and ice themselves occupy, a spare volume of just 1.6 litres. If the fish are loaded warm at 30 °C instead of 5 °C, the sawdust design ceases to be workable inside the same envelope: it would need 190.5 mm walls and its 29.7-litre interior could not hold the load. The paper gives the sizing rule, a thickness table across trip lengths and temperatures, and the volume each wall costs, so a builder can choose with the trade-off in front of them. The study is proven in simulation.

A No-Ice Working Clock for Landed Fish: Hours to the Quality Limit across Temperatures, and the Extension from Wet Sacking in Shade

The result. Where ice is unavailable, unaffordable, or exhausted, a seller's practical question is simply: how long have I got? This paper builds a working clock for that question. Taking a reference shelf life on ice of 12 days (288 hours) at 0 °C to the limit of acceptable quality, and a Q10 of 2.5 for the temperature dependence of spoilage, the remaining saleable time is 182.1 hours at 5 °C, 115.2 hours at 10 °C, 72.9 hours at 15 °C, 46.1 hours at 20 °C, 29.1 hours at 25 °C, 18.4 hours at 30 °C and 11.7 hours at 35 °C. The clock therefore runs about 15.62 times faster than on ice at 30 °C and 24.71 times faster at 35 °C: a catch landed in the morning in hot weather reaches the quality limit the same evening. The paper then evaluates the oldest no-ice remedy, covering the boxes with wet sacking in shade so that evaporation holds the fish near the wet-bulb temperature. The benefit depends entirely on humidity. In arid air at 35 °C with a wet-bulb depression of 9 °C, the window lengthens from 11.7 to 26.6 hours, an extension of 14.9 hours. In temperate summer air at 24 °C with a depression of 4 °C, it lengthens from 31.9 to 46.1 hours, an extension of 14.1 hours. In humid tropical air at 32 °C with a depression of only 2 °C, it lengthens from 15.3 to 18.4 hours — an extension of just 3.1 hours, helpful for reaching a market but not for holding a catch overnight. Wet sacking is worth doing in dry air and is a weak defence exactly where heat and humidity combine. The study is proven in simulation.

The Escape Length of a Stretched Mesh: An Inscribed-Circle Rule Linking Mesh Size, Opening Shape, and Fish Girth

The result. Fishermen and gear designers routinely need to answer a plain question: given a netting mesh of a stated size, what is the largest fish that will still get through it, and what is the smallest fish that will be held? This paper originates a simple geometric rule for that question. A mesh is treated as a rhombus of four bars, and the widest fish cross-section that can pass is identified with the circle inscribed in the opening, whose diameter is the altitude of the rhombus. Combining that diameter with a stated girth-to-length relation for a fusiform fish (maximum girth equal to half the length) and a stated allowance for body compression gives two thresholds for every mesh: a nominal escape length and a larger escape length that a fish can reach by compressing its girth by one tenth; fish longer than the second value are reliably retained. For a 100 mm stretched mesh the rule gives an escape length of 335.1 mm for a square opening and 288.3 mm for a diamond opening, a ratio of 0.86, so opening shape alone changes the escaping size by about one seventh at fixed mesh size. Across stretched mesh sizes from 25 mm to 150 mm the escape length rises from 73.3 mm to 509.6 mm for square openings and from 61.6 mm to 439.5 mm for diamond openings. The full table is provided so that a mesh can be chosen directly from the length of fish to be released or held. The study is proven in simulation.

A Safe-Load Rule for Dugout Canoes: Freeboard, Metacentric Height and the Cost of Standing Up

The result. A dugout canoe is usually loaded by eye: fish, gear and people are added until the boat feels low or tender, and the judgement is made afloat, when changing it is hardest. This paper replaces that judgement, for planning purposes, with a computed rule. A simple hydrostatic model of a dugout hull — displacement from a block coefficient, freeboard from hull depth minus draft, and transverse stability from the metacentric height — is applied to four canoe sizes, and the largest payload is found at which two stated limits both hold: a minimum freeboard of 0.15 m and a minimum metacentric height of 0.15 m. In every size the stability limit is reached long before the freeboard limit. For the reference canoe of 5.0 m length, 0.85 m beam and 0.45 m depth, the safe payload with everyone seated is 300 kg, while the freeboard alone would allow 588 kg; loading to the freeboard figure would leave the metacentric height negative in the standing case and at the stated minimum in the seated case. When the payload is treated as standing, the safe payload of the same canoe falls to 151 kg, a reduction of 149 kg or 49.7 percent. Across the four sizes the reduction on standing ranges from 47.5 percent to 55.9 percent. The practical rule is therefore twofold: load a dugout by its stability figure, not by the space or freeboard that remains, and treat standing up in a loaded canoe as roughly halving the load the canoe can safely carry. The study is proven in simulation.

Hanging Ratio Moves a Gillnet's Catch Band but Cannot Narrow It: A Geometric Selection Rule

The result. Gillnetters often speak of the hanging ratio — the length a net is hung to, divided by its stretched length — as a way of tuning which fish the net takes, and it is natural to hope that slackening or tightening the hang might narrow the range of sizes caught around a target length. This paper tests that hope with a geometric model. The mesh opening at a given hanging ratio is a rhombus whose width for a fish's body is set by its altitude; a fish is caught when the mesh can be pushed over its gills but cannot pass over its greatest girth. Both edges of the caught-length band turn out to be proportional to the same quantity, so their ratio is fixed at 1.4238 for every hanging ratio from 0.30 to 0.70: hanging ratio moves the band and stretches it, but cannot change its relative width. The result is then worked in the form a fisherman actually needs. On a fixed 100 mm mesh, raising the hanging ratio from 0.30 to 0.70 moves the band from 172.9–246.2 mm to 302.0–430.0 mm. If instead the mesh is re-chosen at each hanging ratio so the band stays centred on a 300 mm target, the band is 247.5–352.5 mm at every hanging ratio — a width of 104.9 mm that no choice of hanging ratio reduces. What hanging ratio does change, once the target is held fixed, is the bill: twine per hung metre falls from 6.67 m at a ratio of 0.30 to 2.86 m at 0.70, and a 50-mesh-deep net hangs 6.83 m deep at 0.30 against 2.93 m at 0.70. The selection rule is therefore to set the band with mesh size and the target, and to spend hanging ratio on depth and twine cost, where it genuinely has leverage. The study is proven in simulation.

A Minimum-Freeboard Bound for Small Open Boats: Wave Height, Bow Immersion and a Hand-Span Rule

The result. An open boat ships water when a wave encounter, at its worst moment, lifts the sea surface at the bow above the gunwale. This paper turns that moment into a simple bound that can be applied before leaving shore. The relative immersion at the bow is estimated as the sum of two parts: the crest height of a sinusoidal wave above still water, with no credit taken for the boat heaving with the wave, and the dip of the bow as the boat pitches with a stated fraction of the wave slope over a stated wavelength of 30 m. The sum is adjusted by a stated factor for bow shape. Boarding is taken to begin when this immersion reaches the loaded freeboard. For a 5.0 m boat of standard bow shape, a loaded freeboard of 0.30 m is exceeded at a wave height of 0.439 m; a freeboard of 0.20 m is exceeded at 0.293 m, and one of 0.40 m at 0.585 m. In units a fisherman can measure on his or her own hand, taking a stretched hand span as 0.20 m, the rule for the 5.0 m boat is that the largest wave that can be met without boarding is about 1.46 times the loaded freeboard, so one and a half spans of freeboard meet about 2.2 spans of wave. Bow shape matters at the same order as loading: at a length of 6.0 m and a freeboard of 0.30 m, a fine bow raises the limiting wave height to 0.490 m while a full, blunt bow lowers it to 0.362 m. Longer boats need more freeboard for the same wave, because the bow sits further from the pitching centre. The study is proven in simulation, and the bound is deliberately conservative in the ways stated in the text.

A Closing Bound for Beach Seining: The Largest Seine a Standard Hauling Team Can Close, and the Haul Rate Longer Seines Require

The result. A beach seine traps fish in a pocket between the net and the shore, and the fish leave the way they came in: around the ends, through the gap that stays open until the haul is nearly finished. This paper originates a closing bound for that process. The seine is modelled as a semicircle against a straight beach, both ends are hauled toward each other at a speed limited by the hauling team's strength working against water drag on the net, and every fish is assumed to head straight for the nearer end at an effective speed of 0.25 m/s for the whole closing time, which makes the computed escape fraction an upper bound. Against a stated design bound of one fish in five escaping (a fraction of 0.20), the largest seine a standard team of two haulers per end, pulling 400 N per end, can close in time is 25 m: at 25 m the model's escape fraction is 0.1677, and at 30 m it has already risen to 0.2471. For longer seines the paper inverts the calculation and gives the minimum haul rate. The required haul speed turns out to be almost independent of length — 0.768 m/s at 40 m of seine and only 0.821 m/s at 200 m — but the force needed to haul that fast against a longer net grows in proportion to length, from 615 N per end (4 haulers) at 40 m to 1,615 N per end (9 haulers) at 100 m and 3,283 N per end (17 haulers) at 200 m. The bound is sensitive to fish speed: the limiting length is 40 m if the fish's sustained speed is 0.3 m/s and only 10 m if it is 0.8 m/s. The practical reading is that closing speed is bought with hauling force, not technique, and that long seines cannot be saved by hauling harder at any realistic team size — they stand or fall on keeping fish from making for the ends at all. The study is proven in simulation.

Sizing the Outrigger Float of a Single-Outrigger Canoe: Ama Volume, Crossbeam Length and Heel Limits

The result. A single-outrigger canoe depends for most of its stability on one small float, the ama, held away from the hull by a crossbeam. Builders size that float by inheritance and by eye, and the penalty for undersizing is paid in a capsize or in a boat that cannot be worked. This paper derives a sizing rule from statics and applies it to three crew and load cases. The rule has two parts. First, a reserve-buoyancy requirement: the righting moment available when the ama is pressed to full immersion, equal to water density times gravity times ama volume times the distance from hull centreline to ama centreline, must reach twice the stated heeling moment, the factor of two being a stated dynamic allowance. Second, a heel check: the equilibrium heel under the stated heeling moment, from the combined stiffness of the hull and the immersed ama, must stay below a stated limit of 10 degrees. For a two-crew canoe of 260 kg total mass facing a heeling moment of 715 N m, the rule requires an ama of 48.6 litres at a centreline distance of 3.0 m, of diameter 0.153 m over a length of 3.3 m; the corresponding volumes are 58.3 litres at 2.5 m and 41.6 litres at 3.5 m. The one-crew case requires 24.3 litres and the three-crew case 80.4 litres at the same 3.0 m distance. In every case the heel check is met with a wide margin — equilibrium heels range from 0.54 degrees to 1.47 degrees across all cases and beam distances — and a float sized by the heel check alone would be under 2 litres, so it is the reserve-buoyancy requirement, not heel stiffness, that sets the size of a safe ama. The capacity when heeled away from the ama, so that the float is lifted rather than pressed, is smaller than the pressed capacity in every case and is tabulated as a warning. The study is proven in simulation.

The Soak-Time Peak: Why a Longer Soak Does Not Always Catch More in a Baited Fish Trap

The result. A widely repeated rule of thumb holds that a baited fish trap left in the water longer always holds more fish, so the best soak is the longest soak that weather and schedule allow. This study tests that claim with a simple dynamical model in which fish enter at a rate set by the remaining strength of the bait and by the space left in the trap, and leave through escapement and predation at a rate proportional to the number already inside. The claim does not survive the arithmetic. Under the baseline assumptions the catch rises to a peak of 19.43 fish at 18.04 hours and then falls: a trap hauled at 48 hours holds 12.86 fish, which is 31.9 percent fewer than it held at 24 hours and fewer than the 18.49 fish it already held at 12 hours. Halving the peak soak time costs 12.8 percent of the peak catch; doubling it costs 17.2 percent. The peak arrives earlier when bait decays faster, when escapement is higher, or when predators learn the trap, and later in cold water with slow bait decay (28.98 hours) or with a tight, low-loss funnel (25.08 hours). The paper gives a practical soak-time rule: haul near the computed peak for the conditions at hand, and treat the interval over which the model holds at least 95 percent of the peak catch — 11.94 to 26.34 hours in the baseline case — as the working window. This study is proven in simulation.

A Trip Fuel-Planning Rule for a Small Outboard Fishing Boat: Speed, Load, and Why a Larger Engine Does Not Burn Proportionally More

The result. This simulated study builds a trip fuel-planning rule for a 6.0 m semi-displacement open fishing boat powered by a small petrol outboard, and uses it to test the common belief that fitting a larger engine raises trip fuel consumption in proportion to the increase in rated power. Required shaft power is modelled as a function of speed and total displacement, and fuel consumption is modelled through a specific consumption curve that worsens at low load fraction, so that an engine working well below its rated output is penalised for part-throttle inefficiency but not charged for power it is not producing. For the headline case — a total trip distance of 20 nautical miles at 7 knots with a medium load — the 15 hp engine burns 4.434 litres for the trip, and the planning rule proposed here, fuel to carry equals computed trip burn multiplied by a reserve factor of 1.50, gives 6.651 litres to carry. Speed is expensive in this model: the same 20 nautical mile trip burns 3.110 litres at 5 knots and 5.421 litres at 9 knots. Load matters almost as much: at 7 knots over 20 nautical miles the trip burn rises from 3.772 litres with a light load to 5.135 litres with a heavy load. The larger-engine belief does not survive in its proportional form. The 25 hp engine, whose rated power is two thirds higher again than the 15 hp engine, burns only 10.9 percent more fuel on the headline trip (4.919 litres against 4.434 litres), with the extra consumption ranging from 6.2 percent at 5 knots to 14.2 percent at 8 knots across the cases computed. A larger engine does burn more for the same trip in every case computed here, because it runs at a lower load fraction and carries extra weight, but the increase is a fraction of what a proportional rule would predict, and buying fuel, tankage, or range on a proportional assumption would substantially overstate the cost of the larger engine. The study is proven in simulation.

A Sizing Rule for Fish Trap Funnel Throats: Throat Diameter and Cone Angle Against Entry and Escape

The result. A trap funnel has to do two opposed things: admit the fish that are wanted and then refuse to let them back out. This study originates a sizing rule for the funnel entrance cone by modelling both duties from stated assumptions. Entry is modelled as a rising function of the ratio between throat diameter and the body depth of the target fish; escape over a 24-hour soak is modelled from the throat area a searching fish is presented with, the cone half-angle that guides it back to the throat, and the cone length it must traverse. A design is accepted when modelled entry probability is at least 0.80 and modelled escapement over 24 hours is at most 0.10. A throat equal to the body depth admits only half the target fish (entry probability 0.50), so the throat must exceed the depth: the recommended window runs from 1.18 to 1.40 times the body depth for every target size computed. The cone angle ceiling tightens sharply as the target fish grows: at the stated standard, a 60 mm fish tolerates angles up to 40 degrees, a 100 mm fish up to 27 degrees at its narrowest recommended throat, and a 150 mm fish only 12 degrees. Computed windows are tabulated for target body depths of 60, 80, 100, 120 and 150 mm. This study is proven in simulation.

Choose the Line by Its Knotted Strength, Not the Number on the Spool: A Weakest-Link Table for Handline and Longline Gear

The result. This simulated study performs a weakest-link accounting for handline and longline gear and derives the rule that line should be chosen by its knotted strength, not by the breaking strain printed on the spool. Each knot is assigned a stated retained-strength fraction chosen inside a published range: 0.93 for a Palomar knot, 0.82 for a Uni knot, 0.75 for an improved clinch knot, 0.65 for a blood knot join, and 0.50 for a plain overhand knot. A fighting fish is modelled as imposing a peak load of 3.0 times its body weight. On a line whose spool figure is 20 kgf, the knotted strength is 18.6 kgf with a Palomar knot, holding a fish of 6.2 kg under the load model, but only 15.0 kgf with an improved clinch knot (a fish of 5.0 kg) and only 10.0 kgf with an overhand knot (a fish of 3.33 kg). Using the spool figure in place of the knotted strength overstates the gear's capacity by 7.5 percent for the Palomar knot, 33.3 percent for the improved clinch knot, 53.8 percent for the blood knot, and 100.0 percent for the overhand knot; the spool figure promises a fish of 6.67 kg on the 20 kgf line, where the overhand-knotted gear holds 3.33 kg. The accounting also shows that the main line is often not the weakest link at all: a 20 kgf main line joined to a 12 kgf leader fails at the knotted leader, at 11.16 kgf even with Palomar knots, holding a fish of 3.72 kg. Strengthening the main line while leaving the leader and its knots unchanged adds nothing. The study is proven in simulation.

A Degradable Escape Panel Specification for Fish Traps: A Cotton-Twine Fastener That Ends Ghost Fishing Within Thirty Days

The result. A lost fish trap does not stop fishing. Its bait draws a first catch, the catch itself becomes bait for the next, and the trap can go on taking fish long after its owner has written it off. This study first models that process: with a first cycle of 14 fish, six-day cycles, and each cycle retaining 0.78 of the previous cycle's catch as self-bait, a lost trap fishes for 97.2 days and takes 62.5 fish before its per-cycle catch fades below a quarter of a fish. The study then originates a specification for a degradable escape panel intended to stop a lost trap within a chosen deadline of 30 days: a buoyant 200 mm by 200 mm panel held shut by four ties of untreated cotton twine, sized so that seawater decay brings the twine's strength down to the panel's steady opening load at the right time, neither during normal service nor long after the deadline. The computed fastener rule accepts twine diameters from 2.35 to 2.75 mm; the nominal 2.5 mm tie, with an initial breaking strength of 12.5 kgf and a strength half-life of 8.75 days, opens the panel at 24.5 days, still shows a safety factor of 1.77 against the greatest service load after the longest permitted 10-day service interval, and cuts the lost trap's fishing life by 74.8 percent, preventing 21.9 of the 62.5 fish (35.1 percent) the unprotected trap would have taken. This study is proven in simulation.

An Anchor Sizing and Scope Rule for Small Fishing Boats: Holding in Sand and Mud at 20 and 30 Knots of Wind

The result. This simulated study derives an anchor sizing and scope rule for a 6.0 m open fishing boat, by setting the load the weather imposes on the boat against the holding force an anchor of a given weight can develop in sand and in mud, with holding reduced at short scope. Wind load is computed from a stated windage area and the square of wind speed; a stated current of 1.0 knot adds a constant load of 14.94 kgf; and the steady total is doubled by a stated surge allowance to give a design load of 66.90 kgf in 20 knots of wind and 113.18 kgf in 30 knots. Holding is modelled as anchor weight multiplied by a bottom factor at a scope of 7:1 — 25 in clean sand and 10 in soft mud — multiplied further by a scope factor of 0.40 at 3:1, 0.75 at 5:1, 1.00 at 7:1, and 1.15 at 10:1. At a scope of 7:1 the anchor weight required is 2.68 kg in sand and 6.69 kg in mud at 20 knots, rising to 4.53 kg in sand and 11.32 kg in mud at 30 knots. Scope is worth more than weight in this model: at 30 knots in sand, shortening the scope from 7:1 to 3:1 raises the required weight from 4.53 kg to 11.32 kg, exactly the weight that soft mud demands at full scope, while in mud at 3:1 the requirement reaches 28.30 kg, beyond what such a boat can reasonably carry. The rule is therefore twofold: size the anchor for the worst bottom and the worst weather in the table that the boat will actually face, and treat scope as the first remedy when holding is in doubt, because veering more rode multiplies the holding of the anchor already aboard. The study is proven in simulation.
