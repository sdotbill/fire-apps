A strength test for wood-ash lye without instruments

The result. Wood-ash lye needs to fall within a narrow concentration band for soap-making, yet the household maker usually has no hydrometer or titration kit. The traditional substitute is the floating-egg test: lye is judged ready when a fresh egg floats with a small cap exposed. This paper calibrates that test in a physical model, derives a sizing rule for ash, water and leaching passes, and sets a failure bound. The central egg, at 1.060 g/mL, reaches neutral buoyancy at 65.2 g/L of alkali as potassium carbonate, but eggs spanning 1.030 to 1.090 g/mL indicate anywhere from 32.6 to 97.8 g/L at the same visual point, a range 65.2 g/L wide against a target band only 40.0 g/L wide. An egg floating with 5% exposed corresponds to 125.9 g/L, near the top of the target band of 90.0 to 130.0 g/L. The sizing rule for mixed household ash is 15 kg of ash per 10 L of water leached in 2 passes, giving 115.5 g/L. The same recipe across ash sources gives 64.2 to 154.0 g/L, a 2.4-fold spread. The claim that stronger lye is always better does not survive: dosing a fixed volume sized for mid-band lye with lye at 180 g/L leaves 89.7 g of excess potassium hydroxide equivalent per kilogram of tallow, an upper-bound free alkali content of 6.85% in the finished bar, 68.5 times a 0.1% reference limit.

One recipe does not fit every fat

The result. Household soap recipes are often transmitted as a single quantity of alkali per unit of fat, for use with whatever fat is at hand. This paper tests that practice against published saponification values for six common fats and shows, in numbers, that it does not survive. A fixed recipe of 140.0 g of sodium hydroxide per kilogram requires, in fact, 99.1 g for lard and 182.5 g for coconut oil, a range whose maximum is 1.84 times its minimum. On tallow the fixed recipe leaves 39.5 g of surplus alkali per kilogram, a free alkali content of 3.06% in the cured bar, 30.6 times a 0.1% reference limit. On coconut oil it falls short by 42.5 g and leaves 23.3% of the fat unsaponified. No single fixed quantity satisfies both extremes: the ceiling from the lowest-demand fat is 100.4 g per kilogram, while the floor from the highest-demand fat, allowing at most 8% unsaponified fat, is 167.9 g per kilogram, a gap of 67.5 g. The replacement rule is fat-specific: dose 95% of the requirement calculated from the fat's published value, giving 95.5 g per kilogram for tallow, 173.4 g for coconut oil and 118.8 g for a stated 70/30 tallow and coconut blend.

The curing bound: when is a bar of soap actually ready?

The result. Fresh soap is routinely judged ready by feel: it no longer feels slippery, and a touch to the tongue no longer produces a sharp sensation. This paper models the three processes curing must complete, water loss, decline of free alkali, and development of hardness, and tests those signs against the resulting timetable. For a stated 120.0 g bar measuring 80 by 55 by 25 mm, starting at 28.0% water and curing at 20 °C and 50% relative humidity, water falls to 20.2% at day 7, 15.6% at day 14 and 11.3% at day 28. Free alkali in a zero-superfat batch falls from 2.40% at demoulding to 0.43% at day 7 and reaches 0.1% only at day 22.9. The minimum cure satisfying all three criteria is 23 days for a zero-superfat batch and 21 days with a 5% lye discount. The traditional signs clear far earlier: the tongue-test proxy clears at day 9.2 for the zero-superfat batch and day 2.1 for the superfatted batch, and the slippery-feel proxy at day 5.1, a gap of 13.7 days between the tongue test clearing and the zero-superfat minimum. A hardness index from saturated fatty-acid content, H = 12 + 0.92 times the saturated percentage, gives 87.4 for coconut oil, 58.0 for tallow and 24.9 for olive oil, and shows that no cure duration compensates for a low-saturation blend.

Sizing a solar salt pan

The result. A household that can reach the sea often asks a deceptively simple question: how large must a solar salt pan be? This paper derives a sizing rule from a water balance rather than from custom or anecdote. Salt can leave a pan no faster than water does, so the salt production rate is the net evaporation rate multiplied by the ratio of salt to water in the feed brine. Net evaporation is gross evaporation reduced by a factor of 0.85 for concentrated brine, minus rainfall. For seawater at a mass fraction of 0.035, the rule gives 5.059 m2 of pan per kilogram of salt per day in an arid coastal climate with net evaporation of 5.45 mm per day, and 19.694 m2 per kilogram per day in a humid coastal climate with net evaporation of 1.4 mm per day. Spread over a dry season, a stated household need of 30.0 kg per year requires only 0.63 m2 in the arid case and 4.92 m2 in the humid case, provided the pan is refilled as it dries. The binding constraint is therefore not area in most coastal settings. It is the seasonal total of net evaporation. A pan on 20.0 m2 fails that household need when the season total of net evaporation falls below 41.4 mm, and it produces nothing at all when average rainfall reaches 5.95 mm per day against arid gross evaporation, or 3.4 mm per day against humid gross evaporation. A single 10.0 mm rain event on nearly saturated brine dilutes it from a mass fraction of 0.26 to 0.1418 and costs 1.83 extra days of evaporation in the arid case and 7.14 days in the humid case.

The break-even salinity for boiling brine

The result. Boiling seawater directly into salt is technically straightforward and energetically ruinous. This paper computes the fuel cost of boiling brine as a function of its starting concentration, using a latent heat of vaporisation of 2257 kJ per kg of water, a brine specific heat of 3.9 kJ per kg per K, dry wood at 15.0 MJ per kg, and a stove efficiency of 0.15. Boiling seawater at a salt mass fraction of 0.035 requires 70.586 MJ of heat and 31.371 kg of wood for each kilogram of salt. At a mass fraction of 0.2 the cost falls to 10.491 MJ and 4.662 kg of wood, and at 0.26, near saturation, to 7.549 MJ and 3.355 kg of wood. Against a stated household affordability bound of 5.0 kg of wood per kilogram of salt, the break-even concentration is a mass fraction of 0.1888, or 188.8 g per kg. Below that concentration, boiling from scratch does not pay under the stated bound; above it, fuel finishing becomes a reasonable use of gathered wood. The arithmetic points to a hybrid schedule: concentrate seawater by solar evaporation to a mass fraction of 0.2, which removes 0.8549 of the original water at no fuel cost, and then finish by boiling. That schedule reduces wood use by 85.1 percent compared with boiling seawater directly.

Evaporate to dryness is worse salt

The result. A common instruction in household salt making is to evaporate seawater completely and use everything that remains. This paper shows, by a fractional crystallisation model, that the instruction produces worse salt. The model starts from 1000 g of seawater containing 965 g of water and 35.0 g of dissolved salts, of which 27.0 g is common salt (NaCl). Each salt is held in solution until the remaining water falls below the amount its stated solubility requires. Calcium sulphate saturates early, when 500.0 g of water remains. NaCl begins to crystallise when 75.0 g of water remains, after a fraction of 0.9223 of the water has been removed, and it crystallises through a window that ends when magnesium chloride begins, at 7.04 g of water remaining. Stopping at 7.0 g of water remaining recovers 24.48 g of NaCl, a recovery of 90.7 percent, at a dry-crystal purity of 0.9532, or 0.9163 after allowing for retained mother liquor. Evaporating to complete dryness recovers the full 27.0 g of NaCl but delivers 35.0 g of mixed salts at a purity of 0.7714. Going to dryness therefore gains only 2.52 g of additional NaCl, which is 9.33 percent of the total NaCl present, while purity falls by 18.18 percentage points on a dry basis. The recommended stopping rule is to harvest when about 7.0 g of water remains for each 1000 g of seawater started — a fraction of 0.9927 of the original water removed — and to discard or separately use the remaining bitter liquor, known as bittern.

Can a small cane operation fuel itself?

The result. A small cane operation is often assumed to be fuel-self-sufficient because industrial mills burn their own bagasse. This paper tests that assumption at household and village scale, on a single-effect open pan, for a basis of 1000.0 kg of cane. A single-roller press recovering 54.0% of the soluble solids extracts 464.7 kg of juice and leaves 535.3 kg of bagasse at 60.1% moisture; it delivers 944.9 MJ of useful heat against 897.9 MJ needed to make 127.1 kg of syrup at 68% solids, a ratio of 1.052 — self-sufficient, but only just. A three-roller press without imbibition water, recovering 68.0% of the solids, also passes narrowly, at a ratio of 1.044. The same three-roller press with 180 kg of imbibition water recovers 82.0% of the solids and makes more syrup, 192.9 kg, yet fails: it delivers 1158.2 MJ against 1745.3 MJ needed, a ratio of 0.664, and requires 93.8 kg of outside wood per tonne of cane. Sun-drying the bagasse and improving the furnace narrows the deficit but does not close it. Only vapour reuse, modelled at a factor of 1.7, turns that high-extraction case self-sufficient, at a ratio of 1.33. The governing rule is therefore not extraction alone: on a single-effect pan, every kilogram of imbibition water must be evaporated again, and that second evaporation can consume more heat than the extra sugar is worth in fuel terms. All device and furnace figures in this paper are Proven in simulation.

Knowing the endpoint without a thermometer

The result. Syrup makers working without instruments judge the endpoint by hand: a thread drawn between the fingers, a drop set in cold water, a flake falling from the skimmer, or an egg floated in the pan. This paper calibrates each of those tests against final solids concentration, boiling temperature at three altitudes, and final moisture content, using stated correlations for density and boiling-point rise, and it prices the error of each test in fuel and in product loss. The target used throughout is syrup at 70% solids and 30% moisture. The thread test, the cold-water test, and the sheet test each centre between 70% and 72% solids with an error of ±3.0 percentage points of moisture. A litre measure weighed on a kitchen scale does better, at ±1.0 percentage points. The floating-egg test, judged by a coin-sized area of shell exposed, does not survive as a precision test: its nominal centre is 72.5% solids, but combining the stated ranges for egg density and for the exposed area an observer calls a coin gives a range from 54.0% to 95.0% solids. From juice at 15% solids, finishing 1 kg of syrup at the 70% target costs 1.980 kg of wood on the stated open-pan assumptions. Stopping at 65% solids saves 8.8% of that fuel but leaves 50 g of excess water in every kilogram. Running to 78% solids costs 14.1% extra fuel and, for a pure sucrose syrup cooled to 20 °C, puts an upper bound of 331.3 g per kilogram into crystals that fall out of solution. The recommended household method is a weighed litre as the primary test, with the thread test as a cross-check. All apparatus figures are Proven in simulation.

Why household crystallisation yields cap where they do

The result. Household and small-scale sugar makers commonly recover much less crystal from a pan of concentrated syrup than the sugar it contains, and the shortfall is often blamed on technique. This paper shows, by mass balance, that most of the shortfall is structural. From a stated massecuite of 100 kg at 93% solids and 85% purity — 79.05 kg of sucrose and 13.95 kg of non-sucrose solids — the theoretical first-strike crystal yield, with the mother liquor exhausted to 40% purity, is 69.75 kg of sucrose, which is 88.2% of the sucrose present and 69.8% of the massecuite mass. The remaining 9.30 kg of sucrose, 11.8% of the total, stays dissolved in 30.25 kg of mother liquor and cannot be removed by any amount of stirring, cooling, or draining, because the non-sucrose solids hold it in a liquor of that purity. Retention then taxes the result a second time: draining in cloth or a basket leaves 18.0% of the mother liquor on the crystals, 5.44 kg, dropping product purity to 95.0%, while a household basket centrifuge leaving 5.0% gives a product purity of 98.5%. After a stated handling loss and washing off the retained liquor, the practical single-strike cap for a drained, washed product is 66.68 kg, which is 84.4% of the sucrose and 66.7% of the massecuite. Reboiling the mother liquor recovers only 3.32 kg in a second strike and 1.33 kg in a third, taking cumulative recovery to 74.4 kg, or 94.1%, at the cost of two further boils. The paper also derives the sap-to-syrup rule from mass balance: litres of sap per litre of syrup equals 87.1 divided by the sap sugar percentage, so 2.0% sap requires 43.6 litres per litre of syrup. All separation equipment figures are Proven in simulation.

A wick selection rule for tallow and beeswax candles

The result. A candle fails in two opposite ways. If the wick is too small for the candle, its flame melts only a narrow well in the centre, the walls never melt, and the flame sinks into a tunnel of its own making. If the wick is too large, its flame melts a pool wider than the candle, the liquid overflows, the flame is fed faster than it can burn cleanly, and the candle smokes and drips. This paper states a heat-balance model that predicts melt-pool diameter from wick burn rate and wax properties, converts that prediction into a selection rule with computed failure bounds on both sides, and tabulates the rule for tallow and for beeswax. The energy needed to melt a kilogram of wax, at 20 °C ambient, is 234.0 kJ/kg for tallow and 280.0 kJ/kg for beeswax, a ratio of 1.197. At the same wick class, W3 at 7.0 g/h, the predicted pool is 36.9 mm in tallow and 29.8 mm in beeswax, a reduction of 19.2%. The practical consequence is that a wick chart for one wax does not transfer to the other: a 50 mm candle is correctly served by W4 in tallow but needs W5 in beeswax, and candles of 20 mm in tallow and 80 mm in either wax fall outside the five classes considered altogether. All results are model calculations. Proven in simulation.

Light per kilogram: candles against oil lamps

The result. Households that buy or make fuel for light face a simple question that is rarely answered in comparable units: how much light does a kilogram of fuel deliver? This paper computes total light delivered per kilogram for three sources — a tallow candle, a beeswax candle, and a simple open-wick oil lamp burning vegetable oil — from stated burn rates and stated luminous flux assumptions for each flame. The results are 1500.0 lumen-hours per kilogram for the tallow candle, 2000.0 lumen-hours per kilogram for the beeswax candle, and 3333.3 lumen-hours per kilogram for the oil lamp. For a stated evening of light, defined as 100 lumens of source flux for 4 hours, or 400.0 lumen-hours, the fuel required is 266.7 g of tallow candle, 200.0 g of beeswax candle, or 120.0 g of vegetable oil. The oil lamp leads because its larger flame produces more flux per gram burned, not because its fuel contains more energy. Its lead is not fragile: its assumed flux of 40.0 lumens would have to fall to 24.0 lumens, a drop of 40.0%, before it merely tied the beeswax candle. All results are calculations from stated assumptions. Proven in simulation.

The burn-time law for candles

The result. Two rules of thumb about candle burn time are widely repeated: that a candle gives so many hours per inch of height, implying burn time is proportional to height at fixed diameter, and that doubling the diameter doubles the burn time. This paper derives burn duration from a melt-and-consumption model and shows that neither rule survives in its simple form. In the model, burn rate grows as diameter to the power 1.5, so burn time at fixed height grows only as the square root of diameter: doubling diameter multiplies burn time by 1.414, not by 2. For beeswax at 100 mm height, burn times are 18.8 hours at 25 mm diameter, 26.5 hours at 50 mm, and 37.5 hours at 100 mm. Height scaling is not proportional either, because a 10 mm stub is left unburned: at 50 mm diameter, doubling height from 50 mm to 100 mm multiplies beeswax burn time by 2.25, from 11.8 hours to 26.5 hours, while hours per inch rise from 5.99 to 6.74. Draught and warmth shorten life through dripping loss; in a strong draught at 32 °C the waste fraction reaches 0.326 and beeswax burn time falls by 30.5%. The paper also gives production arithmetic for dipped tapers: 25 dips at 0.35 mm per dip build a 200 mm taper to 20.5 mm diameter, using 62.0 g of beeswax, at 86.4 candles per hour of hands-on dipping labour. All results are model calculations. Proven in simulation.

The strength ceiling of household vinegar

The result. Household vinegar is often described as if its strength were a matter of patience: leave wine exposed to air long enough and it will become as strong as the maker wishes. Stoichiometry sets a firmer limit. This paper calculates, from the oxidation of ethanol to acetic acid, the theoretical and practical acetic-acid concentration obtainable from starting liquids containing 5–12% alcohol by volume, after allowing for evaporation, over-oxidation, and incomplete conversion. At a combined retention of 0.8035, a 5% cider yields a practical vinegar of 4.13% acetic acid by weight per volume, and a 12% wine yields 9.92%, against a theoretical 12.34% for the wine. A starting liquid of at least 6.05% alcohol is required for a practical 5.00% vinegar. A surface-area-to-volume rule for acetification time is derived, in which time is inversely proportional to specific surface; under the stated calibration a 20-litre barrel requires 42.0 days, a wide tray 14.8 days, and a trickle system over packing 2.7 days. Finally, a pickling bound is derived for the dilution that occurs when vinegar meets the water held in vegetables. For 1 kg of vegetables covered with only 0.5 L of vinegar, the starting vinegar must be at least 6.75% to leave an equilibrated pickle at 2.5%; a nominal 5% vinegar leaves only 1.85% in that case, and does not satisfy the stated bound. All results are model calculations. Proven in simulation.

Starch recovery, stage by stage

The result. Wet extraction of starch looks simple — grind, wash, settle, dry — yet a large share of the starch in the feedstock never reaches the bag. This paper models cassava and maize extraction as a chain of four stages, each with a stated recovery fraction, and multiplies the chain to obtain overall yield per 100 kg of feedstock. Fresh cassava containing 25.0 kg of starch yields 18.44 kg of dry starch, or 20.95 kg of finished starch at 12% moisture, an overall recovery of 0.7375 of the starch present. Maize grain containing 62.0 kg of starch yields 46.09 kg dry, or 52.37 kg finished, a recovery of 0.7434. The largest single loss for cassava is washing and extraction, 2.88 kg; for maize it is steeping and washing, 9.02 kg. Water use is 40.6 litres per kilogram of finished cassava starch and 11.8 litres per kilogram of finished maize starch under the stated water inputs. Drying requires a fuel heat input of 2.67 MJ per kilogram of finished cassava starch and 2.30 MJ per kilogram for maize at 55% dryer efficiency. One concrete change is quantified for cassava: counter-current wash-water reuse combined with extended settling raises finished yield to 22.87 kg per 100 kg, a gain of 1.92 kg or 9.2%, while cutting fresh water per kilogram of finished starch from 40.6 litres to 24.0 litres. All results are model calculations. Proven in simulation.

More dye does not mean proportionally deeper colour

The result. A common household assumption is that doubling the dye in a bath doubles the depth of colour on the fibre. For madder on alum-mordanted wool, a saturation model shows that this assumption fails, and by how much. Dye uptake is modelled with a Langmuir-type isotherm, saturation capacity 90 g of dyestuff equivalent per kilogram of fibre and affinity constant 0.45 L/g, combined with a bath mass balance at a liquor ratio of 20 L per kilogram. Uptake rises from 28.94 g/kg at a dose of 50 g/kg to 48.37 g/kg at 100 g/kg, 67.41 g/kg at 200 g/kg, and only 79.05 g/kg at 400 g/kg. Doubling the dose from 200 g/kg to 400 g/kg buys a depth gain of 17.3%; doubling from 400 g/kg to 800 g/kg buys 7.2%, while bath exhaustion falls to 10.6%. A linear prediction calibrated at 50 g/kg overstates uptake at 800 g/kg by a factor of 5.46. Liquor ratio matters at fixed dose: at 100 g/kg, uptake is 58.58 g/kg at a liquor ratio of 10, 48.37 g/kg at 20, and 37.24 g/kg at 40. Mordant arithmetic is set out: alum at 20% on weight of fibre is 200 g per kilogram of wool, and raising alum from 20% to 25% raises modelled uptake at a 100 g/kg dose only from 48.37 g/kg to 49.11 g/kg. Colour yield per kilogram of dyestuff is tabulated so a household can price a shade; at an illustrative root price of 18 per kilogram, dyeing 1 kg of wool costs 1.8 in dyestuff at the 100 g/kg dose and 7.2 at the 400 g/kg dose, for a depth gain of less than two thirds. All results are model calculations. Proven in simulation.

The fuel bound of a small lime kiln, and a weighing test for completeness

The result. Pure calcium carbonate requires 1.78 MJ per kilogram of stone, or 3.178 MJ per kilogram of quicklime. With air-dry wood at 15.0 MJ/kg and stated efficiencies of 0.15 for a clamp or pit kiln and 0.30 for a small shaft kiln, the fuel bound is 1.412 kg of wood per kilogram of quicklime in the clamp and 0.706 kg in the shaft kiln. A 100 kg charge of pure stone yields 56.03 kg of quicklime and needs 79.13 kg of wood in a clamp or 39.56 kg in a shaft kiln. Because pure CaCO3 loses 43.97% of its mass as CO2, weighing stone in and product out bounds the uncalcined fraction through x = (1 − m_out/m_in)/(p × 0.4397), where p is purity — a completeness check needing only a scale. The claim that any pale rock that looks like limestone makes good lime does not survive a numerical test: a stone with 80% CaCO3, 10% silica and 10% inert material gives only 0.355 kg of free CaO per kilogram of stone against 0.560 kg for pure limestone, and pure dolomite gives only 0.304 kg of CaO per kilogram of stone while raising wood per kilogram of CaO by 68.4%.

What a household charcoal filter can and cannot do

The result. This paper sizes a household charcoal filter for a representative organic contaminant, phenol at 5.0 mg/L, from stated adsorption capacities and a contact-time model, and then applies the same arithmetic to two claims the filter cannot meet. For granular activated carbon at a stated capacity of 50 mg/g, usable volume before breakthrough at a utilisation factor of 0.50 is 5000.0 litres per kilogram; for plain wood charcoal at 10 mg/g it is 1000.0 litres per kilogram. A household using 20.0 litres per day needs, over 90 days (1800.0 litres), 0.36 kg of activated carbon or 1.8 kg of plain charcoal on capacity alone, or 0.72 kg and 3.6 kg with a factor of two for safety. Contact time sets flow: a 2.0 kg bed occupies 5.0 litres and, at a required empty-bed contact time of 10 minutes, may run at no more than 0.5 litres per minute. Salt removal fails by orders of magnitude: even at a generous capacity of 2.0 mg/g, 1 kg is exhausted by 4.0 litres at 500 mg/L salinity, and 90 days of salt removal would need 450.0 kg. Disinfection fails by mechanism: adsorption micropores are about 250 times narrower than a small bacterium, flow channels are far wider than bacteria, and a plain bed has no inactivation mechanism, so credited log removal is 0. Within its bound — taste, odour, free chlorine and many dissolved organics, for a stated volume at a slow flow — the filter is useful. It is not a desalinator and not a disinfecting device.
