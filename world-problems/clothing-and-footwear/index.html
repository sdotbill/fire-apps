Clothing and footwear

What people wear, priced in protection: sun, rain and cold — and what wetting, stretching and age do to the protection printed on the label.

Bedding and Clothes Without Machines: A Drying Rule for Mould Risk and a Solar Heat Treatment for Parasites

The problem. People who wash by hand and have no machines need to know how to dry bedding before mould takes hold and whether sunshine alone can heat bedding enough to end louse and mite life cycles — and when it cannot.

The result. A cotton bedding set spends 1.31 hours above the mould-risk moisture level spread outdoors in hot dry air but 26.37 hours bunched in a basket indoors, with single-layer spreading roughly halving the risk window in every climate; for solar treatment, a black bag in strong sun holds 55 °C for 279 continuous minutes and an insulated box in moderate sun for 250 minutes (both meeting the stated 60-minute criterion), while a bag in moderate sun fails with 52 minutes and in weak, overcast, or winter sun both containers fail outright, never reaching 50 °C.

The Evaporative Head-Cloth Ceiling: A Humidity Bound on the Cooling a Wetted Head and Neck Wrap Can Deliver

The problem. People rely on wetting a head cloth as a heat remedy in humid and still air where evaporation cannot deliver useful cooling.

The result. At 35 °C in a 1.0 m/s breeze a wetted 0.08 m² head-and-neck wrap reaches the 15 W useful threshold only at or below 27% relative humidity (17.6 W at 10% RH, 14.49 W at 30%), never reaches it in still air at 30–35 °C, and falls below a plain hat's humidity-independent 9.8 W solar saving above 52% RH; where it works it costs only about 21.5 ml of water per hour.

Head Shade Priced in Heat: A Brim-Width Rule, Its Saving in Watts and Sweat, and Its Limit at Low Sun

The problem. Heat load and sweat loss for outdoor workers, and hats sold by style rather than by the watts and water they actually save at the sun angle of the working day.

The result. A 100 mm straw brim saves 46.8 W overhead (0.069 L of sweat per working hour) but only 8.9 W at a 35° sun; the least-cost hat removing at least half of the head's 32.5 W load at 35° is a palm-leaf hat with a 160 mm brim at USD 2.00 (removes 53.8%, saves 17.5 W), and no hat scanned removes 70% of the total load including shoulders at low sun — the best removes only 33.7%.

Insulation per Unit Cost: A Clo-per-Dollar Sizing Rule for Layered Cold-Weather Garments Built from Locally Obtainable Materials

The problem. People with the least money for clothing face the greatest cold exposure, and insulation is sold by brand rather than by warmth delivered per dollar.

The result. Shredded cloth delivers the most still-air insulation per dollar (0.860 clo per dollar, plant fibre 0.782, wool last at 0.134); the least-cost stack for a sedentary wearer costs $11.25 at 0 °C (4.59 clo required), $12.75 at −10 °C, and $14.25 at −20 °C, while light street clothes meet only 45.3% of the sedentary requirement at 0 °C.

The Mesh Aperture Bound: Insect Exclusion, Ventilation Penalty, and a Two-Zone Garment Design

The problem. Workers must choose mesh fine enough to exclude biting insects without making the garment too hot to work in.

The result. Largest excluding apertures are 1.20 mm (mosquito), 0.60 mm (biting midge), and 2.00 mm (stable fly); the midge aperture costs 40.4% of screen-limited ventilation and cuts the evaporative margin at 35 °C/50% RH from 41.6% to 8.4% over an 80 W requirement, while a two-zone design with fine mesh on 25% of the area restores the margin to 33.3% and keeps 89.9% of the coarse flow; at 70% RH no design meets the requirement.

How Long Does Proofed Cotton Stay Waterproof? A Reproofing Interval Rule and the Cost per Dry Hour

The problem. Users of oil- and wax-proofed cotton are told to re-proof "when beading stops" with no interval in days of wear, so garments spend most of their life no longer waterproof while still being trusted as such.

The result. With hydrostatic resistance decaying exponentially from 900 mm to a 350 mm leakage threshold, the re-treatment interval is 17.5 wearing days at typical use (3 rain-hours/day), 12.1 days in a wet season and 31.5 days from flexing alone; costed over a 120-day season, proofed cloth costs $0.390 per dry hour against $0.194 for a disposable plastic poncho, a factor of 2.00.

Rain Cape Geometry: A Sizing Rule for a Walking Cape and the Wind Speed at Which No Practical Cape Keeps the Lower Legs Dry

The problem. Walking rain capes are sold by length and tradition with no stated wind limit, so wearers discover in freshening wind that their lower legs were never geometrically protected.

The result. The longest practical cape (hem at 0.45 m) needs a hem radius of 0.490 m and circumference of 3.08 m at 6 m/s wind for a 1.75 m wearer at 0.75 m stride, and above a wind of 8.5 m/s no cape within the stated practical limits (hem at or above 0.45 m, radius at or below 0.55 m) keeps a walking wearer's lower legs dry; shortening the stride to 0.55 m raises the limit to 11.8 m/s.

The Heat Cost of Staying Dry: A Work-Rate Bound for Sealed Rain Garments

The problem. Workers who must keep working in rain get soaked by their own sweat inside sealed waterproof clothing and lose cooling, with no published bound matching garment vapour transmission to work rate.

The result. The activity ceiling is 100.0 W for a sealed plastic garment, 167.5 W for oiled cotton, and 662.5 W for a standard breathable membrane; at 300 W a sealed garment accumulates 237.0 g/h of the wearer's own sweat, and it becomes wetter than the rain itself above 160.7 W in light rain (2 mm/h) and 327.8 W in moderate rain (7.5 mm/h).

Footwear Drying and the Rotation Rule: A Drying-Time Bound and the Number of Pairs a Wet Climate Requires

The problem. Feet kept in continuously damp footwear under daily wear, with the fungal and skin-breakdown risk that follows, and rotation advice that is never stated as a number of pairs for a given climate.

The result. In temperate indoor conditions (20 °C, 65% RH) a leather boot needs 45.0 h and a rubber boot 74.4 h to dry enough, so a two-pair rotation (38 h rest) suffices only for canvas (18.3 h); in the humid still climate (28 °C, 90%) leather needs 149.8 h and rubber never reaches dry-enough, and no rotation of two or three pairs suffices for leather or rubber footwear.

Sole Cost per Kilometre: A Cost-per-Distance Ranking of Sole Materials a Low-Income Wearer Can Obtain

The problem. Low-income walkers choose soles by purchase price and overpay per kilometre walked because short-lived cheap soles must be bought again and again.

The result. Cut tyre rubber is the cheapest obtainable sole per distance at 0.1029 US dollars per 1,000 km over an 11,666.7 km life, about one seventeenth of leather's 1.80 dollars per 1,000 km; the cheapest purchase, layered cloth-and-resin at 0.30 dollars a pair, stops being cheap at 6,401 km, beyond which its cumulative cost exceeds the 1.20 dollars a tyre-rubber pair costs.

A Slip Bound for Sole Materials on Wet Hillside Surfaces

The problem. Falls on wet hillside paths and terraces injure people carrying loads, and sole choice for such ground has been made by habit rather than by any stated slip bound.

The result. Of 20 sole-material/wet-surface combinations, only 7 reach a 20-degree slope even at the bare slip threshold and none reaches it with a safety factor of 1.5 (best: lugged tyre tread on wet clay, μ = 0.50, safe angle 18.43 degrees); the worst common combination, smooth leather on mud (μ = 0.12), slips at 6.84 degrees, and flat rubber on mud would need 13.84 mm of lug depth — beyond the 12 mm practical cap — to meet the safe standard, while 9.84 mm suffices on wet clay.

A Minimum-Thickness Bound Against Sole Puncture by Nails and Thorns

The problem. Nail and thorn puncture wounds of the foot injure people who walk in thin soles, and there has been no stated thickness bound connecting walker weight, intruder, and material.

The result. At a safety factor of 2.0 against a steel nail delivering 961.4 N, the minimum sole thicknesses are 6.12 mm for recycled HDPE sheet, 7.65 mm for leather, 12.75 mm for tyre rubber, and 19.13 mm for hardwood; the thorn case (force capped at 180 N by thorn failure) never governs, no customary thickness meets the nail bound (as-obtained safety factors 1.05–1.63), and hardwood's bound carries a flexural rigidity about 2,799 times that of a flexible 5 mm leather sole.

The Stone-Bruise Bound: A Minimum Sole Stiffness and Thickness for Walking on Sharp Gravel

The problem. Stone bruises and plantar pain from walking on sharp gravel in thin, flexible footwear that does not spread a stone's point load.

The result. For a 75 kg walker on a 5 mm sharp stone against a 700 kPa pain threshold, the heel contact must spread to at least 14.5 mm radius — 11.9 mm of ordinary foam, 8.7 mm of stiff leather/rubber, or 7.3 mm of stiff moulded material — while a commonly sold 6 mm thin flexible sole delivers 2313 kPa at the heel, 3.3 times the threshold, and soles meeting the bound weigh only 164–525 g per pair with USD 0.66–4.72 of material.

Sun Protection of Common Cloth, and Its Collapse When Wet and Stretched: A Post-Penalty Selection Rule

The problem. Outdoor workers rely on ordinary clothing for sun protection, but dry garment ratings greatly overstate the protection a sweaty, stretched shirt actually delivers at midday.

The result. Of 14 common garments modelled, none retains the target protection factor of 15 after wetting and 10% stretching — the best, a dark woven polyester work shirt ($12 stated), retains 12.2, while a white cotton T-shirt falls from 2.5 dry to 1.4 and dark denim from 85.1 to 7.9; a specified dark woven polyester canvas construction (open fraction 0.012, thickness 0.85 mm, $18) does meet the target, retaining 16.9 after both penalties.

Thorn Protection in Cloth: A Layer Bound for Garments Worn in Thorn Scrub

The problem. Workers in thorn scrub are injured by thorns through ordinary clothing that was never graded against the forces real contact applies.

The result. Pressing contact (45 N, safety factor 1.25) sets a 56.25 N stack bound that no single common trouser fabric meets (twill 20 N, denim 35 N, canvas 55 N); canvas over trousers meets it at 69.0 N, 0.44 kg, and USD 6.60 for both legs, canvas gaiters over jeans give 79.5 N on the lower leg, and common practice fails — jeans 35 N and jersey garden gloves 5 N — while a 170 N stumble defeats every wearable cloth stack.

Wet Insulation Collapse: A Bound on the Insulation Common Fills Retain as They Take Up Water, Ranked by Insulation Retained per Dollar When Wet

The problem. Garments are rated dry but worn in rain, sweat, and river crossings, and the most trusted fills lose nearly all their insulation exactly when the wearer is wet and coldest.

The result. Soaked fills retain only 27.8% (polyester), 27.4% (kapok), 12.6% (plant fibre), 11.4% (wool), 7.5% (cotton), and 2.8% (down) of their dry insulation; ranked by insulation retained per dollar when soaked, plant fibre leads at 0.0987 clo per dollar and down is last at 0.0053, and wool in rain still loses about three-quarters of its value.

The Wind Shell Bound: How Much of a Loose Insulating Layer's Still-Air Value Moving Air Destroys, and the Shell Permeability That Restores It

The problem. Clothing is rated in still air but worn in wind, so people overestimate the warmth of loose garments and underestimate the value of a cheap windproof outer layer.

The result. An unshelled loose-knit garment loses half its still-air value at a wind speed of only 1.84 m/s and retains 20.1% at 10 m/s, while a shell of air permeability 10 mm/s retains 79.3% at 10 m/s; holding the loss under 20% up to 15 m/s requires permeability of 5 mm/s or below, and a loss under 10% at 5 m/s or above is unattainable at any permeability because the surface air layer alone loses 11.3% there.
