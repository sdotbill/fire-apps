An Honest Optics Bound for Adjustable-Focus Spectacles: Delivered Power Range, Usable Field, and the Astigmatism Ceiling of Alvarez and Fluid-Membrane Lenses

The problem. Adjustable spectacles promoted as replacing refractionists where eye care is scarce, without a computed bound on the power, field and wearer fraction they can actually serve.

The result. Alvarez spectacles deliver ±6.76 D and membrane spectacles ±5.11 D, but neither corrects astigmatism — cylinder alone exhausts the 6/12 budget at 1.60 D, coverage is 0.0% at 2.00 D or more, and only 66.4% (Alvarez) and 62.4% (membrane) of wearers reach 6/12 in both eyes.

A Body-Powered Prosthetic Hand Priced by Cable Force and Excursion Budgets

The problem. People who cannot afford or maintain a powered prosthesis need an honest specification of what a body-powered hand can and cannot hold all day.

The result. At the recommended voluntary-closing transmission ratio of 3.5 under a 35.0 N sustained cable ceiling, the printed hand delivers 13.0 N of all-day grip (40.8 N peak, 60.4 N absolute class ceiling) for $58.16 in parts, meeting light daily tasks but not jar opening or heavy tool use.

A Cost Bound on Refreshable Braille and a Latching-Actuator Display That Beats It on Paper

The problem. Refreshable Braille displays cost thousands of dollars for a single 40-cell line, putting screen access by touch out of reach for many blind readers.

The result. The piezoelectric cost floor computes to $78.70 per character (40-cell bill of materials $3803.00, estimated street $5514.35, inside the observed $3500–$6500 class), while the latching design computes to $24.16 per character and $1509.40 per line with 0.12 W standby, at the cost of 1.20 s worst-case refresh, 20.0 mm thickness, and 2.10 expected pin failures per line-year.

A Stopping-Distance Bound for Phone-Grade Obstacle Warning for White-Cane Users: Which Hazards a Cheap Sensor Can Honestly Cover

The problem. White canes miss head-level obstacles (signs, branches, open windows) and give only contact warning of drop-offs, causing head injuries and falls for blind and partially sighted pedestrians.

The result. Warning range needed is 1.18 m at 1.0 m/s and 1.67 m at 1.4 m/s (time-of-flight), but a 0.05 m pole is detected at only 0.95 m and a 0.03 m branch end at 0.57 m, so no phone-grade sensor covers thin targets in time while broad targets (3.40–4.00 m) and a chest-30° drop-off geometry (in time to 1.69 m/s) are covered.

Crutch and Cane Tip Failure Physics, and a Wide Wet-and-Mud Tip Design

The problem. Crutch and cane users fall when rubber tips slip on wet floors or sink and skate in mud, and are left unprotected as ferrule tips wear through.

The result. A standard tip slips at ordinary gait angles on wet tile (margin 0.821 at 15 degrees) and punches through mud at 524.6 kPa, while a proposed 90 mm cleated skirt tip cuts mud pressure to 40.5 kPa, limits sink depth to 34.1 mm, restores wet-surface slip margins above 1.0 at 15 degrees, and costs $2.11 per tip in parts.

A Pressure Bound for Wheelchair Cushions and a Low-Cost Cushion Specification That Respects It

The problem. Pressure ulcers that injure and kill long-term wheelchair users who cannot afford pressure-relief cushions.

The result. A $22.00 layered contoured cushion (chip-foam base, medium-foam top with soft ischial-well inserts) computes to a peak pressure of 57.3 mmHg and a 3.34 h safe interval at 70 kg and keeps the safe interval at or above 2.0 h for 40-100 kg users, against 97.0 mmHg and 1.52 h for a 50 mm flat foam slab at 70 kg.

Fall-Impact Flooring Priced by Impact Physics: How Much Compliance a Floor Needs to Keep a Hip Below the Fracture Threshold

The problem. Hip fractures in older people from sideways falls onto hard floors, and impact flooring sold at thicknesses that cannot physically protect the hip.

The result. A rigid floor delivers a computed 5,645 N to the hip of a 70 kg person falling sideways (fracture range 2,500–4,500 N), thin cork, rubber and foam underlays reduce this by only 0.1–6.7%, and meeting the 2,500 N target requires a floor deflecting 136.6 mm — 273.3 mm of foam at $3,260 per room or a sprung platform with 136.6 mm of travel at $3,786 per room.

A Hand-Crank Drivetrain for Rural Roads: Gearing Computed from an Arm-Power Budget and Unpaved-Surface Resistance

The problem. People whose legs cannot pedal are excluded from bicycle transport on rural unpaved roads because standard gearing assumes leg power and pavement rather than a 30–60 W arm budget and dirt-road rolling resistance.

The result. A US$280 commodity drivetrain (24T/40T chainrings, 11–51T cassette) cruises at 12.8 km/h on pavement and 7.4 km/h on flat packed dirt at 45 W and climbs to 6% on dirt within a 70 N hand-force bound, while sand is unreachable at any grade and dirt above 6.5% is force-closed at any gearing.

Hearing-Aid Fitting from First Principles on a Phone: Gain Targets, Latency, and Feedback Bounds

The problem. Most hearing loss is never fitted because audiology is scarce and hearing aids are priced as luxuries, leaving hundreds of millions without usable hearing support.

The result. A derived half-gain fitting matches stated NAL-style targets to 2.06 dB RMS, but latency (15.64 ms wired, 125.64 ms Bluetooth Classic) and feedback (maximum stable gain 9.0 dB open, 24.0 dB closed, 39.0 dB foam/custom) limit honest service to mild, moderate and sloping losses with a sealed wired fitting.

A Hip Protector Sized by the Energy It Must Actually Absorb, and the Fall-Energy Window in Which Passive Protection Works

The problem. Hip fractures in older adults from sideways falls, by sizing passive hip protection to the fall energy it must absorb and stating the energies above which it cannot protect.

The result. At a 3,000 N fracture threshold the protection window is 75.0–201.7 J, and absorbing the 48.6 J excess of a 70 kg standing fall (123.6 J) requires a 20.8 mm foam-plus-shell pad of 337.3 g per pair costing $17.68, which caps the trochanter force at 2,520 N.

A One-Handed Food-Preparation Station for Stroke Survivors, Priced by Force and Torque Budgets

The problem. Stroke survivors with one functional hand cannot simultaneously hold and cut food, hold and open jars, or stabilise a board, because ordinary kitchens assume two hands for every task.

The result. Friction alone holds food with margins of only 1.02 to 1.29 while a spike couple holds with worst-case margin 7.27, a mounted opener applies 3.75 N m against 1.01 N m for gripping a typical lid, rocking cuts the hardest peak force from 45 N to 19.5 N, and the station costs $43.00 in parts — with stubborn 5.0 N m lids failing at full size.

A Low-Cost Energy-Storing Prosthetic Foot from Flat Spring Stock: Sizing a Leaf Spring by Gait Physics for Body Masses of 40 to 100 kg

The problem. People with below-knee amputation in low-income settings must walk on rigid low-cost feet that return almost no push-off energy, raising the metabolic cost of walking, because commercial energy-storing feet are priced far out of reach.

The result. The 80 kg design (9.5 mm glass-fibre strip) stores 9.14 J and returns 8.04 J per step at 187.8 MPa with a modelled fatigue life of 9,849,082 cycles and a total parts cost of $27.96, for an estimated 8.6% metabolic saving against a SACH foot.

A Stance-Stability Bound for Passive Prosthetic Knees and a Low-Cost Single-Axis Design That Satisfies It

The problem. Affordable passive prosthetic knees buckling during stance because alignment is set without a quantitative stability bound, causing falls for users who cannot access powered knees.

The result. For a 70 kg user at 1.0 m/s the stable alignment window is 20.5 to 86.6 mm, covered by a 0 to 50 mm sliding-plate single-axis knee with 3.92 to 22.4 N m adjustable friction at 51.00 USD parts cost.

How Much Uncorrected Refractive Error Can Ready-Made Spectacles Actually Fix? A Coverage Bound as a Function of Astigmatism and Anisometropia

The problem. People with uncorrected refractive error in places where custom refraction is scarce are given ready-made spectacles whose actual coverage — and the high astigmats, anisometropes, and high myopes they structurally leave behind — has been asserted rather than computed.

The result. Ready-made spectacles (0.50 D steps, −6.00 to +4.00 D, optimistic matching) cover 95.2% of the stated fully-correctable population on a better-eye 6/12 criterion but only 59.8% when both eyes must reach 6/12, with uncorrected cylinder failing above 1.61 D and a minimum stock of three powers (+1.50, −1.50, −3.50 D) covering 77.1%.

A Tip-Over Bound for Rollators and Walkers, and a Geometry Change That Widens It

The problem. Rollators tipping over — on slopes, at door thresholds, and during stumble-catches — under the people who lean on them because their balance is already compromised.

The result. At a 20% handle lean the standard rollator's forward tip angle is 10.59 degrees and its threshold-arrest critical speed is 0.563 m/s (below slow walking), while the extended geometry raises those to 17.74 degrees and 0.948 m/s and cuts the 15 mm threshold force from 186.2 N to 140.3 N — but a 1.5 m/s full-weight stumble-catch still carries 2.24 times the energy any passive base can absorb.

A Wheelchair That Survives Unpaved Ground: Step-Climbing and Soil Rolling Resistance Computed from Wheel Geometry, and a Long-Wheelbase Design Priced from Bicycle Parts

The problem. Wheelchair users are confined to paved ground because small front casters cannot climb ordinary steps and narrow wheels sink into unpaved soil.

The result. A long-wheelbase chair with 660 mm bicycle wheels at all four corners needs 49.5 N on packed dirt and mounts a 63.6 mm step head-on versus 25.9 mm for a standard caster chair, at 837 USD in parts, but still fails on loose sand and wet clay with rear sinkage of 57.5 mm and 54.9 mm.

The problem. Older or weak users who cannot rise from a chair without a carer present.

The result. From a standard 45 cm chair, a 5th-percentile-strength older user fails at 60 kg and above (48.7–73.1 N·m per leg against 48.0 N·m capacity), and foot placement plus arm push ($0) or that plus a $14 seat raiser brings the whole 50–90 kg class under a 38.4 N·m per leg ceiling at a 1.25 safety factor.
