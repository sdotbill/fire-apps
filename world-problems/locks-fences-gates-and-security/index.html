Locks, Fences, Gates and Security

The house defended at its edges: locks, bolts, fences and gates, each tested against the force or the search it actually faces.

Euro Cylinder Protrusion: Testing the Three-Millimetre Rule Against a Wrench Torque Model

The result. Euro-profile cylinders that stand proud of the door furniture can be gripped and snapped, and fitting guidance commonly allows at most 3 mm of protrusion. This study tests that rule against a stated torque model in which a wrench attack develops T = 18 + 2.6 p newton-metres at protrusion p millimetres, a standard cylinder bridge fails at 28 Nm, and a reinforced cylinder with a sacrificial cut fails at 55 Nm. The standard cylinder's margin is exhausted at a protrusion of 3.9 mm: at the customary 3 mm the attack torque is 25.8 Nm, already close to the bridge strength, while at 10 mm it reaches 44.0 Nm and the cylinder fails with room to spare. The reinforced cylinder holds its margin until 14.3 mm of protrusion. The 3 mm rule is therefore confirmed as a sensible fitting limit for standard cylinders, but it is a limit with little slack, and the calculation argues for measuring protrusion at fitting and for choosing a reinforced cylinder whenever the door and furniture combination cannot be brought under about 3 mm. Proven in simulation.

Deadbolt Throw Length: How Much Engagement Depth a Longer Bolt Actually Buys Against Frame Splitting

The result. A deadbolt is sold by its throw, the distance the bolt travels into the frame, yet what a household needs is resistance to the frame splitting around the strike. This study models that resistance from stated assumptions: a steel bolt of 11 mm by 20 mm section has a shear capacity near 61,600 N and never governs, while the timber frame around the strike pocket is modelled as failing at F = 700 + 330 e^0.8 newtons, where e is the engagement depth in millimetres after a 4 mm allowance for door gap and strike recess. A standard 25 mm throw then resists 4470 N and delays entry by about 3.99 minutes under the stated attack model, while the longest throw tabulated, 38 mm, resists 6242 N, a gain of 39.6 percent, for a delay of 5.26 minutes. Resistance reaches the 5,000 N level used here as a working target only at a throw of 28.8 mm. The practical rule is therefore to specify engagement depth, not catalogue throw, and to expect diminishing returns beyond about 32 mm because the exponent on engagement is well below one. Proven in simulation.

The Door Chain Claim Examined: Why a Chain Assembly Cannot Hold What Its Packaging Suggests

The result. Door chains are sometimes sold with language suggesting they withstand hundreds of kilograms of force. This study examines a claim of 750 kg, about 7,350 N, by computing the strength of each element of a typical chain assembly from stated assumptions: the chain itself fails in tension at 4,200 N, its keep plate bends at 1,800 N, and the anchor plate's four small screws, embedded 16 mm into the edge grain of a timber door, pull out as a system at about 676 N. An assembly fails at its weakest element, so the fitted chain resists about 676 N, which is 9.2 percent of the claimed figure. Even doubling the screw embedment to 32 mm raises the assembly only to 1351 N, at which point the bending plate governs. The claim is therefore not sustained for the assembly as fitted, whatever the chain link alone may hold on a test machine. A door chain should be understood as a device for identifying a caller while the door is ajar, not as a forced-entry barrier, and households relying on it for resistance should redirect the same money to the strike and frame. Proven in simulation.

Boundary Fence Height: Climb Delay, Cost per Metre, and the Height at Which Extra Panels Stop Paying

The result. A boundary fence delays rather than stops, and the useful question is how much delay each additional increment of height buys for its cost. This study models unaided climb delay with the logistic relation t = 30 / (1 + exp(-2.6 (h - 1.75))) seconds for height h in metres, and fence cost as USD 34 + 52 (h - 1.2)^1.25 per metre run, both stated relations. Delay rises from 5.8 seconds at 1.2 m to 16.0 seconds at 1.8 m and 25.3 seconds at 2.4 m, while cost rises from USD 34.0 to USD 99.31 per metre over the same span. Delay bought per USD 100 peaks at 2.0 m in the table. A trellis or angled topping adding a stated 9 seconds at any height improves the 1.8 m fence by 56.2 percent for USD 11 per metre, which is a better purchase than the final increment of panel height. The resulting guidance is to build to about 1.8 m where regulations allow, add a topping that is awkward to grip, and treat heights beyond about 2.2 m as spending on appearance and privacy rather than on delay. Proven in simulation.

Fence Post Spacing Under Wind Load: The Widest Bay a Timber Post Can Carry Before Bending Governs

The result. A solid garden fence is a sail, and its posts are the masts. This study computes post bending stress under a stated gust pressure of 0.55 kN per square metre on a 1.8 m solid fence, with each interior post carrying one bay and acting as a 125 mm square timber section of stated allowable bending stress 8 MPa. Stress rises in direct proportion to bay width: 4.92 MPa at the conventional 1.8 m spacing, reaching the allowable limit at a spacing of 2.92 m and 8.2 MPa at 3.0 m. Widening bays from 1.8 m to 3.0 m would save about 6.7 posts over a 30 m run, but it spends the entire structural margin to do so. The practical rule is to keep bays at or below about 2.4 m on exposed sites, where a margin of roughly a fifth remains, and to treat the common 3 m panel span as suitable only for sheltered gardens or stronger posts. Proven in simulation.

Timber Gate Sag: Diagonal Brace Angle and the Widest Gate That Still Latches After Five Summers

The result. A timber garden gate that sags drops at its latch edge until the latch misses its keep, at which point the gate stops performing any security function at all. This study models sag from stated assumptions: an unbraced ledged gate drops 4.5 W^3 millimetres at width W metres after five seasons, a correctly fitted diagonal brace removes a fraction of that drop peaking at a brace angle of 45 degrees, and a latch tolerates 10 mm of drop. Unbraced, the widest gate that still latches is 1.3 m; braced, it is 1.96 m. At the common width of 1.5 m the brace angle is 35.0 degrees, the unbraced drop is 15.2 mm, and bracing reduces it to 3.1 mm. The brace must run from the lower hinge corner toward the upper latch corner; a brace fitted the other way assists the sag it was meant to prevent. The rule is to brace every ledged gate wider than about a metre and to check the brace direction before checking anything else. Proven in simulation.

How Many Different Keys a Five-Pin Lock Really Has: Counting Usable Bitting Combinations Under an Adjacent-Cut Rule

The result. A five-pin cylinder with six depth steps per pin is often described as offering 7,776 different keys, the value of six to the fifth power. That figure counts combinations a manufacturer cannot actually cut, because a working key must satisfy the maximum adjacent cut rule: neighbouring depths that differ too steeply produce a ramp the key cannot slide past the pins. This study enumerates all 7,776 combinations exactly and tests each against a stated adjacent-cut limit of three steps. Only 4034 combinations survive, so 48.1 percent of the advertised space is unusable, and two randomly keyed locks share a key with probability 0.0248 percent. Among 200 houses keyed at random from the usable set, the expected number of pairs sharing a key is 4.93. The figures argue for reading lock specifications by their usable combinations rather than their pin count, and for preferring six-pin cylinders or additional depth steps where key uniqueness across a street or a housing development actually matters. Proven in simulation.

Worn Keypad Digits: How Visible Wear on a Code Lock Collapses Its Search Space

The result. A keypad lock's code is only as secret as its buttons are unworn. When daily pressing polishes or fades a subset of digits, an observer learns which digits the code contains, and the search space collapses. This study counts the collapse exactly, by enumerating all 10,000 four-digit codes and retaining those built only from the worn digits. If wear reveals four distinct digits, only 256 candidate codes remain, a reduction by a factor of 39.1, exhaustible in 25.6 minutes at six seconds per attempt without a lockout. Three worn digits leave 81 candidates and two worn digits leave 16. A six-digit code with four worn digits leaves 4096 candidates, or about 6.8 hours of continuous trying, which restores practical safety even when fully worn. The rules that follow are to prefer six-digit codes, to choose codes whose digits repeat so wear reveals less, to clean or replace worn keypads, and to treat a lockout feature as worth more than extra digits against a patient attacker. Proven in simulation.

Letterbox Reach: The Minimum Distance Between a Mail Slot and the Inside Latch That Defeats a Hooked Tool

The result. A mail slot through a front door is also a hole through which a hooked wire can be worked toward the inside latch, and on many doors the latch sits within easy reach of the slot. This study models the geometry from stated assumptions: a 700 mm hooked tool, after losses at the flap, the door thickness and its own bends, commands an effective radius of 520 mm from the slot centre, while an internal cover box behind the slot reduces that radius to 180 mm. A latch is taken as vulnerable when its distance from the slot is within the effective radius, and a safety margin of 80 mm is added for the design rule. On that basis an unprotected door wants at least 600 mm between slot and latch, a distance many doors, where the slot sits in the mid-rail a few hundred millimetres from the latch, do not have. With a cover box fitted, the required separation falls to 260 mm, which almost any door satisfies. The cheapest robust rule is therefore not to move the latch but to cage the slot, and to keep keys and thumbturns out of the unprotected radius in the meantime. Proven in simulation.

Motion-Activated Lighting: The Mounting Height That Covers a Driveway Without Leaving a Dark Path Beneath the Sensor

The result. A motion-activated light protects only the ground its sensor actually sees, and mounting height moves two boundaries at once: the far edge of detection, set by the sensor's 12 m slant range, and the dead zone beneath the fitting, which grows with height. This study samples a 5 m by 8 m driveway on a 0.1 m grid from stated assumptions, testing each point against the range limit and a dead zone whose radius is 0.45 times the mounting height. Coverage is highest at the lowest tabulated height and falls as the dead zone swallows the near corner: 97.5 percent at 2.5 m against 90.2 percent at 5 m, where the dead radius reaches 2.25 m and an approach hugging the wall passes beneath the sensor entirely. The practical rule balances this geometry against reach and tampering: mount the sensor between about 2.5 m and 3.5 m, above casual reach but low enough that the dead zone stays under about 1.6 m, and aim the fitting across the likely approach path rather than along the wall it is mounted on. Proven in simulation.

Padlock Shackle Diameter: The Thickness at Which Hand Bolt Cutters Run Out of Jaw Force

The result. A padlock is usually attacked through its shackle with bolt cutters, and whether the attack succeeds is a contest between the force the jaws can develop and the force the shackle's cross-section demands. This study models the demand as F = 330 d^2 newtons for shackle diameter d in millimetres, a stated relation combining hardened steel shear strength with jaw wedge action, and sets it against stated jaw capacities of 17,000 N for a compact 450 mm cutter and 38,000 N for a full-size 900 mm cutter. The compact cutter is defeated at a shackle diameter of 7.1 mm and the full-size cutter at 10.7 mm; an 8 mm shackle requires 21120 N and an 11 mm shackle 39930 N. Diameter is therefore a strong defence because demand grows with its square, but it is a defence with a ceiling, since a determined attacker can bring a larger tool. The purchasing rule is to specify a shackle of at least 10 mm in hardened steel, shrouded where possible so the jaws cannot seat, and to remember that the hasp and chain beside the lock must be graded to match. Proven in simulation.

Pool Gate Latch Height: The Placement at Which a Latch Leaves a Young Child's Reach Without Leaving an Adult's

The result. A gate between a garden and a swimming pool must be operable by every adult and by almost no young child, and the dimension that decides the matter is latch height. This study models a child's chance of reaching and operating a latch as a logistic function of height, with a midpoint of 1.38 m for five-year-olds and 1.43 m for six-year-olds, both stated values, and asks where the chance falls below one percent. At 1.35 m a five-year-old still succeeds 66.08 percent of the time; at 1.5 m the figure is 6.5 percent. The one-percent level for five-year-olds is reached at 1.587 m, and for six-year-olds at 1.637 m, which sits close to the 1.65 m level above which shorter adults begin to find a latch awkward. The window between child resistance and adult convenience is therefore narrow but real: a latch placed between about 1.60 m and 1.65 m, ideally shielded so that reach alone is not enough, satisfies both sides of the requirement on the stated model. Proven in simulation.

Sliding Door Security Bars: The Dowel Diameter at Which a Timber Bar Stops Buckling Under a Forcing Load

The result. A length of timber dropped into the track of a sliding patio door is among the cheapest security devices sold, and among the least specified: dowels are bought by eye, in diameters that vary by a factor of two. This study computes the buckling load of a 0.9 m pine dowel from the Euler relation with a stated elastic modulus of 9 GPa, and compares it with a stated forcing load of 2,600 N at which the door's rollers and track begin to deform. A 25 mm dowel buckles at 2103 N, below the forcing load, so the door's own hardware is not the weakest element; the bar is. The buckling load reaches 2,600 N at a diameter of 26.4 mm, and a 32 mm dowel carries 5645 N at a stated price of USD 12.96. Because buckling load grows with the fourth power of diameter, small increases in thickness are disproportionately effective, and the rule is to fit a bar of at least 30 mm diameter, cut to a close fit in the track so that it is loaded axially rather than bent. Proven in simulation.

The Hidden Spare Key: How Much of a Burglar's Search the Common Hiding Places Already Cover

The result. A spare key hidden outside the house is a second front door whose lock is the obscurity of its hiding place, and obscurity can be priced. This study uses a stated distribution of hiding places among households that hide a key, from 22 percent under a mat down to single-figure novelties, and models an intruder who searches places in order of popularity at 40 seconds per place. The three commonest places, mat, fake rock and plant pot, already cover 49 percent of hidden keys; the first five places cover 66 percent and take 3.33 minutes to search; eight places cover 83 percent in 5.33 minutes. Only the tenth category, genuinely original places at 10 percent, lies outside a short search. The conclusion is that a hidden key in any commonly used place should be treated as a key handed to a patient stranger, and the safe alternatives are a coded key safe, which replaces obscurity with a combination, or a key left with a person rather than an object. Proven in simulation.

Strike Plate Screws: The Length at Which a Door Strike Stops Being the Weakest Part of the Lock

The result. Many deadbolts are defeated not at the bolt but at the strike plate, whose short screws bite only into the thin frame facing rather than the structural timber behind it. This study computes strike resistance from stated assumptions: a 4 mm screw embedded in softwood resists pull-out at 35.2 N per millimetre of embedment, four screws share load with a factor of 0.7, and the frame itself splits at 5,400 N, capping the assembly. The short 19 mm screws supplied with many locks give a system resistance of only 1873 N. Resistance climbs linearly with length until, at 54.8 mm, the screws would hold as much as the frame itself can bear; the 76 mm screws tabulated reach the 5400 N cap, an improvement by a factor of 2.88 over the short screws, for a total screw cost of USD 0.48. Beyond the crossover, longer screws buy nothing because the frame becomes the failing part. The resulting rule is simple: fit strike screws of at least 65 mm that reach the structural timber, and spend any further money on the frame rather than on still longer screws. Proven in simulation.

Thorn Hedge Planting Spacing: Closing the Gaps in a Living Barrier Before Its Second Winter

The result. A thorn hedge is a fence that grows, and its weak years are its first ones, when the whips stand in a row of separate bushes with walk-through gaps between them. This study models shrub spread as r(t) = 0.18 + 0.22 t metres from stated values, and computes when neighbouring canopies meet at each planting spacing: at 0.9 m spacing the hedge closes in 1.23 years, at 1.5 m spacing it takes 2.59 years, a full extra growing season and more spent as a dotted line rather than a barrier. Crossing delay at maturity, computed from a stated stem density, is 40 seconds for a single 0.6 m row at 0.9 m spacing and 80 seconds for a double staggered row of 1.2 m depth. Planting cost at 0.9 m spacing is 12 plants per 10 m. The rule is to plant thorn hedging at 0.9 m or closer in a double staggered row where the ground allows, accepting the plant cost as the price of closing the barrier before its second winter, and to protect the young hedge with a temporary fence rather than pretending it is already a barrier. Proven in simulation.

Window Sash Lock Spacing: How Many Locks a Wide Window Needs Before Prying Flexes the Rail Open

The result. A wide sliding or casement window fitted with a single central lock can be opened by prying at a corner, flexing the meeting rail until the latch clears its keeper, without breaking anything. This study models the rail as a beam from stated values: a 700 N pry, an aluminium rail of elastic modulus 69 GPa and second moment of area 7.5e-9 m^4, and a 6 mm flex at which the latch disengages. Deflection grows with the cube of the unsupported span, reaching 6.09 mm at 0.6 m and 14.43 mm at 0.8 m, and the widest span that stays below the escape gap is 0.59 m. A window 1.8 m wide therefore needs 3 locks rather than the customary one, at a stated hardware cost of USD 27, while a 1.2 m window needs 2. The rule is to space window locks no more than about 0.55 m apart along the meeting rail, and to count a window's locks by its width rather than by habit. Proven in simulation.
