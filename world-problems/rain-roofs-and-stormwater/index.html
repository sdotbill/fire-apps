Rain, Roofs and Stormwater

Rain caught from the roof, kept clean in the tank, and led safely away: gutters, downpipes, storage and drainage sized from the storm itself.

Counted, Not Placed for Symmetry: How Many Downpipes a Roof Actually Needs

The result. Counting downpipes from rational-method design flow rather than elevation symmetry: a 200 m2 roof at 150 mm/h produces 8.33 L/s and needs three 82 mm or two 110 mm downpipes, so the customary pair of 82 mm pipes is undersized, and every roof needs at least two outlets for blockage redundancy.

The Level Gutter Is a Sediment Trap: Minimum Fall for Self-Cleansing in a 125 mm Half-Round Gutter

The result. For a 125 mm half-round gutter flowing half full, a level installation gives zero sediment transport: reaching the 0.6 m/s self-cleansing velocity requires a fall of 0.443 percent and moving 2 mm grit requires 0.528 percent, so gutters should be installed at about 0.6 percent and checked over the full run.

One Millimetre for Every Roof? First-Flush Diversion Depth Set by Wash-Off Behaviour

The result. Against the exponential wash-off model, the popular fixed 1 mm first-flush rule captures only 19.7 percent of the load on a typical suburban roof; 90 percent capture needs 10.47 mm there and 19.19 mm under trees, while the extra diversion costs only a few percent of the annual harvest.

Buy the Knee, Not the Tank: A Daily Water Balance Locates Where Rainwater Storage Stops Paying

The result. A twenty-year daily water balance for a 110 m2 roof and a demand averaging about 130 L/day shows reliability rising from 87.4 percent at 1,000 litres to 99.5 percent at 3,000 litres and reaching 100 percent at 5,000 litres, while overflow stays near 27,000 litres a year at every large size — so buyers should purchase the knee of the curve, around 3,000 to 5,000 litres, not the largest tank on offer.

Maintenance-Free for How Long? Debris Matting and Bypass Growth on Guarded Gutters

The result. Modelling debris matting on a guarded gutter, bypass of a 2.0 L/s design inflow first exceeds ten percent at month 4 under trees and month 9 on an open roof, reaching 62.0 percent under trees by month 12 — so the product relocates maintenance rather than abolishing it, with service intervals of about a season under trees and about a year on an open roof.

Depth Is a Load Before It Is a Sponge: Substrate Depth, Retention, and the Structural Cap on Green Roofs

The result. Substrate depth buys annual runoff retention with diminishing returns — 72.8 percent at 40 mm, 86.9 percent at 100 mm, 92.4 percent at 200 mm — while saturated load rises linearly, so a stated spare allowance of 1.5 kN/m2 caps a retrofit roof at 122 mm of substrate: set depth from the structure first, then take the retention the permitted depth provides.

An Expiry Date for Permeable Paving: Sediment Loading, Infiltration Decay, and the Sweeping Interval

The result. With sediment-driven clogging, a permeable driveway laid at 250 mm/h falls to its 40 mm/h design floor in 3.7 years at street-side loading and 7.4 years sheltered; vacuum sweeping restores a threshold surface to 176 mm/h against 92 mm/h for ordinary sweeping, giving maintenance intervals of roughly three and six years.

Drawn to Fit the Site, Sized by the Flow: The Length a Grass Swale Needs to Treat Runoff

The result. At a stated first-order removal rate, eighty percent suspended-solids removal needs 14.6 minutes of residence time, which for a standard trapezoidal swale at 0.15 m treatment depth means 55.7 m of length at 10 L/s and 111.5 m at 20 L/s — swales must be sized by the flow they treat, not drawn to fit the site.

Three Hundred Millimetres of What? Ponding Depth Set by Soil Drawdown, Not by the Standard Detail

The result. Drawdown arithmetic displaces the standard 300 mm ponding detail: at stated soil infiltration rates a 300 mm pond clears in 50 hours on loam and 120 hours on clay loam against a 48-hour limit, so ponding depth must be set at no more than forty-eight times the measured infiltration rate — 288 mm on loam, 120 mm on clay loam — with slow soils answered by area or an underdrain, not depth.

Two Gates for a Soakaway: Storage Scanned Over Storm Durations, and a Recovery Test That Size Cannot Pass

The result. Sizing a soakaway for a 120 m2 catchment by maximising storage over eight storm durations gives 3.48 cubic metres on sandy loam (critical duration 6 hours, half-emptying 9.7 hours) and 6.17 cubic metres on clay loam, whose half-emptying time of 171.4 hours fails the 24-hour recovery gate at any size and disqualifies infiltration on that soil.

A Square Metre of Roof Is Not a Square Metre of Water: Annual Harvest by Covering

The result. On the same 100 m2 roof under 800 mm of rain, annual harvest after system losses ranges from 69,920 litres for standing-seam metal to 25,760 litres for an extensive green roof — a factor of 2.71 set by the covering alone — so yield calculators, tank sizes, and payback claims that ask only for area and rainfall inherit that error whole.

Blamed on the Arc: Sheet Flow Cannot Jump a Gutter, but Rivulets Can

The result. Trajectory calculations acquit uniform sheet flow of gutter overshoot — its throw at 300 mm/h is 0.085 m against a standard lip offset of about 0.125 m — and convict concentrated rivulet flow, which occupying a tenth of the slope width throws 0.181 m at the same intensity: overshoot is a concentration problem, remedied by breaking up threads and valley discharges or placing the gutter for the concentrated throw.

The Drip Lands Further Out Than the Trench: Width, Wind, and Impact Energy at an Unguttered Eave

The result. Water leaving a 3.5 m eave lands 0.253 m from the wall in still air, so a trench must be 0.45 m wide once splash is allowed for, and the conventional 300 mm trench misses the drip line even with no wind; a 2 m/s wind raises the required width to 0.70 m, a 600 mm trench tolerates only about 1.55 m/s of wind at that height, and impact energy rises from 34.4 J per litre at 3.5 m to 68.7 J per litre at 7.0 m.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24120029

A Barrier Is a Pump Specification: Hydrostatic Load, Sandbag Count, and Leakage at a Household Flood Door

The result. Floodwater 0.6 m deep exerts 1,589 N across a 0.90 m doorway — four times the 397 N at 0.3 m, because load grows with the square of depth — a sandbag wall for that duty takes about 93 bags per metre (roughly 139 for a doorway with returns), and even a well-compressed seal passes 11.1 L/min across the door at 0.6 m depth, so a barrier specification is incomplete without a leakage class and a matched pump.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24120033

The Screen Sizes the Overflow: Mosquito Mesh, Surcharge Head, and Vent Area on a Covered Rainwater Tank

The result. A 1.2 mm mosquito screen leaves an overflow only 46.8 percent of its bare capacity, so a 100 square metre roof delivering 4.17 L/s needs a 136 mm screened overflow (select 150 mm) where bare-pipe arithmetic suggests 93 mm, a screened 75 mm overflow passes only 1.27 L/s, and a pumped draw-off of 30 L/min needs a screened vent of just 10.4 mm — making vent blockage, not vent size, the real hazard.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24120040

Dose Past the Demand, Then Mind the Calendar: Chlorine, Contact Time, and Re-Dosing for Stored Roof Water

The result. A 1.0 mg/L chlorine dose in water with a demand of 1.5 mg/L leaves no free residual and disinfects nothing at any waiting time; the typical case (demand 0.8 mg/L, dose 2.0 mg/L, residual 1.2 mg/L, 40 mL of 5 percent bleach per 1,000 L) reaches the bacterial CT target of 15 mg·min/L in 12.5 minutes, and its protection lasts 35.8 days in a cool dark covered tank but only 4.5 days in a warm light-admitting one, so re-dosing is set by the store, not the dose.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24120044

Clean by the Mound, Not by the Calendar: A Sediment Accumulation Rule for Rainwater Cisterns

The result. Sediment concentrates near the inlet, so the typical tank (60 g/m²/yr, no first-flush diverter) builds local depth at 3.57 mm per year and reaches a 50 mm cleaning trigger in 14.0 years rather than the 70 years a whole-floor average suggests; intervals across load classes run from 4.2 years (ageing shingles, no diverter) to 117.6 years (clean metal roof, diverter fitted), a 28-fold spread, and a maintained diverter multiplies any interval by 2.8.
