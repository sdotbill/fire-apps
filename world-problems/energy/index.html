Energy where people live

Power and heat for homes and villages the grid fails: storage that pays for itself, refrigeration without batteries, cooking without smoke, and the storage ideas that do not survive arithmetic, disproved in print.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108419

A Village-Fabricable Chimney Rocket Stove for Clean Cooking: Design, Thermal Performance, Draft, and Indoor Exposure Estimates

The problem. Indoor smoke from open biomass cooking fires, which harms the health of cooks and children and consumes large amounts of gathered wood every day.

The result. The modelled stove cooks a stated day's food on 1.202 kg of wood instead of 3.505 kg and brings carbon monoxide below the guideline level used, but its fine-particle average remains 6.68 times the WHO 24-hour guideline.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108436

Sand-Battery Thermal Storage for Home Heating: A First-Principles Residential Design for Three Sunless Days in a Cold Climate

The problem. Heating a home through cold, sunless days without high-cost batteries or high fuel bills.

The result. A 4.51-tonne sand store in 2.82 cubic metres holds enough heat for three sunless days in the stated cold-climate house, beats direct electric heating when charged with surplus solar and used often, but usually loses to a heat pump and cannot be filled from winter rooftop solar in the stated case.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108475

Building-Scale Gravity Energy Storage for Off-Grid Homes: A First-Principles Design and Cost Test

The problem. Off-grid homes need overnight electricity storage that does not depend on short-lived, hard-to-replace chemical batteries in places with thin supply chains.

The result. Gravity storage fails at household scale — 235.8 tonnes at 10 metres delivers only 5 kWh overnight at about $1.96 per kWh versus $0.11 for lithium — and beats lead-acid only as a shared village hillside system of about 25 kWh per night or more.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108531

Battery-Free Solar Refrigeration for Off-Grid Clinics and Homes: A Direct-Drive Design with Ice-Bank Thermal Storage

The problem. Clinics and homes without reliable electricity lose vaccines, medicines, and food when refrigeration fails, and the batteries that normally carry a solar refrigerator through the night are costly, short-lived, and hard to replace in remote places.

The result. A 600 Wp direct-drive solar refrigerator with a 55.2 kg ice bank holds a 200-litre cabinet at 5 °C in a 32 °C ambient through three sunless days and costs USD 4,830 less over ten years than the battery-based equivalent.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108666

An Iron-Air Battery from Abundant Materials for Household and Village Electricity Storage: A First-Principles Design and Cost Test

The problem. Homes and villages where lithium batteries are unaffordable or unavailable need electricity storage built only from abundant, locally obtainable materials.

The result. An iron-air battery loses to lithium iron phosphate for daily cycling at 10-hour duration because its round-trip efficiency is only 58.6 percent, but at 48-hour duration it delivers energy at $0.104 per kWh — about a quarter of the lithium cost — and it beats lead-acid at every duty tested.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108667

Small-Scale Compressed-Air Energy Storage for a Village or Workshop: A First-Principles Design, Efficiency and Cost Test

The problem. Unreliable, short-lived battery storage for evening lighting and phone charging in off-grid villages and workshops, where a long-lived store built from air, steel and maintainable machinery would be valuable if it worked out cheaper.

The result. For evening light and phone charging for 50 households, village compressed-air storage returns only 15.9% of its charging electricity without heat storage (28.3% with it) and costs $1.545 per delivered kWh against $0.324 for lithium iron phosphate — and even with a free vessel the machinery alone costs more than a complete battery system — so it wins only where a tested vessel already exists, the charging power is truly surplus, and the air also does direct workshop work.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108691

A Village Microgrid Architecture Designed to Survive Its Own Failure Modes

The problem. A single failed component — one inverter, one battery string, one departed technician — taking an entire village's electricity down at once.

The result. In an hourly simulation of a low-sun week, a 60-household village microgrid built as four independent clusters with a direct-current path for critical loads served 72.3% of demand through a dead inverter where a conventional single-inverter design of the same capacity served 0.0%, at a premium of $321.66 per household (14.19%).

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108732

Waste-Heat Recovery for a Small Intermittent Brick Kiln: Air Preheat and Brick Drying, with Costs, Failure Modes and the Cases Where It Is Not Worth Building

The problem. Small kilns and workshops in the developing world vent most of their fuel energy as hot exhaust while paying real money, or spending real labour, for every kilogram of that fuel.

The result. A bypassed steel-tube recuperator plus a brick-drying shed recovers 11.21 GJ per batch from a 10,000-brick intermittent kiln, saving 747 kg of firewood per batch (19.7%) and paying back in 2.12 years at 70 batches per year and 75 dollars per tonne of wood, while neither an electric tail nor a low-duty or cheap-fuel case survives the arithmetic.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108893

Solar Water Pumping That Stores Water, Not Electricity: A Battery-Free Design for Drinking Water and Small-Plot Irrigation

The problem. People away from a reliable electricity supply need water at all hours, and the battery that conventional solar pumping uses to provide it is expensive, short-lived in heat, and hard to replace or dispose of — this design stores the water itself instead, in an elevated tank.

The result. A 340 Wp direct-drive array with a 6.67 m³ elevated tank delivers 3.265 m³ per day in the worst month for the stated 3,000 litre daily demand and costs $5,756.54 over 20 years in present value ($0.263 per m³), saving $1,050.39 against a lithium battery-buffered equivalent.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108986

Water as a Seasonal Heat Store: A First-Principles Design for Half of a Cold-Climate Winter, Its Six-Month Loss, and the Scale at Which the Loss Becomes Acceptable

The problem. Heating homes through cold winters with summer sunshine instead of winter fuel, and knowing honestly at what scale storing summer heat in water works and at what scale it does not.

The result. A buried 202.3 cubic metre water tank can deliver half of the stated cold-climate house's yearly heat, but it loses 35.7 percent of the stored heat over six months and delivers heat at $1.167 per kilowatt-hour against $0.148 for a heat pump, and the loss fraction only falls to 15 percent or below when about twenty houses share one store.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24108995

Flywheel Energy Storage at Household and Village Scale: A Reference Design, Its Self-Discharge, and the Duties It Can and Cannot Serve

The problem. Households and villages whose solar batteries die young in the heat and must be replaced every few years, where a store with no chemistry that could cycle for decades would be valuable if its costs and losses allowed it.

The result. A 71.7 kg carbon-fibre flywheel can deliver a household's 2.00 kWh overnight load, but its 42 W standing loss drains 1.41% of its stored energy per hour — empty in about 71 hours — so at one cycle per day it costs $1.084 per delivered kWh against $0.376 for lithium iron phosphate, and it wins only at 10 or more cycles per day, in short power smoothing, and in minutes-long bridging.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24109015

Thermochemical Heat Storage for Homes: A Strontium Bromide Seasonal Store Designed From First Principles

The problem. Houses need heat most in winter when solar heat is scarcest, and ordinary hot stores leak their charge away while they wait for winter.

The result. A strontium bromide store holding 3,500 kg of salt stores 795.0 kWh usable in 8.16 m³, retains 0.996 of its charge over four months, and delivers 779.8 kWh, but at $4.93 per delivered kWh in seasonal use it loses decisively to a heat pump at $0.166 per kWh and to a seasonal water tank at $0.92 per kWh.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24109093

Household Biogas from the Waste a Family Actually Has: An Honest Energy Budget and a Digester Design for a Six-Person Household with Three Cattle

The problem. Families are sold or given biogas plants sized from assumed waste and promised full cooking coverage; when the gas runs short the plant is blamed and abandoned. This paper gives the honest fraction before anything is built, and states how many animals, or how much extra waste, full coverage would actually take.

The result. A digester sized from a family's real waste — 18 kg of collectable dung and 1.5 kg of kitchen waste a day — covers 53% of that family's cooking energy over a year, falling to 38% in a cold spell.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24109105

Pico-Hydro Power Without a Dam: A Run-of-Stream Design for a 45-Household Hill Village, Sized to Its Dry Season

The problem. Hill and mountain villages that sit beside perennial streams but lack electricity able to carry productive loads such as milling, cold storage, and pumped water.

The result. Sized to its dry season, a dam-free pico-hydro plant on the reference stream delivers 12.35 kW continuous in its driest month — 1.73 times the village's daily energy demand — at $0.060 per kWh served, against $0.42 for solar plus battery delivering the same continuous service.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24109110

A Solar Cooker That Can Cook the Evening Meal: A Panel Cooker with an Oil Thermal Store and a Retained-Heat Basket, Sized for a Family of Five

The problem. Solar cookers cook at midday but the main family meal is cooked in the evening after the sun has gone, so households keep burning charcoal or fuelwood for the meal that matters most.

The result. A 1.6 m² panel cooker charging 22 kg of palm oil to 180 °C can deliver 1.93 MJ in the evening against a 1.62 MJ meal need for a family of five, serving food at about 92 °C, but it delivers nothing on a heavily overcast day and it cannot fry.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24109304

Counter-Rotating Vertical-Axis Wind Turbine Arrays: Designing Against the Spacing Tax from the Other Side

The problem. Wind power near where people live is taxed by spacing — large horizontal-axis machines must stand far apart, so generation sits far from homes, farms and workshops; packing small vertical-axis rotors tightly is the obvious counter-move, and this paper prices honestly what that move gains on a small patch of land, what it cannot gain at scale, and what it costs in money and upkeep.

The result. A specified array of small counter-rotating vertical-axis rotors (771.6 per hectare) produces a mean of 6.351–8.861 W per square metre of its own hectare across the full range of assumed neighbour-interaction factors — above the 0.5–1.0 W/m² horizontal-axis landscape baseline even when neighbours are assumed to hurt rather than help — but that advantage is borrowed from surrounding air and falls back to the same 0.5–1.0 W/m² bound at landscape scale, while costing 3.067 USD per rated watt and requiring 385.8 maintenance visits per hectare per year.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24109326

Wake-Steering Fleet Control for a Wind Farm: Designing Against the Spacing and Wake Tax

The problem. Wind farms lose a large share of their power to the wakes of their own upwind turbines and pay for the loss in extra land; this paper designs and prices the fleet-control countermeasure and states plainly where it stops paying.

The result. For a 20-turbine, 60 MW reference farm spaced 6 by 4 rotor diameters, an optimised wake-steering yaw schedule recovers +0.68% of fleet power in the dominant sector and +389 MWh a year (+0.25%, +64.3 W per hectare) in neutral air — rising to +4.37% in stable air, vanishing in turbulent air, and turning negative under fixed scheduling or direction spread, for a net of +$7,109 a year after added yaw maintenance.
