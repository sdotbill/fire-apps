Measuring land and keeping records

Knowing what you have: pacing a field, levelling a drain, a rain gauge made from a bottle, and ledgers that catch their own mistakes.

Walking a Contour with an A-Frame Level: How Far the Line Drifts After a Hundred Stations

The result. After 100 stations with a 2.0 m A-frame (200 m of hillside), the walked contour drifts by a design bound of 208.59 mm from the true contour — 111.11 mm of steady bias from a 0.5 mm crossbar mark offset plus a random walk of 48.74 mm standard deviation — displacing the line 2.086 m sideways on a cross-slope gradient of 0.10; the bias over a fixed width is independent of frame size, the random term is minimised near a 1.0 m span, and holding a 50 mm tolerance requires checks against a known level every 13 stations (26 m), or every 26 stations if the frame is reversed at every station, since no unchecked 200 m walk meets the tolerance at any span (best bound 195.67 mm).

Rounded Ages in a Written Register: A Digit-Preference Bound on the Error in Mean Age and Dependency Counts

The result. In a stated model register of 100,000 people (true mean age 32.7351 years), heaping a fraction of ages onto the nearest multiple of five drives the Whipple-style index from 99.98 to 259.99 at 40 percent heaping and 420.00 at 80 percent, yet mean age errs by only −0.0582 years even at 80 percent heaping, while the dependency counts err in a fixed direction (at 40 percent heaping: under-15 share −1.336 points, 65-and-over share +0.628 points, dependency ratio −2.101 points). The fitted registrar's bound is at most about 0.0133 dependency-ratio points and 0.00018 mean-age years per index point above 100, so an index of 200 permits a dependency error of about 1.304 points but a mean-age error below 0.018 years. Uniform redistribution within five-year groups removes the dependency error almost entirely (residual −0.118 points) but does not help mean age and worsens it at low heaping. Proven in simulation.

Setting the Fall of a Drain: The Shortest Board and Level That Can Hold a Stated Gradient

The result. Over a 100 m run with a stated 1.5 mm per-station reading error and 0.3 mm per-station bias, a 2 m board sets 1:200 (outfall bound 35.789 mm against a 50.0 mm limit), 1:500 needs a 6 m board because the outfall error — not reversed fall — binds (a 2 m board holds reversed length to 0.383 m yet fails the outfall at 35.789 mm against 20.0 mm), and 1:1000 cannot be set by any board up to 10 m with an unreversed level (best case 12.297 mm against a 10.0 mm limit), becoming feasible only when the level is reversed end-for-end at every station (10 m board, 9.297 mm).

Dating the Solstice with a Stick: The Shadow Bound, the Stick Height That Dates It, and Why the Equinox Is Easier

The result. With a ±2 mm shadow-tip reading error, a 1 m stick dates the solstice to only ±4.89–5.74 days and a 0.30 m hand stick to ±8.94–10.48 days across latitudes 0–55°, so a hand stick cannot date the solstice within a week; dating it within ±2 days requires a stick 5.99–8.24 m tall, while the same 0.30 m stick dates the equinox to within 0.31–0.95 days.

A Grain-Bank Ledger That Catches Its Own Mistakes: Check Digits, Cross-Casts, and the Errors That Still Get Through

The result. A modulo-11 check digit with weights 5, 4, 3, and 2 catches all 409095 enumerated single-digit substitutions and all 32728 adjacent transpositions across 9091 issuable accounts, and together with page cross-casting it reduces undetected errors under the stated model from 21.6 to 1.56 per 1000 entries (94.22 percent detected) at a cost of 17.5 seconds per entry, with the residual dominated by whole entries omitted from the book altogether.

The Water Level in a Hose: An Accuracy Bound by Length, Sunlight, and Reading Error

The result. A water level in a hose, with both ends shaded and the hose purged, carries a typical level-difference bound of 2.004 mm independent of hose length and can certify a true fall of 4.007 mm over a 50 m baseline at approximately 95 percent confidence, but with one end in full sun (a 2.07 mm thermal term from a 10 K difference over a 1.0 m column) and an unpurged hose the bound reaches 5.556 mm at 50 m and fails a ±5 mm specification, crossing it at 36.09 m; settling time rather than hydrostatic error sets the practical ceiling, giving a recommended maximum useful length of 75 m with a 13.5 minute wait after each move.

How Much Livestock Loss Can Hide in a Register: The Audit Interval That Bounds It

The result. Under stated assumptions (counting error the larger of 2 percent of the herd and 3 animals, register corrected to the count at each audit), the largest loss that can pass one audit is twice the counting error — 20 animals in a 500-head herd, but 6 animals, 30.0 percent of the herd, in a 20-head herd. Because the slack renews at each reconciliation, a steady loss of 4.0 percent of the herd per year passes annual audits of a 500-head herd indefinitely (16.0 percent under quarterly audits); holding a steady hidden loss to 2 percent per year requires counting accurate to 1 percent at annual audits, and a 20-head herd cannot meet the bound at any interval shorter than 15.0 years, so register-keeping alone cannot protect a very small herd. A Monte Carlo check found a steady loss at the 2 percent bound in a 500-head herd is detected at only 50.59 percent of annual audits. Proven in simulation.

A Planting Calendar Counted in Moons: The Drift Bound and the Intercalation Rule That Holds It

The result. An uncorrected 12-lunation calendar drifts 10.87512 days per year and is a full lunation wrong in 2.715 years; the proposed rule — insert a 13th month whenever the drift judged from one seasonal sign reaches half a lunation (14.765 days) — held the planting error over 200 years to a maximum of 17.24 days and a mean of 7.47 days with the sign judged to ±3 days, inside its analytic bound of 17.77 days, and its guarantee reaches the ±20-day window edge at a judging error of 5.235 days.

Two Ways to Measure an Irregular Field: An Error Bound for Baseline Offsets Against Fan Triangulation

The result. On the strongly wavy 100 m by 50 m field at 10 m spacing, offsets give a total root-sum-square bound of 0.1381% and fan triangulation gives 0.1428%, while at 20 m spacing both approach a 1% worst case through a -0.7959% discretisation error, so spacing set by waviness governs accuracy and offsets are preferred because triangulation needs a rope reaching 108.87 m where offsets need at most the 50 m depth; the narrow 20 m-deep field is the exception, where triangulation totals 0.1581% against offsets at 0.1953%.

Pacing a Field: An Error Bound for Area Measured by Counting Paces

The result. Under stated assumptions (pace 0.75 m, ordinary walker coefficient of variation 0.03, pace bias 0.01), a 50 m by 50 m field carries a 2.01% area bias and a 3.0284% total bound at approximately 95% confidence, so the belief that pacing gives area accurate to about one percent does not survive: a 0.5% pace bias alone gives 1.0025% area error, and a 1% total bound is impossible at bias 0.01 at any field size, while a calibrated walker (coefficient of variation 0.01, bias 0.002) achieves 0.7399% on the same field.

A Rain Gauge Made from a Funnel and a Bottle: Reading Resolution, Wetting Loss, and the Evaporation Deadline

The result. For a 200 mm funnel over a 30 mm cylinder (gain 44.444), one 2 mm graduation is 0.045 mm of rain, wetting loss is 0.0707 mm per event, and a 10 mm storm read at once is wrong by at most 0.1148 mm (1.15 percent); evaporation sets the deadline and the storage vessel sets the evaporation — the 0.5 mm bound is reached in 88.9 hours in hot arid wind if the catch waits in the narrow cylinder, but in only 16.3 hours in a 70 mm bottle, where a 10 mm storm left 24 hours is wrong by 1.0458 mm (10.46 percent).

Where to Put the Rain Gauge: A Siting Rule from Wind Undercatch, Height, and Obstacle Distance

The result. For a moderate site with 5 m/s wind at 10 m, a gauge rim at 0.3 m with at least 4 obstacle heights of clearance keeps undercatch at 4.72 percent, inside a 5 percent bound, while a roof mounting shows 13.71 percent and a fence-post mounting 9.27 percent; for a windy site with 8 m/s at 10 m, no siting in the grid meets the bound, the best being 5.88 percent at 0.3 m with no obstacle.

How Complete Is the Village Register? A Two-List Estimate with Its Uncertainty, and When the Second List Is Worth Collecting

The result. Using the Chapman two-list estimator on stated planning counts, a register holding 240 of a true 300 events, checked against an equally complete second list with an overlap of 192, yields an estimated total of 299.94 and a completeness of 80.02 percent with a 95 percent interval of 77.8 to 82.3 percent (half-width 2.25 points). The derived decision rule requires a second list large enough to produce an expected overlap of roughly 60 to 160 events (for example 135 events against a register of 240 at a total of 300) for a ±5-point estimate; below an overlap of about 20 the interval for the total is too wide to quote as a percentage (upper-to-lower ratio 3.948 at an overlap of 5), and an overlap inflated by only 10 percent through dependence between the lists overstates completeness by 7.92 points. Proven in simulation.

Squaring a Field with a Knotted Rope: Corner Error, Area Error, and What the Diagonal Check Can and Cannot Catch

The result. For a nominal 40 m by 30 m field laid out with a 3-4-5 knotted rope, the corner angle has a root-mean-square error of 0.404 degrees and side-length errors produce a typical area error of 0.5657 percent, while the traditional diagonal check — though it bounds corner error to 0.1194 degrees at a 0.10 m tolerance — is exactly blind to isosceles trapezoids, which pass with equal diagonals at area shortfalls of 2.02 percent (12 m total base difference) and up to 10 percent; adding a diagonal bisection check within 0.15 m, sides held to 0.3 percent, diagonals agreeing within 0.25 m, and knots placed within 18.08 mm guarantees a worst-case area error of 0.6055 percent.

How Much Too Large a Sloping Field Measures: The Slope Overstatement Bound and the Correction Rule

The result. Ground measurement overstates horizontal area by 1.5427% at 10 degrees, 3.5276% at 15 degrees, and 6.4178% at 20 degrees, crossing 2% at 11.3649 degrees and 5% at 17.7528 degrees; a field half flat and half at 20 degrees overstates by 3.2089%, more than double the 1.5427% a mean-slope correction would remove, while rope sag at a ratio of 0.02 adds only 0.2134%, so the correction rule is patch-wise division by the secant of each patch slope estimated by rope and plumb line over a 10 m run.

Reading a River to the Nearest Centimetre: A Stilling-Tube Staff Gauge and the Averaging Rule That Certifies It

The result. Direct reading certifies a level within plus or minus 1 cm at 95 percent confidence with 49 readings spaced at least 4 s apart against wind waves of 0.05 m amplitude and 8 s period, but needs 193 readings over 32.0 minutes against a 0.10 m, 20 s surge and is impractical there; a stilling tube of 200 mm diameter meets both a 1 cm residual and a 2 cm lag target at a 2 m per hour rise only with a hole between 11.31 mm and 12.06 mm (a nominal 12 mm hole: time constant 31.99 s, residual 0.99 cm, lag 1.777 cm), and at a 3 m per hour rise no hole in any tube size meets both targets, since damping requires a time constant of at least 31.67 s while the lag target allows at most 24.0 s.

The Split Tally as a Cooperative Record: What Two Halves of a Stick Prove, and the Discrepancy Rule That Makes Them Useful

The result. A zoned split tally on a 300 mm stick represents up to 599.5 kg at 0.5 kg resolution, exposes any one-sided notch addition with certainty when the halves are compared, and cuts expected zone-miscount settlements from 5.97 to 0.6 in 400 when counted fitted together; the proposed 1 kg discrepancy tolerance can hide at most 400 kg per year over 400 settlements, or 1.67 percent of a 24000 kg throughput, in the worst case.
