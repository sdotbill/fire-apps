Tools and the workshop

Hand tools, pedal power and small engines: what a workshop’s tools can deliver, and what their advertising claims cannot.

Boring Rate With a Brace and Bit in White Oak: Torque, Feed and the Resharpen-or-Stop Rule as Functions of Bit Condition

The problem. Wasted effort and wrong repairs in hand boring, by separating dullness (a scheduling decision) from screw-tip damage (a stop decision) with numbers.

The result. Sharp bit bores 62.79 holes/h at 4.69 N m and 3.68 kJ per hole, dulled 46.41 holes/h at 8.67 N m, and a damaged screw tip only 16.45 holes/h at 18.62 kJ per hole; resharpening pays only if at least 25 holes remain, and screw-tip damage is a stop-and-replace condition.

The Charcoal Budget of a Small Forge: Fuel per Hardening, the Temperature a Bed and Blast Can Hold, and the Combinations That Never Reach Hardening Heat

The problem. Small forges are built and run by folklore, so some never harden steel at all and others burn charcoal at rates their owners cannot explain or predict.

The result. Of 72 bed/blast combinations, 19 never reach hardening temperature at the workpiece; the dividing line is blast per unit bed area (at least 85 L/s per m2 works, a superficial velocity of 0.057 m/s or less never does). The reference forge (0.30 m bed, 10 L/s) hardens a 0.25 kg blade for 0.855 kg of charcoal from cold and 0.128 kg per repeat blade.

A Bushed Drilling Guide That Holds a Hand Drill Square: Angular Bounds, Hole-Position Error, and Bush Wear as the Limit

The problem. Hand-drilled holes that leave square and miss, split, or loosen the joint on the far face.

The result. A new 25 mm bushed guide bounds drilling angle to 0.344 degrees and exit error at 50 mm depth to 0.30 mm versus a 5.04 mm freehand 90th percentile (16.8-fold reduction), but a mild-steel bush reaches a 0.5-degree bound after only 22 holes and a hardened bush after 170 holes.

Edge Included Angle as a Design Choice: Cutting Force, Chipping Life, and a Test of the Claim That a Thinner Edge Is Always Better

The problem. Tool users thin edges for easier cutting and pay for it in chipped apices and repair time, with no rule connecting the angle to the work environment.

The result. Cutting force falls from 49.8 N at 60 degrees to 25.2 N at 15 degrees, but mean chip life falls from 344.8 hours to 0.9 hours; combined daily output peaks at 20 degrees (index 30.49) and the 15-degree edge (26.48) is worse than 20, 25, and 30 degrees, so a thinner edge is not always better. A 40-hour chip-life target requires 25 degrees in gentle work and 40 degrees in mixed work.

A Hand-Crank Blower Sized for a Charcoal Forge: Air, Pressure, Gearing and the Forge Size at Which the Hand Runs Out

The problem. Hand-crank forge blowers are sized by copying old machines, so they are built with too little pressure or impossible gearing for the forge they must serve.

The result. The stated 0.30 m bed needs 12 L/s against 310 Pa, of which the tuyere contributes 240 Pa and the fouled fuel bed only 29.5 Pa; a 0.45 m impeller at 920 rev/min through 13.1 to 1 gearing meets it at only 9.72 W of crank input (6.6 N hand force), but one operator cranking indefinitely runs out at a bed diameter of 0.53 m, and a 0.70 m bed needs 52.9 W.

A Pedal-Driven Grindstone for Tool Sharpening: Surface Speed, Flywheel and Gearing, Throughput, and the Limits of the Design

The problem. Workshops without electricity sharpen edge tools slowly by hand or let them go dull, because pedal grindstones are built by guesswork.

The result. An 8 m/s wet stone (0.30 m, 509 rpm, two-stage 8.49 step-up) needs an 18 kg rim storing 1704 J to hold speed; continuous grinding pressure is capped at 19.6 N by the operator's 55 W, giving about 48 touch-ups or 21 full regrinds per hour, with a 113 W deficit at 60 N pressure.

A Sizing Bound for Human-Powered Machines: The Sustained Mechanical Power Available from Pedals or a Treadle over a Working Day

The problem. Human-powered machines are sized and sold on sprint power figures, so they are built too big for the person who must power them all day.

The result. A worker delivers 72.2 W while pedalling and 54.1 W on average under a 50/10 duty pattern (0.433 kWh per 8-hour shift; treadle about 29.8 W); advertised continuous pedal-generator outputs of 200-750 W exceed the bound by factors of 2.77 to 10.39 and do not survive the arithmetic.

Hand Planing Priced in Force: Blade Sharpness, Cut Depth and a Chipbreaker Sizing Rule in Scots Pine

The problem. Exhausting, torn hand-planed work from blunt blades and loose chipbreaker settings, by giving force limits and a sizing rule for the setting.

The result. At 0.10 mm depth push force is 79.8 N sharp, 133.8 N dulled and 209.0 N blunt against a 120 N sustainable limit, with sustainable depths of 0.162, 0.088 and 0.053 mm; the chipbreaker must sit within 0.098 mm of the edge at that depth to keep modelled tear-out risk at or below 0.10.

A Hand Saw's Cutting Rate as a Function of Its Filing State: Rate Decay Over a Working Day and the Price of Filing Against the Rate It Buys Back

The problem. Hand-saw users decide when to stop and file by feel, either pushing a dull saw for hours or filing so often that the filing costs more time than the restored rate repays.

The result. Rate falls from 205.7 boards per hour freshly filed to 72.0 fully dull; the optimal full-filing interval is 112 boards (110.9 boards per hour sustained), a 6-minute touch-up every 72 boards does better at 123.1, and a filed saw completes 887 boards per day against 671 unfiled. Stop and file at sharpness 0.291 of fresh (about 111 boards), except near the end of a task, where filing at 120 boards pays only if at least 54 boards remain.

A Crosscut Saw Guide Priced in Squareness: Freehand Error, the Fence-and-Slot Bound, and the Gap It Leaves at the Board Edge

The problem. Crosscuts that are off square and leave wedge gaps that rocking, visible joints cannot close.

The result. A fence-and-slot guide bounds cut angle to 0.433 degrees worst case (0.318 degrees slot, 0.115 degrees fence) and the gap on a 200 mm board to 1.51 mm versus a 16.11 mm freehand 90th percentile, but it still misses a 0.5 mm fitted-joint target at 100, 200, and 300 mm widths.

Which Scrap Steels Can Be Re-Hardened Into Tools: A Carbon Bound, Five Common Sources, and the Real Error Rates of the File and Spark Tests

The problem. Workshop time wasted shaping scrap steel that can never harden into a tool.

The result. Retaining 50 HRC after a 200 C temper requires at least 0.499 percent carbon; files (61 HRC tempered) and uniform saw blades (57.1 HRC) clear it, leaf spring (53.7 HRC) clears it only at a light temper, while mild bar (37.5 HRC) and bimetal blade body (41.9 HRC) do not work at any quench, and the file test mis-sorts 15.0 percent and the spark test 22.5 percent of pieces under stated assumptions.

The Cost of Keeping a Working Edge: Stone Wear, Steel Loss, and the Price of One Sharp Hour for a Machete-Class Tool

The problem. Workers and smallholders spend money on stones and hours on repair without an account that shows abrasive choice barely matters while deep edge damage is uneconomic to repair by hand.

The result. A routine sharpening removes 46.87 mg of steel; stone life is 178.6 sharpenings (natural), 468.8 (silicon carbide), and 833.3 (diamond plate), yet one sharp working hour costs 0.52, 0.49, and 0.50 dollars respectively because labour dominates. Repair cost reaches the 22 dollar replacement price at a chip depth of 0.99 mm on silicon carbide and 0.80 mm on natural stone.

The True Cost of a Small Petrol Engine as a Workshop Prime Mover: Fuel per Shaft Kilowatt-Hour, Lifetime Maintenance and the Load Factor Below Which Sharing Wins

The problem. Small engines bought on price alone and run at part load, by showing the true cost per unit of delivered work and when sharing or human power is cheaper.

The result. Total cost is $0.749 per shaft kWh at full load, $1.967 at quarter load and $3.826 at load factor 0.10, with $0.55/h fixed costs and a $3,340.50 lifetime cost at half load; below load factor about 0.74 a shared engine run at 0.60 ($0.955/kWh) beats a private lightly loaded one.

Sizing the Flywheel of a Treadle Lathe: Stroke Energy, Cutting Torque, and a Speed-Sag Criterion with a Worked Wheel

The problem. Workshops without electricity size treadle-lathe flywheels by copying old wheels, with no way to adapt the design to new stock, speeds, or materials.

The result. A 39.6 J treadle stroke against a 30 N cut needing 33.9 J per unsupported interval requires 178.6 J stored — an 11.2 kg cast-iron rim of 0.45 m radius (or 22.3 kg oak disc) holding sag to 10.0 percent; the sustainable cutting-force ceiling is 28 N and an 80 N cut sags 29.8 percent.

Daylight at the Bench: The Opening Fraction Needed for Fine Work, and a Test of the Claim That One Translucent Roof Sheet Daylights a Workshop

The problem. Workshops are sold a single translucent roof sheet as full daylighting, leaving fine bench work chronically under-lit.

The result. One translucent roof sheet (1.8 m2) gives a bench daylight factor of 1.31% and only 131.1 lux under a standard overcast sky against a 500 lux fine-work threshold, with 0 hours at or above 300 lux in winter, equinox, and summer alike; holding 500 lux at the bench requires 6.87 m2 of roof opening (57.2% of the roof) under the standard sky and 13.73 m2 in winter, which exceeds the roof itself.

Airborne Dust in a Small Unpowered Workshop: Settling Times, Breathing-Zone Concentration, and the Ventilation Rate a Simple Opening Must Provide

The problem. Workers in small unpowered workshops breathe wood dust with no extraction, relying on settling and small vents that do not control the fine fraction.

The result. A 1 micrometre pine particle takes 19.82 hours to settle 1.5 m, so settling does not clear the fine fraction within a shift; a sealed-room day of 1 h sawing and 2 h hand sanding peaks at 109.62 mg/m3 with an 8-hour average of 52.12 mg/m3 against a 5 mg/m3 limit, and holding the steady sanding concentration at the limit requires 20.02 air changes per hour while 4.65 air changes per hour suffices for the daily average alone.

Noise Dose in a Small Corrugated-Metal Workshop: Reverberant Build-Up, Daily Dose from Hammering, and What Cheap Treatments Actually Buy

The problem. Hearing damage in small metal workshops, where money is spent on room lining that cannot protect the person holding the hammer.

The result. The steel room (7.83 sabins, RT60 1.11 s) raises hammering to 105.18 dBA at the operator's ear, so the NIOSH daily dose is exhausted after 3.6 minutes of hammering and 60 minutes gives 1341.2%; the best-value room treatment buys only 0.81 dB for the operator, $540 of mineral wool buys 2.71 dB, and even 20 dB hearing protection leaves a full day of hammering at 104.2%.
