The Longest-Lived Bar: Soap Bar Dimensions That Maximise Washes per Gram at Fixed Mass

The result. At 100 g the best grippable block found, 5.6 x 5.0 x 3.4 cm, lasted 916 washes, while the thinnest admissible slab lasted 417 washes and a typical oval approximated as a 22 mm thick tablet lasted 584 washes. The best block therefore delivers 56.8% more washes than the typical tablet from the same mass of soap, at a modelled cost of 0.1 US cents per wash against 0.15 cents. Storing the typical tablet on its smallest face raised its life to 584 washes, recovering part but not all of the geometric penalty. Surface area at first use ranged from 128.1 to 161.9 square centimetres across the grid, and washes tracked initial surface area closely throughout.

Buckets to a Safe Bath: A Mixing Ratio for Bath Water Without a Thermometer

The result. At a cold supply of 15 C, the exact ratio is 4.9 buckets of hot per 10 buckets of mixed water, and whole-bucket filling gives a bath at 36.5 C, inside the band. At 8 C the hot share rises to 5.6 buckets in 10 and at 22 C it falls to 3.9 in 10. Hot-supply drift of plus or minus 3 C moves the finished bath by plus or minus 1.3 C at the middle ratio. Whole-bucket counting stays within the 36 to 38 C band for cold supplies between 10 and 20 C; outside that range a half-bucket final adjustment is required. Filling hot first exposes a peak intermediate temperature of 60 C, against 33 C for the cold-first alternating sequence at the same final temperature.

Keeping Shared Water Honest: The Exchange Rate a Communal Bath Needs to Hold a Stated Load Limit

The result. At six bathers per hour the exchange flow needed to hold the stated limit was 24 litres per hour, or 0.6% of the bath volume per hour. Stepped simulation reached a peak of 30000 units per litre, at the limit as designed. Session operation with a full change every 3 hours spent 74 minutes above the limit per session and used 8000 litres per operating day against 288 litres for continuous exchange. Heating the continuous exchange cost USD 1.0 per operating day. Continuous exchange became cheaper than session changes above 4 bathers per hour.

Warm Water Is Not the Active Ingredient: Energy Saved by Cold-Water Handwashing at Equal Modelled Removal

The result. Modelled removal at twenty seconds was 79.6% at 15 C and 87.5% at 40 C, a difference of 7.9 percentage points across the whole practical range, and 83.7% at 25 C. Heating the wash water to 35 C cost 71.9 kWh and USD 10.79 per household per year at the stated use, for that difference. Warm water would need to extend the average wash by 6 seconds, through comfort alone, to match a full-duration cool wash, and a cool wash extended by just 3 seconds at no energy cost surpassed the warm short wash in the model.

Hard Water Eats Detergent: A Dosing Rule for Washing Cloth Nappies by Water Hardness Band

The result. The hardness tax alone was 14.8 g in soft water and 74.2 g in hard water, a difference of 59.4 g before any cleaning began. Soap That the Garden Can Forgive: A Sodium Budget for Reusing Bath Water on Plants

The result. The baseline household applied 98.6 g of sodium per square metre per year before leaching, or 69.0 g net, against the stated ceiling of 12 g, an exceedance of 5.8 times. Halving bathing soap events reduced the net load to 34.5 g, still above the ceiling. Switching to potassium-based soap reduced it to 7.7 g net, comfortably inside the budget, and spreading the baseline sodium load thinly enough to comply would have required 115 square metres of garden. On sand, with the ceiling raised to 30 g, the baseline still exceeded the ceiling.

A School Day Without an Empty Tap: Sizing a Gravity Handwashing Station and Its Refill Schedule

The result. Daily demand was 259.0 litres, and with the reserve the required capacity was 297.8 litres, so the 25 litre container was selected and the 20 litre container rejected as 277.8 litres short. A Superfat Margin That Cannot Go Negative: Weighing Tolerances and Saponification Uncertainty in Home Soap Making

The result. The computed lye requirement for the recipe is 150.8 grams before any discount. Saponification uncertainty alone can raise the true requirement by 3.0 percent, and adverse weighing errors add a further 1.27 percent, giving a worst-case guarantee threshold of 4.27 percent superfat. The traditional 5 percent discount therefore meets the guarantee, with little to spare for this recipe and equipment. At half batch size the threshold rises to 6.17 percent, and with a scale accurate only to 5 grams it rises to 6.87 percent. A superfat of 8 percent satisfies the worst case with a small additional allowance for spillage and residue on the weighing vessel.

The result. Computed waking intervals were 2.0 hours on day 1, 1.5 hours on day 2, 3.5 hours on day 3, 7.5 hours on day 4 and 8 hours on day 5, giving 19 pads across the waking hours of the five days against 20 for a flat four-hourly habit, which nevertheless modelled a leak on day 2 when peak flow filled the pad in 1.3 hours. One Full Push Is Too Much: The Smallest Pump Dose That Still Covers Both Hands

The result. The ideal film volume before losses is 1.36 mL, and 1.36 mL must actually reach the skin after the modelled losses. A dose of 2.0 mL per push was the smallest tabulated dose reaching at least 95.4% coverage, while the common 3.0 mL push reached 99.0% coverage and used 50.0% more soap than the recommended dose for a gain of only a few percentage points of coverage. Over a year at twelve washes a day the recommended dose consumes 8.76 litres against 13.14 litres, saving USD 17.52 per wash point per year at the stated price. Halving a large push by instruction alone, with realistic variation between users, delivered coverage as low as 75.1% for the least careful quartile in the model.

Foam Without Waste: A Dilution Ratio for Shampoo in a Foaming Dispenser That Preserves Cleaning Power

The result. Neat use applied surfactant at 15% at the point of contact but averaged 4.1% over the head after spreading losses. Dilution to the threshold occurred at a ratio of 1:11.5, and the recommended working ratio of 1:4 delivered 3.0% at the hair, a margin of 150% above threshold. Annual product consumption fell from 1248 mL neat to 374 mL diluted, saving USD 10.48 per person per year at the stated price, while one diluted wash at the working ratio outperformed, on spread coverage, the first of the customary two neat washes, which used 16 mL in total.

Is the Shared Bar a Hazard? A Transfer Bound for Bar Soap Under Rinsing and Drying Conditions

The result. Under the full chain, a bar used again after 3 hours of draining delivered at most 5 organisms to the next user's hands before their wash, and at most 0.1 remained after the wash itself, against a typical pre-existing hand load in the model of 1000000. Slower Is Not Always Drier: The Shower Flow Rate That Minimises Total Water for a Complete Rinse

The result. Under the stated kinetics the volume-minimising flow was 4 litres per minute, using 1.8 litres to complete the rinse in 27 seconds. At 4 litres per minute the rinse took 27 seconds and used 1.8 litres, which is 0.0% more water than the optimum despite the lower flow. At 14 litres per minute the rinse used 2.6 litres. Under the proportional control assumption, rinse volume was 1.2 litres at every flow, confirming that the entire benefit or penalty of restriction turns on whether removal keeps pace with flow.

The result. The optimised sequence used 2.6 litres in total, including cloth rinses, and met every zone standard. The result. A 100 g tube lasted 22.7 days at the ribbon dose and 100 days at the adult pea dose, a 4.4 fold difference. When Does a Towel Stop Drying and Start Seeding? A Reuse Interval from Drying and Growth Curves

The result. In the ventilated case the towel fell below the growth threshold in 5 hours and could serve 6 daily uses before crossing the ceiling, corresponding to a 6 day interval. In the still humid bathroom it needed 13 hours to leave the growth zone and crossed the ceiling after 3 uses, a 3 day interval at daily use. Twice-daily use shortened the humid interval to 2 days while barely changing the ventilated one. Laundering on the conditional rule cost 74 loads per towel per year against 52 for a flat weekly wash, with the humid-room towel, not the ventilated one, driving the extra washes.

How Long Is Long Enough? A Contact-Time Rule for Soap Handwashing Derived from a Removal Model

The result. With soap, 90 percent removal required 27 seconds, 99 percent required 136 seconds and 99.9 percent required 251 seconds. With water alone the same targets required 54, 271 and 502 seconds respectively. The modelled load remaining after 20 seconds of soap washing was 13.52 percent of the starting load, and after 40 seconds 6.88 percent, so the second twenty seconds removed less than a fifth as much as the first. A 15 second soap wash achieved 81.2 percent removal in the model, which supports a 136 second rule for a 99 percent target under the stated rates.
