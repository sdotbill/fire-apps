Hours to Danger: A Lumped Thermal Model of Passive Survivability in a Mid-Rise Apartment Block During a Blackout Heatwave, and a Priced Retrofit Bound

The problem. Residents, especially on upper floors, overheating indoors during a power blackout that coincides with a heatwave, when cooling, fans, and lifts have all failed at once.

The result. In a simulated 38 °C heatwave blackout, the worst flat (top floor, east-facing) crosses the 32 °C indoor danger threshold in 32.9 hours while the best lasts 62.25 hours, and a $1,540-per-flat package of external shading, roof treatment, thermal mass, and secure night ventilation keeps every modelled flat below the threshold for at least 120 hours — at least 87.1 additional safe hours for the worst flat at no more than $17.68 per additional safe hour.

Degrees per Metre: A Canyon Energy-Balance Model of Street-Level Heat and the Price of Cooling Interventions, with a Geometry-Locked Regime

The result. Pedestrian-level excess rises from 2.73 K at a height-to-width ratio of 0.5 to 8.30 K at 4.0, shade trees are the cheapest cooling wherever they can be planted (best value 7.956 degrees per $1,000 per metre in the deep canyon), and in canyons with ratios of 3.0 and 4.0 no surface intervention buys more than 0.25 K, while setback to a ratio of 1.0 at redevelopment buys 3.17 K and 4.07 K. Human problem: Pedestrians in deep, narrow streets between tall buildings endure far hotter air than the city-wide temperature suggests, and cities spend on surface cooling measures without knowing which streets those measures cannot help.

Keeping Medicines Cold Without a Refrigerator: A Passive Phase-Change Cold Box for a Household Medicine Supply During a Blackout

The problem. Keeping insulin and other essential medicines that are labelled for 2–8 °C storage within their stated band in an apartment during a blackout, when the refrigerator no longer works and the room itself is hot.

The result. In simulation, a $121 box of 50 mm PIR board holding 5.0 kg of phase-change panels melting at 5 °C keeps a household medicine supply inside 2–8 °C for 62.6 hours at 30 °C ambient, but fails a 48-hour outage at 40 °C and a 72-hour outage at any ambient at or above 30 °C.

Heat Build-Up in a Stalled Elevator Cabin During a Power Outage: A Cabin Thermal Model and a Passive Design Priced per Cabin

The problem. People trapped in a stalled elevator during a power outage heat the small sealed cabin with their own bodies — a full cabin crosses a 35 °C danger threshold in 17.4 minutes against a warm shaft and 8.5 minutes against a solar-heated shaft — often long before rescue arrives.

The result. A $1,586-per-cabin passive design (0.20 m² vent pair to the shaft, 24 kg phase-change lining, insulated reflective lining) extends the safe wait past the 120-minute target for a cool shaft at any occupancy and for 4 occupants in a warm shaft (danger delayed from 34.9 to 207.4 minutes), but fails for a full cabin in a warm shaft (105.9 minutes) and at every occupancy in a hot 40 °C shaft.

Fire-Break Width and Fire-Service Access Lanes for Dense Informal Settlements: A Spread-Probability Bound

The problem. Fires in dense informal settlements, where dwellings stand about a metre apart with no separation and no road a fire appliance can use, so one ignition can destroy hundreds of homes in a single event.

The result. Including ember transport, a fire break must be 18.75 m wide at a 5 m/s wind to bring dwelling-to-dwelling spread probability below 0.05 for mixed construction — and such breaks every eight rows save 211.1 of 900 simulated dwellings per fire at $4,077 per dwelling saved, while breaks of 10 m or less save essentially none.

Witnesses for Urban Flash-Flood Pockets: A Fill-Time Bound and a Low-Cost Water-Level Warning Network

The problem. People drowning or being trapped when underpasses, subways, basements and other low urban pockets fill in minutes before any city-wide flood warning reaches them.

The result. At 150 mm/h the vehicle underpass fills to dangerous depth in 5.04 minutes with a warning margin of −37.9 seconds and is a no-win pocket for level-only warning, while the other five pockets pass a 60-second margin requirement with a witness network averaging $2631.7 capital per pocket.

No Safe Indoor Placement: A Carbon Monoxide Accumulation Bound for Portable Generators in Apartment Buildings

The problem. People in flats are poisoned by carbon monoxide from portable generators run during blackouts in rooms, on balconies, in stairwells and in courtyards, and the exhaust also drifts into neighbours' homes.

The result. For a large portable generator, a room reaches a dangerous 400 ppm of carbon monoxide in 1.1 minutes, a balcony in 0.22 minutes, a stairwell in 2.85 minutes and a neighbouring flat via a balcony in 39.11 minutes — no indoor, balcony, stairwell or courtyard placement can be made safe, and the minimum safe placement is fully outdoors at least 6.1 m from any open window, door or air intake of any dwelling.

Safe LPG Storage Geometry for Dense Housing: Hazard Radii, Minimum Separation, and a Communal Vented Store Design

The result. The governing safe separation is 12.52 m (13 m design) for a 15 kg cylinder, governed by the fireball, so balconies, alleys and dense courtyards have no practical individual separation and need a communal vented store at the full distance, priced at 242.70 USD per household protected. Human problem: Households in dense housing store cooking-gas cylinders only a few metres from neighbouring dwellings, placing neighbours inside the jet-fire, flash-fire and fireball hazard radii if a cylinder fails.

Seeing the Pedestrian in Time: A Detection-Distance Model and a Lighting Specification for Night Crossings

The problem. Night-time pedestrian deaths at crossings, where drivers first see pedestrians inside the distance their vehicles need to stop.

The result. Oncoming LED glare cuts detection of a dark-clothed pedestrian at a 10 lux crossing to 48 m — inside the 55.9 m wet stopping distance at 50 km/h — while the specified 30 lux upstream, cut-off, neutral-white design restores it to 70 m, though near-black clothing and fog remain beyond what any lighting level in the model can fix.

Pedestrian Death Physics: A Survivable Speed Bound for Crossings from Stopping Distance, Fatality Risk, and Available Sight Distance

The problem. Pedestrians are killed at crossings where traffic speed exceeds what the available sight distance allows a driver to stop for — a mismatch set by street geometry and speed choice, computed here as an explicit design bound with the occluded and high-speed arterial regimes, where no limit makes the crossing survivable, printed at full size.

The result. On a wet surface, a crossing with 20 m of sight distance is survivable only up to 28.6 km/h (17.1 km/h at 10 m, 53.2 km/h at 50 m), and about 9.1 m of obstruction clearing is what buys a survivable 30 km/h.

Sizing a Neighbourhood Cold and Warm Refuge: A Design Rule per 1,000 Residents for Floor Area, Thermal Load and 72-Hour Backup Energy

The problem. Neighbourhood refuges for heatwaves and winter storms are opened in public buildings that were never sized for overnight occupancy, so they crowd, turn away the vulnerable, or run out of fuel partway through a multi-day power outage.

The result. Per 1,000 residents at a vulnerable fraction of 0.25, a 72-hour refuge needs 1,010.0 m² at 4.68 m² gross per person present, 51.87 kW of cooling for a 40 °C heatwave blackout in a good building, and 1,720 kWh or 491 litres of generator diesel of backup energy, at $1,748.91 capital cost per protected resident — and a single 600 m² school gymnasium meets only 59.4% of the area rule.

Gravity Water Autonomy in an Apartment Block During a Blackout: A Header-Tank Sizing Rule Bounded by Head Pressure and Roof Load, with a Ground-Level Fallback

The problem. Apartment residents losing all water — drinking, sanitation, and fire protection — during a blackout because the pumps stop, with the upper floors losing pressure first.

The result. A 9,600-litre framed header tank gives a 10-floor, 100-resident block 72.0 hours at the hygiene tier (152.0 hours on a staged rationing schedule), but the top floor keeps a shower for only 26.18 hours and never gets an effective fire jet, and a weak slab-only older block cannot carry even its 72-hour survival volume plus fire reserve (3,058 litres maximum against 4,782 litres required), so it is designed instead around a 7,752-litre ground bladder.

When the Lights Go Dark: Throughput Collapse at a Signalised Junction in a Power Blackout, and a Solar-Plus-Battery Backup Sized for 72 Hours

The problem. Signalised junctions going dark in a power blackout, collapsing into all-way-stop queues that grow without limit, spill back and gridlock surrounding streets, stranding ordinary and emergency traffic alike.

The result. All-way-stop control cuts the canonical junction from 820 to 466.4 vehicles per hour per approach (a 43.1% loss, gridlock regardless of driver behaviour above 650.7), and a 2.75 kWp solar plus 15 kWh battery design holds signals and communications for 72.6 hours at $14,087.50 per junction.

The result. The Road-Underpass Flood Trap: Fill Rate, Escape Window, and a Passive Countermeasure Package

The problem. Drivers misjudge calm, shallow-looking water in a sunken underpass and drive in minutes before the depth blocks their doors and then floats the vehicle, trapping them inside.

The result. For a typical underpass in a 100 mm/h storm, occupants have only 192.0 seconds between first ponding and doors that will no longer open, while a passive barrier, refuge, ladder and drainage package priced at $259,785 prevents filling at or below that design storm.
