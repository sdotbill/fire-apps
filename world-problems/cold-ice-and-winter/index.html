Cold, ice and winter

Cold as an engineering problem: insulation, ice, snow, and the human body’s heat budget through a winter night.

The Cold Battery Bound: What Low Temperature Takes from a Lead–Acid Store, and Oversizing versus Insulating Priced in Usable Winter Capacity

The problem. Off-grid lead–acid stores sized on mild-weather nameplate capacity fail in winter, when light and heat are needed most.

The result. At −20 °C usable energy falls from 4.702 kWh to 2.728 kWh (capacity fraction 0.595, resistance ×2.25); full oversizing costs $633 per usable winter kWh, an insulated enclosure holding 10 °C costs $514 but leaves a 0.660 kWh shortfall, and enclosure plus a 1.163 trim oversize restores full capacity at $502 per usable winter kWh, with the capital crossover at about 7 °C ambient.

Freezing Food Without a Freezer: The Salt–Ice Mixture Bound — Lowest Temperature, and the Ice and Salt Consumed per Kilogram Frozen

The problem. People with ice but no electricity need to know how cold a salt–ice mixture can get food, and how much ice and salt each kilogram frozen will cost, before relying on it to preserve food.

The result. Sodium chloride mixtures reach at most −21.1 °C and calcium chloride about −51 °C (eutectic bounds); freezing 1 kg of food from 30 °C to −18 °C consumes 1.52 kg of ice plus 0.40 kg of NaCl or 0.36 kg of CaCl2, with NaCl holding only 3.1 K of margin at −18 °C, while CaCl2 at −40 °C consumes 3.67 kg of mixture per kg of food and crosses the stated practicality limit.

How Deep the Frost Goes: A Frost-Penetration Bound for Footings and Posts, and the Climates Where a Shallow Foundation Stops Working

The problem. Builders of small structures need to know how deep to found footings and posts, and where a shallow foundation will be lifted by frost, before the damage is built in.

The result. The Stefan frost bound in moist silt runs from 0.77 m at a 200 degree-day freezing index to 3.00 m at 3000 degree-days (founding depths 0.92 m to 3.15 m with a 0.15 m margin); in-place pore-water expansion alone exceeds a 25 mm heave tolerance beyond 329 degree-days in moist silt, and protecting a 0.60 m footing in that soil needs specified insulation from 10 mm in the mildest climate to 100 mm in the subarctic class.

Holding Food Frozen Through a Thaw: Insulation Sizing for an Outdoor Winter Cache Whose Only Refrigeration Is the Season

The problem. People in cold regions who store winter-frozen food outdoors need to know how much insulation, around how much food, keeps it frozen through a warm spell with no power.

The result. To ride a 7-day spell at +8.0 °C from a −30.0 °C start under a hard limit of 0 °C with at most 10% melted, required polystyrene thickness falls from 0.232 m at 10 kg to 0.046 m at 100 kg and 0.027 m at 400 kg (mass exponent −0.461). The −12 °C high-quality limit is crossed after only about 1.4 days at design thickness, and a 25 mm wooden box at 100 kg reaches the hard limit in 0.94 days.

Heating a Small House on Gathered Wood: The Heat-Loss Budget, the Kilograms per Day, and the Hours of Gathering a Winter Actually Costs

The problem. Households heating with self-gathered wood cannot plan fuel or labour because heating costs are never stated in kilograms per day and hours per week.

The result. The stated 40 m2 house (loss coefficient 127.0 W/K) needs 38.69 kg of wood on the -15 C design day and 21.10 kg on an average day, totalling 3.165 tonnes and 126.61 gathering hours over a 150-day winter; wall insulation (U 0.60 to 0.25) buys back the most labour at 19.89 hours per winter.

How Far Can Ice Span: A Creep-Limited Bound for an Ice Beam Footbridge, and What Sawn Plant Fibre Does to the Bound

The problem. Ice footbridges are commonly judged by short-term strength on a cold day, although sustained-load creep near the melting point is what actually limits how far they can span and how long they can stay in service.

The result. For a 1.2 m × 0.35 m ice beam under sustained pedestrian load over 60 days, the governing span is 4.66 m at −10 °C and falls to 3.13 m at −1 °C (a 32.8 percent collapse) as creep strain replaces strength as the binding limit; the stated fibre composite raises the spans to 6.78 m and 4.01 m but collapses faster in percentage terms.

The Lake Ice Harvest: Ice Growth by Date, Tonnes per Hectare, and the Labour in a Tonne of Hand-Cut Ice

The problem. Communities without reliable refrigeration need to know when lake ice can be cut, how much a hectare yields, and how much labour each stored tonne costs.

The result. A bare sheet reaches the 0.30 m harvest thickness on 2 December and a snow-covered sheet not until 16 January in a 1615 freezing degree-day winter; one hectare at 0.30 m delivers 2475.9 net tonnes, and each delivered tonne contains 5.92 person-hours of hand work, of which 3.30 hours is sawing.

Storing Winter Ice for Summer: A Melt-Loss Bound and an Insulation Sizing Rule for a Small Ice House

The problem. Communities without refrigeration that harvest winter ice need to know how big a store and how thick insulation are required for ice to last the summer, and how much extra ice to cut to cover melt losses.

The result. Over a 120-day season at a mean 25 °C, a 1 m ice store loses everything at 0.15–0.30 m sawdust insulation and keeps only 14.3% even at 0.60 m, while a 3 m store at 0.30 m keeps 52.6% and a 5 m store at 0.60 m keeps 85.8%; the harvest rule runs from 6.99 tonnes cut per tonne surviving in the smallest viable store to 1.17 in the largest, with door openings alone melting 127.8 kg per season at any size.

How Thick Must the Ice Be: A Bearing Bound for Crossing a Frozen Lake on Foot, by Sledge, and by Loaded Vehicle

The problem. People judge frozen lakes by eye or habit and misjudge, by a wide margin, what load a given thickness can bear.

The result. Minimum clear-ice thicknesses for moving loads are 10.0 cm on foot (formula 8.5 cm, floored), 16.2 cm for a 400 kg sledge, 25.6 cm for a 1,000 kg cart, 37.1 cm for a 3,500 kg light truck, and 57.2 cm for a 10,000 kg loaded truck; parking and white ice each multiply thickness by 1.414, so a parked loaded truck needs 80.9 cm of clear ice.

The Snow Shelter Balance: Inside Temperature, Wall Thickness, and the Ventilation a Sleeper Can Afford

The problem. People sheltering in snow need to know how warm the shelter will actually be and how much ventilation they must pay for in heat to breathe safely through the night.

The result. Two sleepers in a 1.1 m radius shelter with a 0.45 m wall hold the inside 12.8 K above the outside (−7.2 °C at −20 °C outside, −17.2 °C at −30 °C); reaching 0 °C takes 4.02 sleepers at −20 °C but 10.14 at −30 °C, and sealing the shelter reaches the stated carbon dioxide limit in 18.0 minutes, while ventilation becomes the largest single loss (up to 60.6 percent) in thick low-density walls.

Keeping Medicine from Freezing Without Power: A Water-Ballast Carry Box Sized to Hold 2–8 °C Through a Winter Night

The problem. Freeze-sensitive medicine such as insulin and many vaccines loses potency when it freezes during unpowered winter transport or storage.

The result. At −20 °C with a partitioned water jacket filled at 24 °C, protection lasts 11.49 h with 6 kg of ballast and 14.34 h with 8 kg; 6.4 kg is the computed requirement for a 12-hour night, and the safe fill window for the 8 kg box is 22–26 °C. At −30 °C the design holds the band for at most 8.74 h even at 10 kg.

How Long Before the Pipe Freezes: A Time-to-Freeze Model, and the Insulation Thickness That Survives the Design Night

The problem. Homeowners in cold snaps need to know how long a static pipe has before it freezes, how much insulation actually spans the night, and what a protective trickle flow costs in water.

The result. At −18.0 °C with water starting at 10.0 °C, bare pipes of 13, 25, and 50 mm bore freeze across in 1.55, 3.22, and 6.64 hours; foam needed to survive a 12-hour night is 4.0 mm (50 mm bore) and 22.3 mm (25 mm bore) but an impractical 367.2 mm at 13 mm bore. The working alternative for the small pipe is a trickle of 0.166 litres per minute costing 119.3 litres per night over a 10 m bare run; a 0.05 L/min drip delivers only 34.9 W against a 115.6 W loss and does not protect it.

Making Ice Under a Clear Night Sky: A Radiative-Cooling Yield Bound and a Shallow-Pond Design for Arid Climates

The problem. Clinics and households in arid places without reliable electricity need ice for medicine cold storage and food safety, and this paper states where a fuel-free sky-cooled pond can supply it and where it cannot.

The result. Night-sky freezing yields at most 7.53 kg of ice per m² per night in very dry, calm air and 2.65 kg/m² in dry air with light wind; the yield is zero under moderately humid or humid skies at any wind modelled and zero in dry air under a fresh breeze. A clinic needing 30 kg/day needs an 11.3 m² pond (14.2 m² with a 25% margin) in the dry, light-wind case.

How Deep Is Cool: The Soil-Temperature Bound for Earth-Sheltered Food Storage, and the Depth That Holds a Store Between 0 and 4 °C

The problem. Households digging root cellars for refrigeration without machinery need to know whether their climate and soil can hold food at 0–4 °C at all, and how deep to dig when it can.

The result. Damping depths are 1.417 m (dry sandy), 2.241 m (moist loam), and 3.169 m (wet clay); in a cool continental climate (mean 2.0 °C, amplitude 14.0 °C) a store holds 0–4 °C all year from 2.76 m, 4.36 m, and 6.17 m respectively. Where the mean annual temperature is 26.0 °C, deep soil rests at 26.0 °C — 22.0 °C above the band at every depth — disproving the claim that digging deeper always finds colder ground.

Sleeping Warm Without Fuel: The Insulation Bound, Why the Ground Pad Decides It, and a Layered System Sized from Stated Materials

The problem. People sleeping in the cold without fuel cannot tell how to divide insulation between above and below the body, or at what temperature a given pile of blankets stops being enough.

The result. In the sized system (four blankets over; two blankets plus a 12 mm foam pad under), 68.1 percent of conducted loss goes into the ground and thermal balance holds only down to 0.44 C; removing the pad collapses the balance temperature to 21.17 C, and balance at -10 C requires R = 1.257 m2K/W on both sides, which compressed underside blankets cannot supply.

Snow on a Cheap Roof: A Load Bound by Climate and Density, and a Rafter Sizing Rule That Follows From It

The problem. Cheap roofs are routinely copied from milder places or sized by eye, and they collapse under snow loads that a simple density-and-climate bound would have predicted.

The result. Stated snow loads run from 0.88 kN/m² (mild lowland) to 9.81 kN/m² (high mountain), and 17.66 kN/m² with the stated drift multiplier; at a 4.0 m span and 0.40 m spacing the carrying rafter steps from 45×145 mm to 75×295 mm across the classes, the drifted worst case passes only at a shortened 3.0 m span, and common constructions such as 45×145 mm at 0.60 m spacing fail even the mildest class.

Water from Snow: The Fuel in a Litre of Meltwater, and Why Eating Snow Does Not Work

The problem. The dangerous advice that a person without fuel can meet their water needs by eating snow persists because nobody prices the melting energy the body must pay.

The result. One litre of water from snow at -10 C costs 417.7 kJ, which is 72.5 g of wood on a 40 percent-efficient stove (0.181 kg for a day's 2.5 litres) and 5.0 litres of settled snow handled; eaten directly, the same litre costs the body 509.8 kJ (121.8 kcal), and a day's water eaten as snow represents an uncompensated core-temperature fall of 4.90 C, to 32.1 C.
