Bees, honey and wax

Hives kept through winter and summer heat, honey that keeps, wax rendered, and the arithmetic of colonies and forage.

A Winter Stores Bound for the Honey Bee Hive: Insulation, Heat Loss, and the Honey a Colony Must Spend

The result. Under a held-cavity upper-bound model over a 150-day cold continental winter (mean −6.83 °C), the stores bound falls from 73.16 kg for bare 19 mm pine to 22.17 kg with 50 mm EPS insulation, a 69.7% reduction, while roof-only insulation barely helps (64.25 kg). The sizing rule originated: whole-hive conductance, floor included, at or below 1.013 W/K holds the cold-winter bound within 20 kg of stores.

Condensation Arithmetic in the Winter Hive: Water Production, Wall Temperature, and Where the Moisture Falls

The result. Honey metabolism yields 0.48 kg of water per kilogram of honey consumed (48.0 g/day at 100 g/day). At −10 °C outside, a bare pine inner wall sits at 1.78 °C, 4.93 °C below the 6.71 °C dew point of cavity air, condensing an estimated 618.1 g/m²/day, while a 50 mm EPS wall holds 8.52 °C and condenses nothing. Insulate first so wall surfaces clear the dew point, then vent modestly: wetting, not temperature alone, is the hazard the arithmetic identifies.

Summer Heat Load in the Honey Bee Hive: Fanning Bees, Water Demand, and a Roof and Shade Sizing Rule

The result. At 43 °C a dark uninsulated hive needs 273.7 g of water per hour and 336 fanning bees (1,642.0 g over 6 hours) against 85.3 g/hour and 105 bees in full shade. Fanning never exceeds 3.4% of a 10,000-bee forager force — water, not bee numbers, is the binding cost — and an 80 g/hour water target at 43 °C is infeasible for any roof colour. The rule originated: light finish first, roof insulation second, shade third.

The Cost of Wrong Frame Spacing: A Season Budget Model and a Millimetre Tolerance for Ten-Frame Boxes

The result. Costs are zero while gaps stay inside the 6.0–9.0 mm bee-space range, then grow linearly; against season budgets of 0.50 kg honey equivalent and 30 extra minutes, the spacing tolerance is −1.7 mm to +1.6 mm (usable shop rule ±1.5 mm). At +2.0 mm error the model charges 329.3 g of burr wax, equal to 2.305 kg of honey, and 105.6 extra minutes per season, so the belief that a few millimetres make no difference does not survive beyond about 1.5 mm.

Population Arithmetic and the Supering Rule: When Storage Binds and What a Week of Delay Costs

The result. The cohort model peaks at 71,400 adults on day 122. At the stated nectar flow the brood box's 26,500 free cells bind in 4.77 days, giving the rule: super at 10 days of storage cover (55,556 free cells). Supering one week late costs 16.84 kg of honey — 5.57 kg never stored plus 11.26 kg forgone because backfilled brood cells remove 22,525 adults — and two weeks late costs 34.34 kg.

What Drawn Comb Costs: The Honey Price of a Box of Comb and a Bound on Annual Comb Renewal

The result. At the stated 7.0 kg of honey per kilogram of wax, a box of comb costs 8.4 kg of honey foundationless against 2.8 kg on foundation and nothing for drawn comb — first-season penalties of 21.0% versus 7.0% of a 40 kg crop. The renewal bound at a 10%-of-crop budget is 4.76 frames per year foundationless against 14.29 on foundation; foundationless is the cheaper route only if honey is valued below 3.32 per kilogram or foundation sheets cost over 5.32 each.

The Economic Forage Radius of a Honey Bee Colony: A Flight-Energy Bound on Net Gain per Trip

The result. At 12.0 J/km flight cost and a 40 mg nectar load, the break-even radius is 4.94 km at 20% sugar, 7.72 km at 30%, 10.51 km at 40% and 13.29 km at 50%; the half-maximum working radius is 3.86 km at 30% sugar. At three miles a 30% trip returns only 37.5% of its maximum, and the annulus beyond three miles is 60.9% of the land but contributes only 31.6% of area-weighted net gain — the claim that bees use everything within three miles does not survive the arithmetic.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24115266

How Many Colonies a Landscape Carries: A Stocking-Rate Bound from Seasonal Sugar Production and Colony Demand

The result. With 20% of produced sugar harvestable and 60 kg of seasonal demand per colony, per-colony surplus halves at 35.3 colonies/km² on oilseed rape, 18.8 on clover and 7.1 on wildflower meadow; energetic break-even is 50.0, 26.7 and 10.0 colonies/km² respectively. These are energetic ceilings for pure stands, stated as bounds rather than recommended stocking rates.

A Scored Hive Placement Rule from Morning Sun, Wind Exposure and Distance to Water

The result. An east-facing entrance gains 128.6 extra foraging minutes on the stated spring morning (first flight 07:26 against 09:34 shaded); exposed wind costs 25.4% of a 3.0 km trip's net gain and delays first flight by 64.3 minutes. On a 35 °C day with 1.0 L of water demand and 200 water carriers, the maximum tolerable water distance is 368 m — water at 1.0 km delivers only 0.55 L. The paper turns the three effects into a 100-point scored siting rule.

Swarm Timing and the Price of a Swarm: A Congestion Crossing, an Inspection-Interval Bound, and a Honey Cost in Kilograms

The result. Under stated spring growth assumptions the bees-per-litre congestion threshold is crossed 36.48 days after 1 March, with cell occupancy following at 38.89 days; the derived inspection-interval bound is 7 days, from queen-cell sealing at day 8 less a one-day margin. A prime swarm imposes an 18-day laying gap — 27,000 bees never laid — and costs 38.46 kg of honey in a strong flow against 10.58 kg in a weak flow, dominated by the foraging deficit rather than the 0.66 kg carried away.

A Bait Hive Sized by Arithmetic: A Capture-Odds Model and the Cheapest Specification That Reaches a Stated Probability

The result. In a multiplicative capture-odds model over 240 specifications, only 8 reach the target probability of 0.4 against a reference box at 0.5, because compromises multiply. The cheapest compliant specification is 30 L volume, 12.5 cm² entrance, mounted at 3 m, with old comb: relative odds 0.693, probability 0.409, cost $73.0 against $94.0 for the reference box. The paper states plainly which parts of the model are assumptions.

Smoker Fuel and the Inspection Clock: A Fuel Sizing Rule in Grams per Hive and the Pace at Which Smoke Demand Outruns the Chamber

The result. The fuel sizing rule runs from 15.6 g per hive for wood pellets to 33.6 g per hive for dried grass, with pine needles at 28.8 g. Chamber endurance is density-driven: 35.77 hives per load for pellets against 2.81 for pine needles, stretching a 24-hive round from 4.8 to 5.4 hours through refills alone. Smoke demand outruns the chamber within the hour above 12.5 hives/hour on burlap and 6.25 hives/hour on cardboard.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24115372

Wax Rendering, Weighed Honestly: Mass Balance, Method Losses, and a Solar Melter Sizing Rule

The result. Old brood comb yields 0.2132 kg of wax per kilogram in a water bath (0.1725 kg solar, 0.2484 kg with a second pressing) against 0.8565 kg for cappings; the belief that old comb renders about half wax does not survive, since its wax fraction is only 0.32 to begin with. Solar melter sizing is governed by tray loading rather than energy: 2.5 m² is recommended for a 500 kg harvest's 12 kg of cappings, and washing cappings recovers 0.153 kg of honey per kilogram.

The Moisture Bound and the Fermentation Clock: Water Activity, Yeast Growth, and a Ripening Rule for Honey

The result. The moisture ceiling for 365-day storage with a safety factor of 2 at 25 °C is 19.0% (water activity 0.601); past it the clock runs fast, with detectable fermentation in 55.3 days at 20% moisture and 29.3 days at 21% at 25 °C (122.3 and 65.1 days at 15 °C), so the slogan that honey never spoils does not survive for high-moisture honey. A 2.5 kg frame at 20% moisture must lose 0.0758 kg of water to reach the 17.5% target.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24115400

Crystallisation and the Storage Temperature Rule: Glucose Supersaturation, Temperature-Dependent Rates, and the Cost of Warming Honey Back

The result. Cellar-cool storage near 14 °C is the fastest-crystallising condition: clover honey at a glucose-to-water ratio of 1.85 shows crystals in 29.6 days at 14 °C against 80.5 days at 25 °C, 2.72 times longer, so liquid honey should be stored warm and out of the low teens. Reliquefying a 20 kg bucket at 40 °C costs 1,563.6 kJ (0.434 kWh), and the honest cost of warming honey back is total time at temperature, not peak temperature alone.

Paper · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24115410

The Varroa Arithmetic That Treatment-Free Claims Must Answer: Per-Cycle Growth, Threshold Crossing Dates, and the Removal Fraction Required to Hold a Colony Flat

The result. Per-cycle mite multipliers are 1.5729 in the worker-brood season and 1.7181 in the drone-brood season, requiring 36.4% and 41.8% removal per cycle to hold the load flat; annual growth is 104.86× untreated, 49.42× with one brood break and 18.26× with drone removal and a break combined, while drone removal alone delivers only 26.7% in-season. A 50-mite start on 1 March crosses the 3% threshold on 17 July (12 August with a brood break). The claim that a healthy colony holds varroa in balance untreated is disproved under the stated assumptions.

Top-Bar Versus Frame Hive Economics Over a Working Life: Cost per Kilogram, Labour Valuation, and the Break-Even Bounds Between the Two Systems

The result. The top-bar hive wins at unpaid labour (1.87 against 4.16 $/kg over 5 years; 1.13 against 2.88 $/kg over 10 years) and the frame hive wins at $20/hr (8.96 against 9.20 $/kg over 5 years; 7.68 against 8.47 $/kg over 10 years). The break-even bounds originated: a labour value of $18.11/hr over 5 years and $13.79/hr over 10 years, and at $20/hr the frame hive needs a yield ratio above 1.6232 (5 years) or 1.5118 (10 years) against the assumed 1.6667.
