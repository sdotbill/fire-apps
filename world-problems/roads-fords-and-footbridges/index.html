A Corduroy Road over Soft Ground: Load Spreading, a Log Sizing Rule, Service Life, and the Speed the Surface Allows

The problem. Crossing swamp and soft ground with a log road whose size, life, and speed limit are computed rather than traditional guesses.

The result. Sizing rule d >= sqrt(P/(35.2 x allowable soil pressure)) gives 0.159 m for a 20 kN wheel on soft peat; a 0.25 m log gives 9.1 kPa soil pressure (safety factor 4.95) and bending does not govern — settlement ends the road at 15.0 years wet and 6.0 years drained, with comfort speed limits of 16.2 km/h new and 9.4 km/h at end of life; corduroy beats a gravel causeway on annualised cost only with on-site timber over ground that stays wet.

Sizing a Culvert for a Dirt Road Crossing a Seasonal Stream: Peak Flow, Pipe Capacity and the Price of Closure Days

The result. Twin 1200 mm pipes (11.03 m3/s) exceed the 50-year peak (9.26 m3/s) and cut expected closure from 1.86 to 0.08 days per year, for 7,700 dollars more than a single 900 mm pipe.

How Fast a Gravel Road Loses Its Surface: Loss per Vehicle Pass, the Re-gravelling Interval, and the Traffic Level Below Which Keeping the Road Costs More Than Its Journeys Are Worth

The problem. Gravel roads re-surfaced by habit and abandoned by guesswork instead of by a measured loss rate and an honest value test.

The result. Baseline loss is 1.14 kg per vehicle pass and 7.8 mm per year, giving a 6.41-year re-gravelling interval and $2,297/km/yr keeping cost ($0.042 per journey); a single kilometre is worth keeping down to about 1 vehicle/day at a stated $9.00 journey value, but a 20 km spur turns uneconomic below 10.6 vehicles/day, and gravel stays cheaper than sealing until about 3,168 vehicles/day.

When an Unsurfaced Track Becomes Impassable: Gully Growth per Season and the Maintenance Interval That Prevents It

The problem. Unsurfaced hillside tracks that become impassable within a wet season for lack of drainage and timely maintenance.

The result. Modelled rut incision is 23.7 cm per wet season, so a pickup fails in 0.8 seasons and a hand cart in 1.1 seasons untreated; cross-drains at 10 m spacing cut incision to 0.95 cm per season and hold the track for 6.0 labour-days per km per year.

The result. Pickup failure falls from 120 days untreated to 2 days with side drainage plus a 150 mm gravel cap; gravel alone (150 mm) leaves 106 days, and an expansive clay at CBR 1.2 percent fails even under 300 mm.

The Steepest Gradient for All-Day Loaded Work: A Bound for Porters, Handcarts, and Draught Carts

The problem. Paths built too steep for the loaded traffic that must work them all day.

The result. All-day gradient bound is 5.1% for a porter carrying 25 kg at 0.9 m/s, 4.6% for a 180 kg handcart pushed by one person, and 3.4% for a 600 kg ox cart (force-limited at 436 N); above 10.2% switchbacks or steps become unavoidable, and descent does not bind harder on routes built to the climbing bound.

A Reaction Ferry Sized from River, Hull and Cable: Crossing Time, Tension, Payload and the Currents at Which It Starts and Stops

The problem. Communities without a bridge or fuel need a current-powered river crossing whose capacity, cable and anchor loads, and working current range are known before it is trusted.

The result. At 1.5 m/s the ferry crosses 60 m in 48 s with cable tension 4190 N (anchors designed for 8380 N per bank) and an operational payload cap of 2000 kg (23 people or 4 cart units, rejecting the unsafe 12440 kg buoyancy figure); it works only between 0.84 and 3.28 m/s, with bank towing as the low-current alternative.

Dust from an Unpaved Road, Priced per Vehicle: Emission, Exposure at a Roadside House, and the Cost of Three Suppressions per Unit of Dose Avoided

The problem. Dust from unpaved roads settling on homes, priced so suppression money goes where it buys the most clean air.

The result. Traffic mix emits 264.7 g PM10 per vehicle-km, giving a 20.4 ug/m3 annual mean at a house 25 m away; a gravel cap is cheapest per unit of dose avoided at $82, versus watering at $481 and speed control at $3,342 once travel time is counted, and below about 2 houses per km none of the three pays.

Stepping Stones and Single-Log Crossings, Sized Honestly: Spacing, Tread, Stability and the Days the Crossing Does Not Exist

The problem. People are injured on informally placed stepping stones and single logs whose spacing, tread, slipperiness and seasonal availability were never sized in advance.

The result. A loaded wet crossing needs stones at 0.40 m spacing (29 for 12 m) with grooved treads, because friction demanded (0.47) exceeds wet rock; stones need anchoring only above 2.66 m/s, yet the crossing is unavailable 169.3 days per year, and a 0.35 m flattened, grooved log is the reasonable-risk single-log specification.

A Sizing Rule for Switchback Paths: Leg Length, Turn Spacing, Extra Distance, and Drainage on a Hillside

The problem. Switchback paths laid out by eye, with legs, turns, and drainage that fail in use.

The result. At a stated 6.0% design gradient and 3.6 m vertical interval, switchback legs are 60.0 m long; on a 30% hillside the extra-distance factor is 5.0, and above a 50% hillside slope switchbacks stop working and terracing or steps take over; cross-drains every 30 m cut modelled erosion by a factor of 2.58.
