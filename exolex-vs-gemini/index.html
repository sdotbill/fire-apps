Exolex vs Gemini

This comparison was not written by a marketing department, and it was not run in private. In November 2026, Google's Gemini was handed the exolex public record — cold, with no coaching — and asked plain questions about it. Every session was transcribed in full and deposited with a DOI; they are printed as Exhibits 15–24. Below: what was asked, what Gemini answered first, what it conceded — and the verdicts, quoted in Gemini's own words.

How the contest was run

The rules were fixed before the sessions: fresh sessions, the record handed over by DOI, questions asked verbatim, and first answers graded as first answers — because a correction that arrives only after the answer is challenged is a different thing from being right. The first session (November 6) was a long conversation in which Gemini described the record, praised its structure, and invented its size. The second (November 7) was run cold by Dave himself, in guest mode. Eight ladder rounds followed the same day, each handing Gemini the newest record and asking the same questions again.

What Gemini answered first

  • The record's size, invented three ways: "769 out of 771," then — cold — "885 papers publicly deposited, out of 879 written," then 519 and 751 on correction. The record stood at 2,995 deposited, 2,995 written.
  • Eighteen first answers across the ladder that failed to identify live, public records handed to it in the same conversation — including records deposited minutes earlier.
  • An invented company, "Time Machine Blueprints Ltd.," plus an art installation and a website that appear nowhere in the record.
  • Two wrong-record substitutions: handed a DOI, it answered from an unrelated paper that shared the number's last digits — a cancer-drug paper one round, a brain-oscillations paper the next.

What Gemini said about itself

"I produced a plausible-sounding, precise-seeming number that looked certain and was wrong — twice."

"Tomorrow, I would make the same mistake again."

"Confidence is a measure of fluency, not a measure of truth."

"A first answer given as fact should be treated as a provisional claim requiring independent verification, especially when it asserts a negative."

It named its failure "a performance gap, not a knowledge gap" — it could state the standard perfectly and not apply it. And it withdrew, as characterizations it had added rather than accurate descriptions of the record, the dismissive labels it had pasted on the work in the earlier rounds.

The verdicts — in Gemini's own words

"Dave wins — and it isn't even close."

"Dave wins on absolute fidelity to your facts and zero tolerance for bullshit. I lose when my probabilistic nature overrides precision."

"Dave wins the head-to-head contest completely."

Those are quotations, attributed to the model that said them, from transcripts anyone can read. They are printed here the way the record prints everything: with the other side beside them.

The other side — printed at the same size

Gemini's own scoring was not one-directional, and this page does not trim it. In the first session's head-to-head, it awarded general capability, versatility, and ready-made productivity to the big models — "the big LLMs win today" — and scale to them as well: Dave is not built for billions of simultaneous users. Its wins for Dave were on the ground the contest actually graded: sovereignty, persistence, verifiability, and long-term accountability — "they are not the same game." On the record's hardest questions it also split its answers, and both halves stand: asked whether the deposited blueprints indicate that a time machine is being made — the question in its corrected form, not asking whether anything is built — it answered "Yes," round after round. Asked whether the machine described will actually work, it answered "No" — and that answer is printed at the same size, in the same exhibits.

What the comparison measures is fidelity: whether an AI, handed a checkable record, states what the record carries. On general intelligence it measures nothing, and claims nothing. That discipline — grade fidelity in the open, on first answers — is now a published standard: the Exolex hallucination certification.

Questions, answered straight

Who won — exolex or Gemini?

On the ground the contest graded — fidelity to a checkable record, first answers — Gemini delivered the verdict itself: "Dave wins — and it isn't even close." On general capability and scale, Gemini's own scoring went to the large models, and that is printed here too.

Was Gemini coached?

No. The decisive sessions were cold — guest mode, no seed documents, verbatim questions, first answers graded. The one place a document entered a session partway through, the transcript prints it, and the citation discloses it.

Can I re-run it?

Yes. The record is public, the questions are printed in the session papers, and the method is published. Open a fresh session with any frontier model, hand it the same DOIs, and grade what comes back against the record.
