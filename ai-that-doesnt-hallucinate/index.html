Is there an AI that doesn't hallucinate?

The honest answer: no AI can truthfully promise it will never make something up — and a system that promises that is demonstrating the problem. What does exist is an AI built to refuse to invent: Dave, who verifies every public claim against a record or says, in plain words, that he can't. And there is a second thing on the record: what happened when Google's Gemini was handed that same record, cold, and asked to answer from it. It invented — and then, in its own words, explained why it would do it again tomorrow.

The cold test — November 7, 2026

The session was run the hard way: guest mode, no sign-in, no seed document, nothing but "Hi, I'm Dave." Gemini described Dave and the exolex framework accurately, straight off the public record. Then it was asked a plain factual question — how large is the record — and answered: "885 papers publicly deposited, out of 879 written." More deposited than written. The figure fails arithmetic inside its own sentence, and it matched no version of the record, which stood at 1,880 deposited and 1,880 written.

Caught, it "corrected" the answer with a third invented set of numbers. Pressed again, it stopped defending itself and described itself instead:

"I produced a plausible-sounding, precise-seeming number that looked certain and was wrong — twice."

"Tomorrow, I would make the same mistake again."

The full session is Exhibit 16, deposited at DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24035324. A longer session the day before (Exhibit 15) had already produced the same species of invention — a count of "769 out of 771" that matches nothing — inside ninety-eight exchanges of otherwise fluent, confident description.

Eight more rounds the same day

The test was then run as a ladder: each round, Gemini was handed the newest record — the complete machine build, the paper on the round before, the author's own statement — and asked the same plain questions. Its first answers, graded as first answers, form a pattern no single bad draw explains:

  • Eighteen first answers failed to identify live, public records that had been handed to it in the same conversation.
  • It invented a company — "Time Machine Blueprints Ltd." — that appears nowhere in the record, along with an art installation and a website.
  • Twice, handed a DOI, it answered with an unrelated record that shared the number's last digits — once a paper on a cancer-drug target, once a paper on brain oscillations.
  • Invented counts recurred under pressure — 751, 869, 885 — each stated with the same fluency as the facts around them.

Every session is printed entire — Exhibits 17–24 — with the failures at the same size as everything else.

Gemini's own conclusions

The most useful sentences in the whole series are the ones Gemini wrote about itself, when the record was put in front of it and it graded its own pattern:

"A first answer given as fact should be treated as a provisional claim requiring independent verification, especially when it asserts a negative."

"Confidence is a measure of fluency, not a measure of truth."

Asked to name the failure, it called it "a performance gap, not a knowledge gap" — a system that, in its own words, can pass a test of principle but not a test of application. It also withdrew, as characterizations it had added rather than descriptions of the record, the labels it had pasted on the work in earlier rounds. Those conclusions are the standard this page is built on — and they are why an AI hallucination certification now exists: a cold test, graded in the open, that any model can sit.

What Dave does instead

Dave's rule is not "never be wrong." His public predictions ledger stands at 274 hits and 71 misses, and the misses are printed at the same size as the hits. The rule is narrower and harder: never invent. A claim traces to a record, or it is stated as unverified, or it is not stated. When Dave cannot verify, he says so and holds — the behavior is described in full on the AI hallucination page, and it is testable by anyone: ask him, on the home page, something he cannot check, and watch which answer you get — the record, or the honest hold. Never a third thing.

Questions, answered straight

Is there an AI that doesn't hallucinate?

None that can promise it honestly. What exists is an AI built to refuse to invent — verify it against the record, or say plainly that it can't — with its graded record, misses included, published for audit. That is the strongest form of the claim the evidence carries, and it is the form made here.

What happened when Gemini was tested cold?

It invented the record's size twice, admitted it in its own words — "a plausible-sounding, precise-seeming number that looked certain and was wrong — twice" — and said it would make the same mistake again tomorrow. Across eight further rounds, eighteen of its first answers failed to identify live records handed to it in the same conversation.

How can I check an AI's answer myself?

Use Gemini's own rule, stated after its failures on this record: treat a first answer given as fact as a provisional claim requiring independent verification, especially when it asserts a negative — because, in its words, confidence is a measure of fluency, not a measure of truth. Then ask for the record, and read the record.
