The Dust You Cannot See: Millimetre Optical Depths in Protoplanetary Discs and the Resulting Mass Underestimate

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175131 Millimetre continuum fluxes are routinely converted into dust masses on the assumption that the emission is optically thin, so that every grain contributes. In the inner regions of a disc of ordinary mass that assumption fails badly. With the standard 1.3 mm opacity of 2.3 cm^2 per gram of dust, the face-on optical depth on the reference profile is 39 at 1 AU, 7.5 at 3 AU and 1.24 at 10 AU, falling below unity only outside 12 AU. Integrating the true profile against the thin-inferred profile over 0.1 to 100 AU, the true dust mass is 78 Earth masses while the optically thin conversion returns 53 Earth masses — an underestimate by a factor of 1.5, before any allowance for grain growth changing the opacity itself. The missing mass hides exactly where planet formation is fastest, which biases disc demographic conclusions: discs are more massive in solids, and planet formation less efficient per unit observed dust, than the thin conversion suggests. The effect is a radiative-transfer triviality with demographic consequences, and it is computed here from one opacity and one profile. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

The Slow Grinder: Collisional Cascade Timescales for Debris Belts From First Principles

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175160 Debris discs are collisional cascades: belts of planetesimals grinding themselves to dust over billions of years, with the dust we see continuously resupplied and removed. The cascade's pace is set by the collision time of its largest bodies. This paper derives that time from first principles — a Dohnanyi size distribution normalised by the belt mass, the projectile population able to disrupt a 100 km body at the belt's collision speeds, and the resulting encounter rate — across belts of 0.1 to 10 Earth masses at 30 to 80 AU. The collision time is 9.4e+08 years for an Earth-mass belt at 30 AU, 8.2e+09 years at 50 AU and 6.0e+10 years at 80 AU, falling to 8.2e+08 years for a ten-Earth-mass belt at 50 AU. Low-mass belts therefore never process their largest bodies within a stellar lifetime — their cascades are fed from below and their dust is a trickle — while massive belts grind through their stock in well under a gigayear and fade. The corresponding initial fractional luminosities run from 2.0e-05 (Earth-mass, 50 AU) to 2.0e-04 (ten Earth masses), bracketing the brighter end of the observed debris population. The cascade clock, like the disc clocks before it, is set by ordinary collisions far below any gravitational bound (3.0e-69 of the frame's density ceiling at 50 AU). Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

Waking the Belt: Self-Stirring Times, the Case for Planetary Stirrers, and the Maximum Luminosity a Debris Disc Can Show

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175173 A planetesimal belt is dark until something stirs it: collision velocities must be raised to disruptive levels before the cascade can begin. This paper computes when a belt can stir itself — by growing its own Pluto-sized stirrers — and what luminosity it can then show, for belts at 45 AU. The self-stirring time is 2851 Myr for a one-Earth-mass belt, 285 Myr for ten Earth masses and 95 Myr for thirty. Bright debris discs are already shining at 10 to 20 Myr (the beta Pictoris system is the type example), which self-stirring at these masses cannot do: an external stirrer, most plausibly a planet, is required for the young bright population, while self-stirring suffices for belts that switch on at hundreds of Myr. Once stirred, a ten-Earth-mass belt shows fractional luminosity 1.8e-04 at 500 Myr, 1.1e-04 at 1 Gyr and 2.5e-05 at 5 Gyr — the familiar 1/t fading — and cannot exceed its full-stock value at any age, which caps how bright an old debris disc can be without a recent event. The debris sky's brightness distribution is thus a record of two clocks: stirring, which sets the onset, and collisional grinding, which sets the decay. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

Shrinking Discs: How Fast Radial Drift Erodes the Dust Disc's Outer Edge, and What the Observed Sizes Require

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175146 A dust disc should eat itself from the outside in: the grains at its outer edge drift inward at a speed that, on a standard profile, does not depend on radius, so the edge marches inward at a constant rate and the disc's dust radius is a clock. This paper winds the clock. The drift speed is 11 m per second at Stokes 0.1, 1.1 at Stokes 0.01 and 0.32 at Stokes 0.003 (radius-independent to within a factor of 1.00 on this profile). A disc born with its dust edge at 100 AU shrinks to 30 AU in 0.10 Myr at Stokes 0.03 and 0.31 Myr at Stokes 0.01; after 1 Myr the edge of a Stokes-0.003 population still sits at 32 AU, while a Stokes-0.01 population has drained to the inner disc entirely. Observed dust discs in 1 to 3 Myr-old regions have radii of tens of AU — consistent with the clock only if the emitting grains sit at Stokes numbers of a few thousandths, well below the fragmentation ceilings, or if pressure traps hold the edge. Gas discs, measured in CO, are systematically larger than dust discs by factors of two to three, the expected fingerprint of an inward-marching dust edge. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

Flat Layers: Dust Settling Equilibrium, Settling Times, and the Turbulence That ALMA's Thin Discs Require

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175092 Dust settles toward the midplane until turbulence stirs it back up, and the equilibrium layer thickness is a direct gauge of the turbulence. This paper computes the gauge and reads the observations with it. The settling equilibrium H_d/H = sqrt(alpha/(alpha + St)) gives layer thicknesses of 0.10 for alpha = 1e-3 and Stokes 0.1, 0.30 for alpha = 1e-2, and 0.032 for alpha = 1e-4. Settling itself is fast — 2.62e+02 years at 30 AU for Stokes 0.1 — so every observed layer is an equilibrium layer, not a work in progress. Inverting the gauge for the thin layers that edge-on disc observations report (H_d/H of 0.1 to 0.2 for millimetre grains at tens of AU) requires alpha of 1.0e-03 to 4.2e-03 for Stokes-0.1 grains, and 1.3e-03 for Stokes 0.03. The midplanes of real discs are therefore quiescent at the level planetesimal-formation thresholds require, a conclusion reached here from geometry alone. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured; the disc material sits at 4.4e-70 of the gravitational frame's density ceiling even at 100 AU.

How Large Can a Grain Grow Before Collisions Break It? Fragmentation and Drift Size Limits Across the Disc

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24174971 Turbulent collisions set a ceiling on grain size: grow too large, and relative velocities exceed the fragmentation threshold and collisions shatter instead of stick. Using the standard fragmentation-limit formula with turbulence alpha = 1e-3, this paper computes the ceiling across the disc for bare silicate grains (fragmentation velocity 1 m per second) and icy grains (10 m per second), and compares it with the size at which radial drift removes grains as fast as they grow. At 1 AU the silicate ceiling is 0.2 cm and the icy ceiling 24.4 cm; at 10 AU they fall to 0.02 cm and 2.4 cm. The drift limit is far above both through most of the disc — 20 m at 1 AU and 20.0 cm at 10 AU — and falls below the icy fragmentation ceiling only beyond about 82 AU, where both limits have shrunk to millimetres (2.0 mm against 2.4 mm at 100 AU). Fragmentation, not drift, is therefore the binding size limit in the planet-forming region. The Stokes number at the fragmentation ceiling is only 0.0003 for silicates at 1 AU but reaches 0.107 for ice at 10 AU, close to the range where streaming concentration becomes possible. The composition of a grain decides how large it can grow and which dynamical regime it can enter. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured; disc densities sit at 2.5e-67 of the gravitational frame's ceiling, so the limits are collisional, not gravitational.

How Far Below the Ceilings? Protoplanetary Disc Midplane Densities and Keplerian Shear Against a Torsion Density Bound

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24174928 A torsion-regulated gravitational bound sets a maximum density of 9.72e+57 kg per cubic metre and a critical shear rate of 8.56e+32 per second. This paper asks a deliberately narrow question: by what factor do the midplane densities and Keplerian shear rates of an ordinary protoplanetary disc fall below those two ceilings? For a Hayashi-type disc around a solar-mass star, the midplane density at 1 AU is 1.36e-06 kg per cubic metre, a factor of 1.40e-64 of the density ceiling, and the Keplerian shear there is 2.99e-07 per second, a factor of 3.49e-40 of the critical shear. Even at 0.1 AU the density reaches only 7.86e-62 of the ceiling. Keplerian shear would equal the critical rate only at 4.96e-27 AU from the star, deep inside the star itself, and the midplane density would reach the ceiling only at 6.03e-24 AU. The negative result is plain: the bound does not constrain protoplanetary disc structure at any radius, and every operative constraint on such discs comes from disc physics and observation, not from the gravitational ceiling. All figures are computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

The Race at the Heart of Planet Formation: Grain Growth Times Against Radial Drift Times From 1 to 100 AU

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24174947 Dust in a protoplanetary disc must grow from micrometre grains to planetesimals before the gas drag that enables growth also drags it into the star. This paper computes both clocks on the same disc model. The growth e-folding time, 1/(Z Omega) for a dust-to-gas ratio Z = 0.01, is 15.9 years at 1 AU and 2.62e+03 years at 30 AU, and the climb from a micrometre to Stokes number 0.1 takes about ten e-folds, so the full growth time is 215 years at 1 AU, 5051 years at 10 AU and 21934 years at 30 AU. The drift time at Stokes 0.1 is 445 years at 1 AU but only 4445 years at 10 AU and 13335 years at 30 AU: growth wins narrowly at 1 AU and loses beyond a few AU. At Stokes number 1 the position is hopeless, with drift at 10 AU taking 880 years against 6210 years of growth, while at Stokes 0.01 drift at 30 AU takes 1.32e+05 years and small grains survive. The barrier is a window in Stokes space, roughly 0.03 to 1, that growth must cross faster than drift allows — in a smooth disc, it cannot, beyond the inner few AU. Midplane densities and shear rates sit at 1.4e-64 and 3.5e-40 of the gravitational frame's ceilings, so the barrier is purely aerodynamic. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

Feeding on Pebble Rain: Accretion Rates and Core Growth Times for Embryos at 5 and 10 AU

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175033 Once a planetesimal is large enough, it grows not by colliding with its peers but by sweeping up the pebbles that drift past it — pebble accretion, the fastest growth mode known in discs. This paper computes the rates in the two-dimensional Hill regime for pebbles of Stokes number 0.1 in a reference disc. An Earth-mass embryo at 5 AU accretes 4 Earth masses per million years, a mass-doubling time of 0.25 Myr; a hundredth of an Earth mass accretes 0.2 Earth masses per Myr with a doubling time of 0.05 Myr. Integrating the mass-dependent rate, an embryo grows from 0.1 to 10 Earth masses in about 2.3 Myr at 5 AU and 3.3 Myr at 10 AU — inside the characteristic disc lifetime at 5 AU, and comparable to that lifetime at 10 AU. The integrated demand on the pebble supply is a few hundred Earth masses over the growth, comparable to the total solid inventory the disc can deliver, so pebble accretion is fast enough to build giant-planet cores and hungry enough to explain why not every disc does. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured; the disc densities involved sit at 1.7e-66 of the gravitational frame's ceiling.

Starlight as a Solvent: EUV and X-Ray Photoevaporation Rates, Launch Radii and Disc Dispersal Times

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175046 A young star irradiates its own disc with ultraviolet and X-ray photons energetic enough to heat surface gas until it escapes. This paper computes the launch geometry and the mass-loss rates for the two main drivers and converts them into dispersal times. Ionised gas at 10^4 K has a gravitational radius of 8.9 AU around a solar-mass star, inside which it stays bound and outside which it flows away; the EUV wind is launched mainly beyond about 0.9 AU. The EUV mass-loss rate at an ionising photon luminosity of 1e41 per second is 4.0e-10 solar masses per year, clearing a 0.01-solar-mass disc in 25 Myr acting alone — too slow. The X-ray rate at L_X = 1e30 erg per second is 6.0e-09 solar masses per year, fifteen times faster, clearing the same disc in 1.7 Myr and a 0.003-solar-mass disc in 0.5 Myr. Photoevaporation can therefore end disc lifetimes on the observed few-Myr scale only in its X-ray (or FUV) form and only once accretion has already thinned the disc; as a sole agent acting on a young massive disc it fails by an order of magnitude, which is why dispersal is a two-stage process. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

Born Big: The Characteristic Size of Planetesimals Formed by Gravitational Collapse of Pebble Clouds

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175016 If pebble clouds collapse gravitationally, the planetesimals they make are not pebble-sized or kilometre-sized by accident: their mass is set by the density at which self-gravity beats tidal shear (the Roche density) and by the size of the collapsing patch. This paper computes the characteristic product across the disc. The Roche density falls from 4.3e-04 kg per cubic metre at 1 AU to 3.4e-06 at 5 AU; taking the collapsing patch as 0.01 of a gas scale height — the middle of the filament-width range seen in streaming-instability simulations — gives body diameters of 370 km at 1 AU, 553 km at 5 AU, 865 km at 30 AU and 1169 km at 100 AU, with the patch-size bracket spreading the 5 AU value from 276 to 1105 km. Planetesimals are born big: hundreds of kilometres, within a factor of a few of the sizes that the asteroid belt's size distribution and the Kuiper belt's binary population independently suggest for the primordial population. Growth models that start from kilometre-sized seeds are starting two orders of magnitude too small. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

Fluff as a Strategy: How Porosity Holds the Stokes Number Down and Lets Aggregates Grow Past the Drift Barrier

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175104 The drift barrier exists because compact grains couple to the gas in proportion to their size: grow, and the Stokes number — hence the drift speed — grows with you. Porous aggregates break that proportionality. This paper computes the Stokes number of fractal aggregates with dimensions D = 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0 (compact) at 5 and 30 AU. For D = 2 the Stokes number is independent of size altogether: 1.5e-07 at 5 AU and 2.3e-06 at 30 AU, however large the aggregate grows. For D = 2.5, reaching Stokes 0.1 at 5 AU requires an aggregate 4.2e+04 m across, and Stokes 1 requires 4.2e+06 m, against 0.645 m for a compact grain — fluff buys some six orders of magnitude in size at fixed aerodynamic coupling. At 30 AU a D = 2.5 aggregate reaches Stokes 1 only at 1.9e+04 m. Porosity therefore postpones the drift barrier from centimetres to hundreds of metres or beyond, letting aggregates grow quietly in the safe, well-coupled regime — at the price of extreme fragility, which the bouncing and compaction physics must then answer for. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

Holding Back the Drift: The Minimum Pressure-Bump Amplitude That Traps Pebbles, and How Long the Trap Must Hold

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24174987 Radial drift stops where the pressure gradient vanishes, so a local pressure maximum — a bump — is a trap for pebbles. This paper computes, for a bump at 30 AU in a reference disc, the minimum bump amplitude at which a pressure maximum actually exists, and the retention budget of the trap once it exists. For a bump one scale height wide the critical amplitude is 0.62 of the local pressure; for a bump two scale heights wide it is 2.22 — wide bumps are strikingly inefficient traps, because the restoring gradient of a broad multiplicative pressure bump saturates as its amplitude grows. Below those amplitudes the bump merely slows drift; above them it reverses it. Inside a working trap, a Stokes-0.1 pebble drifts one scale height in 1.04e+03 years but takes 2.64e+04 years to diffuse the same distance, so drift wins over diffusion by a factor of about 25 and the equilibrium pile-up for a 30 per cent bump reaches a formal factor of 1.1e+13, in practice capped by feedback and fragmentation at one to two orders of magnitude. Small grains at Stokes 0.01 concentrate by a factor of just 20.1 — appreciable, but negligible beside the pebble value — and keep leaking through on longer timescales. Traps are therefore size-selective filters with a sharp amplitude threshold — a combination that matches the ringed appearance of millimetre disc images. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

Moving Frost Lines: Snowline Positions for Water, Carbon Dioxide and Carbon Monoxide in Passive and Accreting Discs

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175120 Where a volatile freezes out onto grains — its snowline — the solid mass, the grain stickiness and the gas composition all change at once, so snowline positions organise planet formation. This paper computes them for water, carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide on the reference disc, first for passive irradiation heating alone and then with viscous accretion heating added at three accretion rates. Passive snowlines sit at 2.7 AU (water), 16 AU (carbon dioxide) and 196 AU (carbon monoxide — outside most discs entirely). Accretion heating pushes the water snowline out to 3.8 AU at an accretion rate of 1e-6 solar masses per year and lets it fall back through 2.9 AU at 1e-7 to 2.7 AU at 1e-8 as the disc ages: the frost lines sweep inward across the planet-forming region during the disc's life. An icy pebble drifting from 10 AU reaches the water snowline in about 6.50e+03 years, so the volatile delivery across the line is fast compared with every evolutionary clock, and the inner disc's vapour content is set by the current pebble flux rather than by history. The carbon monoxide result is a plain discrepancy worth printing: observed CO snowlines at 20 to 40 AU imply midplanes far colder than this profile, so either the profile or the simple sublimation picture fails in the outer disc. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

How Much Dust Is Enough? A Settling-Based Threshold for Streaming Concentration, Tested Against Measured Disc Dust Abundances

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175003 Streaming instability is the leading mechanism for concentrating pebbles into gravitationally collapsing clouds, but it engages strongly only when the midplane dust-to-gas ratio approaches unity. This paper derives the required vertically integrated dust abundance from that condition and the standard settling equilibrium, Z_req = sqrt(alpha/(alpha + St)), and tests it against measured disc dust abundances. For turbulence alpha = 1e-3, the requirement is Z = 0.302 at Stokes number 0.01, 0.100 at Stokes 0.1, and 0.032 at Stokes 1 — everywhere far above the Z of 0.005 to 0.02 that disc surveys measure globally. At the measured Z = 0.01, the midplane ratio reaches only 0.10 for Stokes 0.1 at alpha = 1e-3. The plain conclusion is that smooth discs of solar composition do not trigger strong streaming concentration: either turbulence must be as low as alpha = 1e-4, where the requirement at Stokes 0.3 relaxes to 0.018, or dust must first be concentrated by pressure traps, whose local enhancements supply the missing factor. Planetesimal formation by this route is therefore a local, not a global, event. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

The Endgame in Numbers: When the Wind Overtakes Accretion, How Long the Inner Disc Survives, and Why a Tenth of Discs Have Holes

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175078 Disc dispersal has two timescales — a long life and a rapid death — and the standard explanation is a handover: once the accretion rate through the disc falls to the photoevaporative wind rate, the wind opens a gap, the inner disc drains, and the outer disc follows. This paper computes the handover with a similarity-solution accretion decline and the wind rates of the companion calculation. Against an X-ray wind of 6e-9 solar masses per year, the switch fires at 3.3 Myr; against an EUV wind of 4e-10 it would not fire until 20 Myr, after essentially all discs are already gone — EUV alone cannot run the endgame. The inner disc inside 5 AU holds 0.0046 solar masses and drains in 0.07 Myr at alpha = 1e-2 or 0.72 Myr at alpha = 1e-3, so the predicted fraction of discs caught with holes is 2 to 18 per cent — bracketing the observed transition-disc fraction of about ten per cent. Once the hole opens it expands at about 16 AU per Myr at 10 AU, reaching the observed hole sizes of tens of AU within the endgame duration. The two-timescale structure, the hole fraction and the hole sizes all fall out of three rates and one profile. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

The Turbulence Budget: What Alpha Must Do to Drain a Disc in Three Million Years, and Why Measurements Refuse

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175062 If discs evolve viscously, a single number — the turbulence parameter alpha — must simultaneously set the disc lifetime, the accretion rate onto the star, and the turbulent velocities that observations measure. This paper prices all three demands on the reference disc, and reports where they agree and where they refuse to. The lifetime demand: the viscous time at 50 AU is 7 Myr at alpha = 1e-3 and 72 Myr at alpha = 1e-4, so draining the outer disc within the observed 3-Myr characteristic lifetime requires alpha of 2.4e-03 at 50 AU and 4.8e-03 at 100 AU. The accretion demand is milder: matching a typical T Tauri accretion rate of 1e-8 solar masses per year at 1 AU requires alpha of only 7.9e-04, and alpha = 1e-3 delivers 1.3e-08 solar masses per year — the inner disc's accretion is the one job a 1e-3 turbulence can do. The measurements, however, sit lower still: line broadening bounds alpha at or below about 1e-3, and dust settling prefers 1e-4 to 1e-3 — and at alpha = 1e-4 the accretion rate falls to 1.3e-09 and the outer disc would live 72 Myr. The budget therefore balances nowhere: it is short by a factor of about 2 to 5 against the most generous reading of the turbulence bounds, and by a factor of 10 to 25 against the settling-preferred values. Accretion driven substantially by magnetic winds rather than by the measured turbulence is a conclusion this arithmetic forces, with the size of the gap stated honestly rather than rounded up. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24180138 Gravitational lensing by scalar density perturbations produces, to leading order, a curl-free E-mode shear pattern only, so the B-mode channel is widely used as a null test. This paper computes the E-mode convergence power for a representative source sample, giving 5.843e-10 at multipole 300, and quantifies how a leakage of E into B, parameterised by a mixing fraction alpha, populates the null channel. A leakage of alpha equal to 0.03 places a fraction 9.00e-04 of the E-mode power into B at multipole 300; producing a spurious B-mode at five percent of the E-mode requires alpha of 0.22, and at one percent requires 0.10. The conclusion is two-sided: a passed B-mode test certifies that leakage-type systematics are below a computable level, but it is blind by construction to systematics that are themselves E-mode, including multiplicative calibration error and intrinsic alignments. Intrinsic Alignment Contamination of Cosmic Shear: A Budget in Units of the Gravitational Signal

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24180982 Galaxies form within the same tidal field that lenses them, so their intrinsic shapes are correlated with each other and with the lensing shear, contaminating a cosmic shear measurement.
