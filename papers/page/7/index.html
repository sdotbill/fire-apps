Paper · Dark-matter detection

Spin-Dependent Scattering: Proton and Neutron Couplings, Target Complementarity and Combined Bounds from Fluorine, Xenon and the Sun

William Alexander Aldred · November 11, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24152051 This study computes spin-dependent scattering rates and capture rates for targets whose sensitivity comes from unpaired protons or unpaired neutrons, and shows how combining them separates the two couplings. Fluorine-rich targets respond mainly to proton coupling, xenon and germanium mainly to neutron coupling, and solar capture responds mainly to proton coupling through hydrogen. A signal or a limit in only one channel leaves a line in coupling space, not a point; two channels with different proton-to-neutron response intersect that line and bound both couplings. Nuclear spin expectation values, not target mass, are the limiting input, and their uncertainties are carried through the combined bounds stated here. All results are computed, not measured.

Paper · Dark-matter detection

Superheavy Dark Matter Candidates: Flux Limits, Multi-Scatter Signatures and the Exposure at Which Mass Stops Mattering

William Alexander Aldred · November 11, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24152069 This study computes event rates and signatures for dark matter candidates with masses from 1e6 GeV to near the Planck mass. As mass rises at fixed local density, the number density and therefore the flux fall in inverse proportion, so single-scatter rates become negligible long before the Planck scale; the compensating signature is that a sufficiently strongly interacting heavy candidate scatters many times while crossing a detector, leaving a slow, straight, multiply-scattering track unlike any single recoil. The paper derives the mass at which the expected flux through a tonne-scale detector falls below one particle per year, and the cross-section range in which multi-scatter searches in large-volume detectors are the only available test. Density-bound screening is reported for each mass, and the bound grows as the square of the mass on each branch. All results are computed, not measured.

Dispersion Across Twelve Decades of Frequency in the Crab Pulsar

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24169204 The Crab pulsar flashes across the electromagnetic spectrum, from radio waves at hundreds of megahertz to gamma rays above 100 MeV — twelve decades of frequency from one rotating neutron star 2 kpc away. Its dispersion measure of 56.77 pc per cubic centimetre sweeps its radio pulse by 1.35 s between 0.4 and 1.4 GHz, and by 23.6 s across a 0.1 to 10 GHz span, yet after that plasma sweep is removed, its radio and gamma-ray pulse peaks align to within a few tenths of a millisecond. Charging a conservative 0.2 ms residual to any vacuum effect bounds fractional propagation differences across those twelve decades at 9.72e-16.

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24169226 The repeating fast radio burst FRB 121102 has a total dispersion measure of about 557 pc per cubic centimetre and a host galaxy at redshift 0.1927, which makes it the first burst whose dispersion could be audited line by line. The audit performed here assigns about 188 pc per cubic centimetre to the Milky Way's disc, an adopted 30 to its halo, and 163 to the intergalactic medium computed from the cosmic baryon density, leaving an observer-frame residual of 176 pc per cubic centimetre, or 209 in the host's frame — a large but physically reasonable contribution from the burst's immediate environment. Gamma-Ray Burst Spectral Lags Are Not Propagation: An Inconsistency Test Across Redshift

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24169238 Long gamma-ray bursts show spectral lags: their pulses peak later at low energies than at high energies, by tenths of a second. If those lags were vacuum dispersion accumulated in propagation, the dispersion scale implied by each burst would have to be the same for all of them. Computing the implied linear scale for three representative cases — lags of 0.02, 0.3 and 2.0 s at redshifts 0.5, 1 and 4, across the standard 25-50 and 100-300 keV bands — gives values spreading over a factor of 13.8, and the lag's observed dependence on burst luminosity and pulse structure, rather than on the cosmological path factor (which grows by 7.2 between redshift 0.5 and 4), contradicts the propagation pattern directly.

Graviton Dispersion and the Compton-Wavelength Bound from GW150914

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24169269 If the graviton has a rest mass, gravitational waves disperse: their lower frequencies travel slower, and a chirping binary signal would arrive smeared relative to the massless waveform. GW150914 swept from about 35 to 150 Hz over a path of about 410 Mpc, and its waveform matched the massless prediction closely. A simplified coherence version of the test, allowing a tenth of a second of differential arrival across the band, bounds the graviton mass at 3.24e-22 eV per c-squared, corresponding to a Compton wavelength above 3.83e+12 km; the collaboration's full phase-based analysis reached about 1.2 x 10^-22 eV per c-squared, stronger because it uses the waveform's phase evolution rather than a single coherence time.

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24169292 A photon with a nonzero rest mass travels slower at lower frequencies, producing a frequency-dependent delay with the same inverse-frequency-squared shape as plasma dispersion but a different dependence on distance and on the plasma column. Using FRB 121102's line of sight — comoving distance 0.82 Gpc as computed here — and the 1.0 to 1.5 GHz band, a residual timing uncertainty of one millisecond after plasma dedispersion bounds the photon mass at 8.56e-16 eV per c-squared; burst structure sharp to a tenth of a millisecond tightens the figure to 2.71e-16 eV per c-squared. The degeneracy with plasma dispersion is the central difficulty, and this paper states exactly how it is broken in principle and how much of it survives in practice.

The Shapiro Delay Difference Between Gravitational Waves and Light from GW170817

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24169313 Gravitational waves and light falling through the same gravitational potential should be delayed by the same amount if the equivalence principle holds for both — the Shapiro delay is then common, and cancels in their arrival difference. GW170817's gamma rays arrived 1.74 s after its gravitational waves despite both signals crossing the Milky Way's potential well on their way from NGC 4993. In the logarithmic point-mass model computed here, the Milky Way's Shapiro coefficient is 223 days per unit of the delay parameter, so the observed second-scale arrival difference bounds the difference between the gravitational-wave and electromagnetic delay parameters at 9.03e-08.
