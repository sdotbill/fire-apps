The Dust You Cannot See: Millimetre Optical Depths in Protoplanetary Discs and the Resulting Mass Underestimate

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175131 Millimetre continuum fluxes are routinely converted into dust masses on the assumption that the emission is optically thin, so that every grain contributes. In the inner regions of a disc of ordinary mass that assumption fails badly. With the standard 1.3 mm opacity of 2.3 cm^2 per gram of dust, the face-on optical depth on the reference profile is 39 at 1 AU, 7.5 at 3 AU and 1.24 at 10 AU, falling below unity only outside 12 AU. Integrating the true profile against the thin-inferred profile over 0.1 to 100 AU, the true dust mass is 78 Earth masses while the optically thin conversion returns 53 Earth masses — an underestimate by a factor of 1.5, before any allowance for grain growth changing the opacity itself. The missing mass hides exactly where planet formation is fastest, which biases disc demographic conclusions: discs are more massive in solids, and planet formation less efficient per unit observed dust, than the thin conversion suggests. The effect is a radiative-transfer triviality with demographic consequences, and it is computed here from one opacity and one profile. Computed from stated assumptions, not measured.

The Slow Grinder: Collisional Cascade Timescales for Debris Belts From First Principles

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175160 Debris discs are collisional cascades: belts of planetesimals grinding themselves to dust over billions of years, with the dust we see continuously resupplied and removed. The cascade's pace is set by the collision time of its largest bodies. This paper derives that time from first principles — a Dohnanyi size distribution normalised by the belt mass, the projectile population able to disrupt a 100 km body at the belt's collision speeds, and the resulting encounter rate — across belts of 0.1 to 10 Earth masses at 30 to 80 AU. The collision time is 9.4e+08 years for an Earth-mass belt at 30 AU, 8.2e+09 years at 50 AU and 6.0e+10 years at 80 AU, falling to 8.2e+08 years for a ten-Earth-mass belt at 50 AU. Low-mass belts therefore never process their largest bodies within a stellar lifetime — their cascades are fed from below and their dust is a trickle — while massive belts grind through their stock in well under a gigayear and fade. The corresponding initial fractional luminosities run from 2.0e-05 (Earth-mass, 50 AU) to 2.0e-04 (ten Earth masses), bracketing the brighter end of the observed debris population. The cascade clock, like the disc clocks before it, is set by ordinary collisions far below any gravitational bound (3.0e-69 of the frame's density ceiling at 50 AU). Waking the Belt: Self-Stirring Times, the Case for Planetary Stirrers, and the Maximum Luminosity a Debris Disc Can Show

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175173 A planetesimal belt is dark until something stirs it: collision velocities must be raised to disruptive levels before the cascade can begin. This paper computes when a belt can stir itself — by growing its own Pluto-sized stirrers — and what luminosity it can then show, for belts at 45 AU. The self-stirring time is 2851 Myr for a one-Earth-mass belt, 285 Myr for ten Earth masses and 95 Myr for thirty. Bright debris discs are already shining at 10 to 20 Myr (the beta Pictoris system is the type example), which self-stirring at these masses cannot do: an external stirrer, most plausibly a planet, is required for the young bright population, while self-stirring suffices for belts that switch on at hundreds of Myr. Once stirred, a ten-Earth-mass belt shows fractional luminosity 1.8e-04 at 500 Myr, 1.1e-04 at 1 Gyr and 2.5e-05 at 5 Gyr — the familiar 1/t fading — and cannot exceed its full-stock value at any age, which caps how bright an old debris disc can be without a recent event. The debris sky's brightness distribution is thus a record of two clocks: stirring, which sets the onset, and collisional grinding, which sets the decay. Shrinking Discs: How Fast Radial Drift Erodes the Dust Disc's Outer Edge, and What the Observed Sizes Require

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24175146 A dust disc should eat itself from the outside in: the grains at its outer edge drift inward at a speed that, on a standard profile, does not depend on radius, so the edge marches inward at a constant rate and the disc's dust radius is a clock. This paper winds the clock. The drift speed is 11 m per second at Stokes 0.1, 1.1 at Stokes 0.01 and 0.32 at Stokes 0.003 (radius-independent to within a factor of 1.00 on this profile). A disc born with its dust edge at 100 AU shrinks to 30 AU in 0.10 Myr at Stokes 0.03 and 0.31 Myr at Stokes 0.01; after 1 Myr the edge of a Stokes-0.003 population still sits at 32 AU, while a Stokes-0.01 population has drained to the inner disc entirely. Observed dust discs in 1 to 3 Myr-old regions have radii of tens of AU — consistent with the clock only if the emitting grains sit at Stokes numbers of a few thousandths, well below the fragmentation ceilings, or if pressure traps hold the edge. Gas discs, measured in CO, are systematically larger than dust discs by factors of two to three, the expected fingerprint of an inward-marching dust edge.
