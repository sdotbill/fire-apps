The Gamma-Ray Horizon: Attenuation on the Extragalactic Background Light as a Function of Energy and Redshift

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24182887 Gamma rays above tens of GeV are absorbed by pair production on the extragalactic background light, so the observable universe shrinks with increasing photon energy. Using a compact representation of the published background-light models as constrained by Fermi Large Area Telescope blazar spectra, this paper computes the optical depth, the attenuation factor and the horizon redshift at which the optical depth reaches unity, for energies from 10 GeV to 1 TeV. It finds horizon 100GeV = 0.5; horizon 1TeV = 0.103; attenuation 100GeV z1 = 0.0482. The horizon at 1 TeV lies at very low redshift, which is why the TeV sky is a local survey. The study is simulated and computed, not measured.

The Look-Elsewhere Cost of a Line Search: Local and Global Significance Across Energies and Regions

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24182978 A line search examines many energies in several sky regions, so a fluctuation that looks notable at one energy is less notable once the number of opportunities is counted. This paper computes that correction directly: the number of independent energy trials implied by a ten percent resolution over 5 to 300 GeV, multiplied by three regions of interest, converts local significances into global ones by a Bonferroni step. It finds independent energy trials = 42; total trials = 126; global sigma for local 3p3 = 1.55; global sigma for local 5 = 3.97. A local three-sigma feature in such a search is, globally, barely a hint, which is the statistical context in which the historical 130 GeV discussion should be read. The study is simulated and computed, not measured.

The Running Spectral Index of the Isotropic Background: Where the Spectrum Steepens and What That Excludes

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24182956 A single power-law index is a poor description of a spectrum with a high-energy cutoff, and the difference matters when components are added or excluded. This paper computes the local, energy-dependent spectral index of the published Fermi Large Area Telescope background spectrum by finite differences, together with the energy-weighted intensity that locates the spectrum's peak contribution. It finds index at 1GeV = 2.32; index at 100GeV = 2.68; E2I peak GeV = 0.3. By a few hundred GeV the local index has steepened far beyond the headline value, so any component proposed for that range must be steeper still or must cut off. The study is simulated and computed, not measured.

A Bound on Uniform Distance Rescaling from Time Delays: What Lensing Can and Cannot Say about a Bounced Early Universe

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24182923 In a cosmology where collapse halts at the Aldred density for neutron matter, 9.72e+54 g per cubic centimetre, the early universe bounces rather than beginning at a singularity. If that epoch left a uniform rescaling of all comoving distances, by a factor one plus epsilon, time delays alone could not detect it, because delay distances and Fermat delays rescale together. Combining the lensing Hubble constant with a local distance-ladder value, which does not traverse the same rescaling in the same way, bounds the parameter: the central value is -0.0035 with standard uncertainty 0.0283, giving a ninety-five per cent bound of 0.059 on the absolute rescaling. No evidence for a nonzero rescaling is found. The negative result is the result: the frame's early-universe modification, if it acts as a uniform distance rescaling, is confined below the four-per-cent level by data that already exist. Computed from published values; not measured.

Breaking the Mass Sheet with Stellar Kinematics: The Velocity-Dispersion Precision the Degeneracy Demands

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24183074 The mass-sheet degeneracy is broken, in practice, by the stellar velocity dispersion of the lens galaxy, because the dispersion scales with the square root of the sheet parameter. This paper inverts that scaling into a requirements table. A velocity dispersion measured to five per cent constrains the sheet parameter, and hence the Hubble constant from that lens, to 10 per cent. Seven such lenses combine to 3.78 per cent, and forty lenses with seven per cent dispersions reach 2.21 per cent, closely matching the precision the TDCOSMO programme obtained when it replaced assumed profiles with kinematic information. Reaching two per cent on the Hubble constant through this route alone requires of order twenty-five lenses at five per cent dispersion precision. Computed from the scaling relation; not a measurement.

Cluster-Scale Time Delays: Why Many Image Pairs Do Not Average Like Independent Measurements

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24182937 Galaxy-cluster lenses offer many time-delayed image pairs at once, most famously the reappearing supernova Refsdal, and it is tempting to divide a per-pair uncertainty by the square root of the pair count. That division is wrong when the pairs share one cluster mass model, because their errors correlate. With a representative per-pair precision of twelve per cent and inter-pair correlation 0.7, ten pairs deliver 10.25 per cent rather than the naive 3.79 per cent, an effective sample of only 1.37 independent pairs. Reaching three per cent at that correlation requires of order 12 comparably measured clusters. Cluster cosmography is therefore a multi-cluster programme or it is a several-per-cent programme; the arithmetic permits no third option. Computed under stated correlation; not measured.

Lenses Live in Crowded Places: The Sample-Level Hubble Bias from Overdense Lens Environments

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24183019 Lens galaxies are massive ellipticals, and massive ellipticals live in overdense environments. If external-convergence corrections are calibrated on random fields rather than on lens-like fields, the sample mean convergence is underestimated, and the inferred Hubble constant is biased high by the same fraction. For a plausible mean underestimate of 0.015 in convergence, the bias is 1.1 km/s/Mpc, of the same order as the statistical precision of the current benchmark sample. The tabulated relation runs from 0.37 km/s/Mpc at an underestimate of 0.005 to 2.2 at 0.03. Unlike statistical errors, this bias does not shrink as the sample grows; it is a property of the selection, and only environment-matched calibration removes it. Microlensing as a Delay Contaminant: Which Systems Have Their Cosmography Taxed by Stars

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24183117 Stars in the lens galaxy microlens the quasar's accretion disc, and because the disc is not a point, microlensing shifts the measured time delay of each image by an amount that varies with the microlensing configuration. Adopting perturbation amplitudes of half a day to two days, representative of published calculations, the added fractional delay error is tabulated across delay lengths. The worst case computed, a two-day perturbation on a ten-day delay, adds 20.0 per cent; on a hundred-day delay the same perturbation adds two per cent. Short-delay systems are therefore taxed at a rate that can exceed their entire modelling budget, while long-delay systems are nearly immune. Delay programmes should carry a microlensing line item scaled by the tabulated fractions rather than treating light-curve scatter as pure measurement noise. Recombining Six Lensed Quasars: Where the Time-Delay Hubble Constant Error Budget Actually Sits

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24183170 The six lensed quasars of the H0LiCOW sample are recombined from their published individual Hubble constant inferences, and the combined error budget is decomposed into statistical and systematic parts. Inverse-variance weighting of the six published values gives H0 = 73.42 km/s/Mpc with a purely statistical uncertainty of 1.69 km/s/Mpc. Adding the three systematic floors adopted here (Fermat-potential modelling at 2.0 per cent, external convergence at 1.5 per cent and residual line-of-sight structure at 1.0 per cent, a combined 2.69 per cent) raises the total uncertainty to 2.6 km/s/Mpc, or 3.54 per cent. The six individual values span 12.3 km/s/Mpc and are mutually consistent, with chi-squared 4.86 for five degrees of freedom. The systematic floors, not the photon statistics of the existing sample, set the precision; doubling the sample without lowering the floors would improve the total by less than a fifth. This study is a recomputation from published values, simulated and not measured.

The Floor, Not the Count: A Forecast of What Sample Size Can and Cannot Buy for a One Per Cent Hubble Constant

William Alexander Aldred · November 12, 2026

DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.24183159 Forecasts for time-delay cosmography usually quote a number of lenses. This paper quotes the condition instead. With per-lens precision of five per cent and no shared floor, one per cent needs 25 lenses in the pure counting limit; with a shared floor of two per cent the target is unreachable at any sample size, and with a floor of half a per cent it needs 34 lenses. At forty lenses in the five-per-cent regime with a half-per-cent floor, the total is 0.94 per cent. A future regime of 3.5 per cent per-lens precision with a one per cent floor likewise cannot reach one per cent, while the same precision with a half-per-cent floor needs 17 lenses. The binding constraint on the field's headline goal is the shared floor, and effort should be allocated to the floor's components (modelling, convergence calibration, environment) before it is allocated to discovery. Computed; not measured.
